QUESTION 1 – COMPOSITION

Question 120 marks

Write a composition (in approximately 400 – 450 words) on any one of the following subjects.[20]

(You are reminded that you will be rewarded for the orderly and coherent presentation of the matter, use of appropriate style and general accuracy of spelling, punctuation and grammar.)

(i)20 marks

One sultry summer night, the electricity in your neighbourhood failed for several hours. Describe what people did, the sights, sounds and smells of the neighbourhood in the dark, and the moment the lights returned. Describe how you felt at the end of the night.

Answer

This is a descriptive topic. Candidates must use adjectives to describe what they saw, the sounds they heard, the smells and the people they met and interacted with. They must also express how they felt at the end. The essay must be written in the past tense and in the first person.

DETAILED PLAN (no full model for this topic)

Thesis / angle. Past tense, first person. The power cut is described as a change of senses: sight fades, so sound, smell and touch take over, and a street of strangers briefly becomes a community. The feeling at the end is a strange regret when the lights return.

Paragraph-by-paragraph outline

  1. The moment it happens: ten o’clock, the fans slow and stop, the television dies mid-sentence, and a groan rises from every flat at once.
  2. The heat and the dark: sticky air, candles and mobile torches flickering in windows, mosquitoes, the smell of melting wax and kerosene lamps.
  3. The sounds that appear: crickets, a distant dog, neighbours’ voices carrying from balcony to balcony, a baby crying, a transistor radio playing old songs.
  4. People on the terrace and the street: families bring out mats, the elderly fan themselves with newspapers, children play hide-and-seek, someone shares a bottle of cold water, a stranger tells ghost stories.
  5. The sky: for once the stars are visible over the city; my grandfather points out a constellation he last saw as a boy.
  6. The lights return at two in the morning: a cheer, a sudden glare, fans roaring back, doors closing; my feeling that something precious had also been switched off.

Opening paragraph

It happened at exactly ten o’clock, in the middle of the hottest week of May. The ceiling fan above my head slowed, sighed and stopped, the television went dark in the middle of the news, and from every flat in our building rose the same long groan of disappointment. For a moment the whole neighbourhood seemed to hold its breath in the thick, damp heat. Then, one by one, small yellow flames began to appear in the windows around me.

Forecast 5.1 item 2 (Tier A): a home/neighbourhood scene with the full sensory recipe (13 of 13 descriptive topics use it).
(ii)20 marks

You taught an elderly neighbour to use a smartphone for making digital payments. Narrate the challenges you faced and how the experience changed your view of the elderly.

Answer

This is a narrative topic. Candidates must begin the essay by referring to the situation given in the question and then narrate what happened and what they did. The lesson learnt or the impact of the experience must be brought out. The essay must be written in the past tense and in the first person.

MODEL ESSAY (full)

Lessons in Both Directions

Mr Sengupta, our neighbour on the second floor, had been a mathematics teacher for thirty-eight years, and he was proud of never having needed a machine to do his sums. So when his son sent him a smartphone from Bengaluru, with instructions to pay his bills through it, he carried it downstairs to our flat as though it were a parcel that might explode. “Your mother tells me you are clever with these things,” he said to me. “I shall pay you in sweets.”

We began the next evening, and I discovered at once how much I had taken for granted. His fingers, stiff with age, pressed too hard or too long, so that every tap became a hold and every icon trembled. The letters on the screen were too small for his eyes, and the words were a foreign language: he could not see why a telephone needed a “wallet”, or how anything could be “scanned” without a machine. When a message asked him to enter a code sent to his phone, he looked at me with deep suspicion and asked who exactly was sending it.

I confess that I was impatient at first. I kept taking the phone from him and doing the steps myself, until he gently took it back and said, “If you do it for me, I shall need you every time.” That sentence changed the way I taught. I enlarged the text, wrote each step in his own notebook in large, numbered lines, and let him make his mistakes. We practised by sending ten rupees back and forth between our phones, again and again, until he could do it without looking at the notebook.

The real test came a fortnight later, at the vegetable market. I stood at a distance while he chose his tomatoes, scanned the vendor’s code and entered the amount with great care. When the vendor’s phone announced the payment aloud, Mr Sengupta turned to me with the delighted face of a boy who has solved his first difficult problem.

That fortnight changed my view of the elderly entirely. I had thought of them as people who refused to learn, but Mr Sengupta learnt faster than I could teach once he was allowed to learn in his own way. What slowed him was not his mind but a world that designs everything for young eyes and quick fingers. He taught me patience, and he taught me that respect sometimes means waiting instead of helping. I never did collect my sweets. He now pays for them himself, with his phone.

Model essay: 432 words (limit 400–450)

Forecast 5.1 item 12 (Tier A): helping others is the commonest narrative situation (B2024 elderly, B2025 technology failing), with a lesson clause.
(iii)20 marks

Social media has made young people lonelier. Argue either FOR or AGAINST the given statement.

Answer

This is an argumentative topic. At least FOUR distinct arguments must be provided. Sitting on the fence is not permitted: candidates must state their stance clearly, preferably in the first paragraph, and once stated the stance cannot be changed. Arguments must be supported with examples.

DETAILED PLAN (no full model for this topic)

Thesis / angle. FOR the statement, stated in the first paragraph and never abandoned. Four arguments: connection has been replaced by performance, comparison breeds isolation, online time displaces real meetings, and constant contact prevents the solitude that builds friendship. One counter-argument is answered.

Paragraph-by-paragraph outline

  1. Introduction and stance: young people have never had so many ‘friends’ and so few conversations; I argue FOR the statement.
  2. Argument 1: posts, likes and followers turn friendship into a performance; one can have a thousand followers and no one to call at night.
  3. Argument 2: endless images of other people’s perfect holidays and parties make the viewer feel excluded and inadequate, and so withdraw further.
  4. Argument 3: hours spent scrolling are hours taken from games, clubs, family meals and visits, the places where lasting bonds are actually formed.
  5. Argument 4: messages that demand instant replies create shallow contact all day, but little depth; surveys of teenagers report rising loneliness in the same years that social media use rose.
  6. Counter-argument answered: it helps isolated young people find others like them, but that works only when online friendship leads to real meetings.
  7. Conclusion: the statement is true; the remedy is to use social media to arrange meetings, not to replace them.

Opening paragraph

Never in history have young people been so connected, and never, it seems, have so many of them felt alone. A teenager today may have eight hundred friends on one application and two thousand followers on another, yet have nobody to sit with at lunch. I firmly believe that social media has made young people lonelier, and in this essay I shall argue FOR the statement. The screen promises company; what it usually delivers is an audience.

Forecast 5.1 item 22 (Tier A): media and technology is the second argumentative theme (S2024 mobile phones, B2025 film stars).
(iv)20 marks

Silence.

Answer

This is a one-word topic, the paper’s expository slot. Any type of composition, even a short story, may be written, as long as it is connected with the word. An expository essay explains what the word means, gives its kinds or aspects with examples, and ends with its value. If a short story is written, it should contain all the elements of a short story: plot, setting, characters and a logical ending.

MODEL ESSAY (full)

The Language of Silence

We usually think of silence as nothing at all: the gap between sounds, the empty space at the end of a conversation. Yet anyone who has sat in a hushed examination hall, or waited for a verdict, or stood beside a sleeping child, knows that silence is rarely empty. It has kinds, uses and a meaning of its own, and in a world that grows louder every year it is worth understanding.

The first kind is the silence of nature, the quiet of a forest at dawn or a desert at night. It is not truly soundless, for there are leaves, insects and wind in it, but it is free of human noise, and it calms us because it is the condition in which our minds evolved. Doctors now recognise that constant traffic and machinery raise stress and disturb sleep. The silence of nature is therefore not a luxury but a need.

The second is the silence of concentration. A scientist at work, a musician before a performance and a student solving a difficult problem all need it, because thought is easily scattered by noise. Great libraries and places of worship are built to protect this silence, and their rules of quiet are a form of respect for the work of the mind.

Thirdly, silence is a form of communication. A pause in a speech can carry more force than a sentence. Two old friends can sit together without a word and feel completely understood. At a funeral, silence expresses sympathy better than any speech, and the two minutes’ silence observed on days of remembrance says to the dead what language cannot.

Silence can also be a weapon or a failing. To refuse to answer someone is a way of punishing them, and to remain silent when one sees an injustice is to share in it. The old saying that silence is golden is only half true; there are moments when speaking is a duty.

Finally, silence is the ground of self-knowledge. It is only when the noise of screens and conversation stops that we can hear our own thoughts clearly. The sages of every culture withdrew into quiet to find wisdom, and even a few minutes of daily stillness can help an ordinary person think more clearly.

Silence, then, is not an absence but a presence. Used wisely, it heals, focuses, comforts and teaches. The challenge of modern life is not to fill every silence, but to make room for it.

Model essay: 416 words (limit 400–450)

Forecast 5.1 item 32 (Tier A): an abstract one-word noun, never set; an expository treatment (kinds, uses, value) fits the syllabus’s expository slot.
(v)20 marks

Failure is the stepping stone to success. Present your reflections on this statement.

Answer

This is a reflective topic. Candidates must provide their views on the given statement and substantiate them by citing examples. They may be for or against the statement or even sit on the fence. A short story cannot be written.

MODEL ESSAY (full)

Falling Forward

Few words frighten students as much as the word failure. We fear a low mark, a lost match or a rejected application as if it were a final judgement on our worth. Yet the proverb insists that failure is not the opposite of success but a step towards it. Having thought about my own experience and the lives of people I admire, I believe that the proverb is largely true, although it needs one important condition.

A failure teaches what no success can. When an experiment goes well, we rarely ask why; when it fails, we are forced to examine every step. The inventor Thomas Edison is said to have tried hundreds of materials before he found a filament that would glow for hours, and he described each failure as a way that did not work. Every unsuccessful attempt narrowed the search.

Failure also builds resilience. A child learning to walk falls dozens of times and never concludes that walking is impossible. As we grow older we lose that simple courage, and a first failure can make us stop trying. Those who have failed and recovered, however, discover that disappointment is survivable. That knowledge gives them the confidence to attempt harder things, which is how larger successes become possible.

History offers many examples. Abraham Lincoln lost several elections before he became President of the United States. The scientist Srinivasa Ramanujan failed his college examinations more than once because he neglected every subject except mathematics, yet his notebooks later astonished the greatest mathematicians of his age. In my own small way, I failed my first swimming test at the age of twelve, and the embarrassment made me practise every morning until I could swim the length of the pool with ease.

The condition, however, is essential. Failure becomes a stepping stone only when we learn from it. A student who fails an examination and blames the teacher, the paper or bad luck gains nothing from the experience, and is likely to fail again. The stone is useful only to someone who steps on it and looks ahead; to someone who sits down beside it, it is merely an obstacle.

So I would not say that failure leads to success automatically. I would say that failure honestly examined is one of the best teachers we have. The goal is not to avoid falling, which is impossible, but to make sure that each fall moves us forward.

Model essay: 406 words (limit 400–450)

Forecast 5.1 item 40 (Tier A): reflective slot, a proverb (13 of 13); the key rule is views substantiated by examples.
(vi)20 marks

Write an original short story that begins with the words: ‘The phone rang at midnight, and I knew at once that…’

Answer

This has to be an original short story. It must contain all the essential elements of a short story — plot, setting, characters, a conflict and a logical ending. The words given in the question CANNOT be changed, and must appear exactly where the question places them.

DETAILED PLAN (no full model for this topic)

Thesis / angle. A first-person story with a false expectation and a warm reversal: the narrator is certain the midnight call brings bad news about an ill grandmother, but it is a stranger who has found the grandmother’s lost dog, and the journey to fetch it becomes a small adventure.

Paragraph-by-paragraph outline

  1. Opening with the given words: the narrator (Class XII, alone at home with a father on night duty) is sure the call is from the hospital where Grandmother has been admitted.
  2. Complication: the caller is an unknown truck driver on the highway, forty kilometres away, who has found an old dog with Grandmother’s number on its collar; Bruno had slipped out when she was taken to hospital.
  3. Rising action: the narrator wakes a neighbour who has a car; the drive through fog; doubts about whether the call is a trick.
  4. Climax: at a roadside dhaba the driver has tied Bruno to his truck and fed him; the dog recognises the narrator and nearly knocks him over.
  5. Resolution: in the morning the narrator carries a photograph of Bruno to the hospital; Grandmother, who had been refusing to eat out of worry, laughs and asks for breakfast.
  6. Ending: the narrator reflects that the midnight call was the best news of the year, and that a stranger’s kindness had done what medicine could not.

Opening paragraph

The phone rang at midnight, and I knew at once that something had happened to Grandmother. She had been taken to hospital that afternoon, my father was on night duty, and I was alone in the flat with my chemistry notes and a cold cup of tea. My hand shook as I picked up the receiver. But the voice at the other end did not belong to a doctor. It was rough, tired and polite, and it wanted to know whether I owned a brown dog called Bruno.

Forecast 5.1 item 46b (Tier A): ‘begins’ is the commoner shape (8 of 13); the given words must open the story unchanged.

You are a staff reporter for a local daily. A fire broke out in a crowded market in your town last evening. Write a report for your newspaper in about 250 – 300 words, based on the following guidelines:

date, time and place of the fire – its suspected cause – the extent of the damage – rescue efforts by the fire brigade and local people – accounts of eyewitnesses – steps promised by the authorities

Answer

This is an exercise in amplification. As this is a Newspaper Report, the format below must be followed. All the points given in the question must be included and amplified; the word limit is 250–300 words.

FORMAT CHECKLIST

  • Headline
  • Byline (By Our Staff Reporter)
  • Dateline: PLACE, date, opening the first paragraph
  • Lead answering what, where, when, who, why and how
  • Past tense and third person throughout
  • Quotations from eyewitnesses or officials
  • No personal opinion of the reporter

MARKING (inferred split)

MODEL ANSWER

Fire Guts Forty Shops in Naya Bazaar; Traders Count Losses

By Our Staff Reporter

RAMPUR, March 14: A major fire broke out in the crowded Naya Bazaar at about 7.30 p.m. yesterday, destroying forty shops and leaving several traders without a livelihood. No lives were lost, but eleven people were treated for burns and breathing difficulties at the District Hospital.

According to officials of the Fire Services Department, the fire is suspected to have started from a short circuit in an overloaded electricity meter at a garment shop. Fed by stocks of cloth, plastic goods and cardboard, the flames spread rapidly along the narrow lanes of the market, which was packed with evening shoppers.

Traders estimate the loss at more than two crore rupees. The garment and footwear sections were completely gutted, and the roof of the old vegetable shed collapsed in the heat.

Eight fire tenders reached the spot within twenty minutes, but the narrow lanes and parked vehicles slowed their work. Local residents and porters formed human chains to pass buckets of water and helped shopkeepers carry out their goods. The fire was brought under control only after midnight.

“We saw sparks from the meter box and then the whole shop was full of smoke,” said Ramesh Gupta, a shoe seller. A college student, Farhana Ali, who helped guide children and shoppers to safety, said that the crowd had remained calm because volunteers kept directing people towards the main road.

The District Magistrate, who visited the site this morning, announced an inquiry into the cause of the fire and immediate relief for the affected traders. He also promised that the market’s wiring would be inspected, that the lanes would be kept free of vehicles, and that fire extinguishers would be installed at every corner.

Model answer: 296 words (limit 250–300)

(ii)10 marks

As a member of the Social Service Club of your school, you wish to organise a blood donation camp in the school in collaboration with a local hospital. Write a proposal in not more than 150 words, stating the steps you would take to make it a success.

Answer

The proposal must have a title, a short introduction, at least two objectives written as a paragraph, at least four measures and a one-line concluding request. These should be written in complete sentences and not in phrases. The measures use may, since the final decision rests with the head of the institution.

MODEL PROPOSAL

Proposal for a Blood Donation Camp

Introduction. The Social Service Club proposes a blood donation camp in the school, organised with the help of the City General Hospital.

Objectives. The camp aims to collect blood for patients who need it urgently, especially accident victims and children with thalassaemia. It also seeks to make students and parents aware that donating blood is safe and saves lives.

Measures to be taken.

  1. The camp may be held on a Saturday in the school hall, with the hospital providing doctors, nurses and equipment.
  2. Parents, teachers and staff above eighteen may be invited to register as donors.
  3. Students may prepare posters and give short talks in assembly to remove common fears about donation.
  4. Club members may serve as volunteers, guiding donors and serving refreshments.
  5. Every donor may receive a certificate of appreciation.

Conclusion. We request the Principal to kindly grant permission for the camp.

Model proposal: 142 words (limit 120–150)

QUESTION 4 – COMPREHENSION

Question 420 marks

Read the passage given below and answer the questions (i), (ii) and (iii) that follow.

(1) It is almost midnight on a beach near the mouth of the Rushikulya river in
Odisha, and Dr Meenakshi Rao has not switched on her torch for three hours. She
will not switch it on tonight at all. Around her a team of eleven volunteers waits in
the dark, and every one of them is watching the sea.
(2) They are waiting for olive ridley turtles. Every winter thousands of female5
olive ridleys arrive at a handful of beaches on this coast within a few nights of one
another and climb out of the water to lay their eggs. Scientists use the Spanish
word arribada for this mass nesting; it simply means “arrival”. Tonight, if the team
is lucky, the arrival will begin.
(3) Dr Rao explains, in a whisper, why the torches stay off. A nesting turtle that is10
disturbed by bright light may abandon her nest and return to the sea without laying.
Worse, the hatchlings that emerge from the sand some seven weeks later find the
sea by moving towards the brightest horizon, which on a natural beach is the
moonlit water. A single floodlit hotel can lead thousands of them inland, where they
die of exhaustion or are eaten by dogs.15
(4) At a quarter past twelve, a volunteer at the northern end of the beach raises a
red lamp twice. A turtle is out of the water. The team moves towards her slowly,
keeping low and well behind her, because she is most easily frightened in her first
minutes on land. She is about seventy centimetres long, grey-green and glistening,
and she hauls herself up the slope with a steady, laborious rhythm, stopping every20
few metres as if to catch her breath.
(5) Above the high-tide line she stops and begins to dig, using her rear flippers like
a pair of scoops. It takes her nearly an hour to shape a flask-shaped pit about
forty-five centimetres deep. Only when she begins to lay, dropping her soft white
eggs one or two at a time, does Dr Rao signal that the team may come closer. A25
laying turtle seems to fall into a kind of trance, and for the next twenty minutes she
will ignore almost anything.
(6) The work is quick and careful. One volunteer counts the eggs in a low voice as
they fall. Another measures the length and width of the shell with a flexible tape. Dr
Rao kneels beside a front flipper, checks it for an old tag, and, finding none, fixes a30
small numbered metal tag to its edge with a pair of pliers. The number, the
measurements and the exact position of the nest are written on a waterproof sheet
by the light of a dim red lamp.
(7) When she has laid one hundred and twelve eggs, the turtle covers the pit,
pressing the sand down with surprising force, and then flings more sand over it35
with her front flippers to disguise the spot. Then she turns, without hurry, and
makes her way back to the sea. Dr Rao watches her until the first wave lifts her
away.
(8) Tagging tells the team where these turtles travel and how often they return.
Turtles tagged on this coast have been found, years later, hundreds of kilometres40
away; others come back to the same stretch of sand season after season. “Every
returning tag is a letter from the ocean,” Dr Rao says. “It tells us that this turtle
survived the fishing nets and the plastic for one more year.”
(9) Those dangers are real. Many olive ridleys drown each year in trawling nets,
which is why fishing is restricted near the nesting beaches during the season.45
Plastic bags, which turtles can mistake for jellyfish, claim many others.
(10) Yet Dr Rao is not gloomy. She points to the villages behind the beach, where
young people who once collected turtle eggs to sell now guard the nests at night.
When the hatchlings emerge, it is these same young people who make sure that the
lights of the village are switched off.50
(11) By four in the morning, the team has recorded thirty-seven nesting turtles.
The sky over the sea is turning pale. Dr Rao stretches, finally switches on her
torch, points it carefully at her own feet, and walks back up the beach. In fifty days
she will be here again, to watch the smallest of these travellers make what she
calls the most dangerous hundred metres in the world.55

(a)2 marks

Why does Dr Rao refuse to switch on her torch while the turtles are nesting?

Answer

Bright light may frighten a nesting turtle into abandoning her nest without laying her eggs. Besides, hatchlings find the sea by heading for the brightest horizon, so artificial light can lead them inland, where they die.

Why do you think Dr Rao calls a returning tag ‘a letter from the ocean’?

Answer

Like a letter from a distant place, a returning tag brings news across a great distance. It tells the scientists where the turtle has travelled and, above all, that it has survived the nets and plastic of the sea for another year.
