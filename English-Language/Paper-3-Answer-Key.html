QUESTION 1 – COMPOSITION

Question 120 marks

Write a composition (in approximately 400 – 450 words) on any one of the following subjects.[20]

(You are reminded that you will be rewarded for the orderly and coherent presentation of the matter, use of appropriate style and general accuracy of spelling, punctuation and grammar.)

(i)20 marks

You spent a few days at your grandparents’ village during the harvest festival. Describe the fields, the fair, the food and the music, and the people you met there. Describe how you felt when you returned to the city.

Answer

This is a descriptive topic. Candidates must use adjectives to describe what they saw, the sounds they heard, the smells and the people they met and interacted with. They must also express how they felt at the end. The essay must be written in the past tense and in the first person.

MODEL ESSAY (full)

Poush in the Village

Every January my grandparents write to say that the new rice has been harvested and that the house is ready for us. This year, for the first time in five years, my examinations allowed me to go, and I spent four days in their village in Bardhaman for the festival of Poush Sankranti.

The fields were the first thing I saw from the bus. The paddy had been cut, and the land lay open and golden to the horizon, dotted with round stacks of straw like enormous loaves of bread. Egrets stepped carefully through the stubble, and in the cold, clear mornings a thin mist hung over the ponds until the sun burnt it away. The air smelt of smoke, wet earth and straw, a smell I had forgotten I remembered.

My grandmother’s kitchen was the heart of the festival. For two days the women of the family sat on low stools around a clay oven, grinding soaked rice into paste and shaping it into pithe, small rice cakes filled with coconut and sweetened with date-palm jaggery. The jaggery arrived every morning in earthen pots, still warm from the boiling pans of the man who tapped the palms, and its dark, smoky sweetness filled the house. I was trusted with nothing more difficult than stirring the milk, and I burnt my finger twice.

On the festival day itself, a fair was held on the open ground beside the temple. There were stalls of glass bangles, clay toys and brass pots, a wheezing merry-go-round turned by hand, and an old man selling popcorn from a pan balanced on burning coals. Children ran everywhere, blowing paper whistles. In the evening, a pair of wandering singers in saffron robes sat under the banyan tree and sang to the one-stringed ektara. Their voices were rough and sad and joyful at the same time, and the whole crowd fell silent to listen.

I met more relatives in four days than I had met in four years: great-aunts who pinched my cheeks, cousins who taught me to fly a kite from the roof, and a farmer who had worked my grandfather’s fields for forty years and who told me stories of my father as a naughty boy.

The return to the city felt like waking suddenly from a warm dream. The traffic seemed louder than ever and the air tasted of dust. Yet I carried home a jar of jaggery, a clay horse from the fair and a new sense of belonging to a place I had thought of only as my grandparents’ village. I understood that it was mine too.

Model essay: 439 words (limit 400–450)

Forecast 5.1 item 3 (Tier A): the festival / place kind (S2023, B2023) without copying either topic; the full sensory recipe and a feeling at the end.
(ii)20 marks

In a group project at school, one member of your team refused to do his share of the work. Narrate how you handled the situation and what the outcome was.

Answer

This is a narrative topic. Candidates must begin the essay by referring to the situation given in the question and then narrate what happened and what they did. The lesson learnt or the impact of the experience must be brought out. The essay must be written in the past tense and in the first person.

DETAILED PLAN (no full model for this topic)

Thesis / angle. Past tense, first person. The narrator’s first reaction (anger, then doing the work alone) fails; the turning point is finding out why the classmate had withdrawn. The outcome is a better project and a lesson about listening before judging.

Paragraph-by-paragraph outline

  1. The situation: a four-member science project on water purification, due in three weeks; Kabir, the fourth member, misses every meeting and says he has ‘no time’.
  2. First response: the other two want to report him to the teacher; I decide to take over his part myself, and am soon exhausted and resentful.
  3. The confrontation: I speak to him sharply after class; he walks away without a word, and I feel I have made things worse.
  4. The discovery: a friend tells me Kabir’s father is in hospital and he looks after his younger sisters every evening; I go to him again, this time to listen.
  5. The solution: we give him the tasks he can do at home at night — the diagrams and the report — and he produces the best-drawn filter model in the class.
  6. Outcome and lesson: the project wins second prize; we become friends; I learn that a problem in a team is often a person with a problem, and that leadership begins with asking why.

Opening paragraph

When our biology teacher divided the class into groups of four for the annual science project, I was pleased with mine. Ritu was organised, Sameer was good with his hands, and Kabir, the fourth member, had topped the class in physics the year before. We chose to build a model of a low-cost water filter, drew up a timetable and fixed our first meeting for the following Monday. Kabir did not come to it. He did not come to the second meeting either.

Forecast 5.1 item 13 (Tier A): teamwork and conflict (B2026 misunderstanding with a teammate); the lesson clause appears in 11 of 13 narratives.
(iii)20 marks

Board examinations should be abolished. Argue either FOR or AGAINST the given statement.

Answer

This is an argumentative topic. At least FOUR distinct arguments must be provided. Sitting on the fence is not permitted: candidates must state their stance clearly, preferably in the first paragraph, and once stated the stance cannot be changed. Arguments must be supported with examples.

MODEL ESSAY (full)

Reform, Not Abolition

Every spring, as the board examinations approach, the demand is heard again that they should be abolished. I understand the feeling behind it, for I am about to sit them myself. Nevertheless, I argue firmly AGAINST the statement. Board examinations have faults that should be corrected, but abolishing them would do far more harm than good.

My first argument is fairness. There are thousands of schools in the country, and they differ enormously in their resources and in the strictness of their marking. A common examination, set and marked by the same standard for everyone, is the only way to ensure that an A from a village school means the same as an A from a famous city school. Without it, a student’s result would depend partly on which school he happened to attend, not on what he had learnt.

Secondly, board results provide an objective basis for admissions and scholarships. Colleges must choose among lakhs of applicants. If there were no common examination, they would rely on interviews, recommendations or their own entrance tests, all of which favour students who can afford coaching or who have influential connections. The board examination, whatever its faults, treats the son of a farmer and the daughter of a minister alike.

Thirdly, the examination builds habits that last a lifetime. Preparing for it teaches students to plan over months, to revise an entire syllabus rather than a single chapter, and to perform under pressure within a fixed time. These are exactly the demands of higher studies, competitive examinations and working life.

Fourthly, board examinations hold schools accountable. Published results show parents and authorities which schools are teaching well and which are failing their students. Remove the common measure, and weak teaching can hide behind generous internal marks, to the lasting cost of the students concerned.

Those who want abolition point to the stress that examinations cause, and that stress is real. The answer, however, is reform: questions that test understanding rather than memory, a second chance to improve a score, and counselling for anxious students. Several of these changes have already been introduced, and they show that the system can improve without being destroyed.

A thermometer that sometimes gives an uncomfortable reading is not thrown away; it is made more accurate. Board examinations are the thermometer of our education system. Let us make them fairer and kinder, but let us not break the only instrument that measures every student by the same scale.

Model essay: 413 words (limit 400–450)

Forecast 5.1 item 23 (Tier A): education is the largest argumentative theme (6 of 13); argued AGAINST here to show the stance rule from the other side.
(iv)20 marks

Home.

Answer

This is a one-word topic, the paper’s expository slot. Any type of composition, even a short story, may be written, as long as it is connected with the word. An expository essay explains what the word means, gives its kinds or aspects with examples, and ends with its value. If a short story is written, it should contain all the elements of a short story: plot, setting, characters and a logical ending.

DETAILED PLAN (no full model for this topic)

Thesis / angle. An expository essay: home is not a building but a set of bonds — of people, memory and belonging. Explain its aspects with examples and end on why homelessness, in every sense, is a loss society must address.

Paragraph-by-paragraph outline

  1. Introduction: the difference between a house (bricks, rent, an address) and a home (the place where one is known and accepted).
  2. Home as people: a family, a grandmother’s kitchen, friends who become family; a migrant worker who sends money home is sending it to people, not to walls.
  3. Home as memory and habit: smells, songs, festivals and food that carry the feeling of home into a hostel or a foreign city.
  4. Home as a place of safety and growth: where a child learns values, makes mistakes and is forgiven.
  5. Home as a wider belonging: a neighbourhood, a country, the earth itself; refugees who have lost a home lose far more than property.
  6. Conclusion: a home is built by care rather than by money, and each of us can help others to feel at home.

Opening paragraph

A house can be bought, rented, sold or pulled down; a home cannot. We may live in many houses in our lifetime, from a rented flat to a college hostel, yet only a few places ever become home to us. The difference has nothing to do with size or comfort. A home is the place where we are known, where we are accepted without explanation, and where our absence is noticed. To understand the word, then, we must look beyond walls to the bonds that fill them.

Forecast 5.1 item 33 (Tier A): the one-word slot, an abstract noun never set; treated here as the syllabus’s expository kind.
(v)20 marks

Comparison is the thief of joy. Present your reflections on this statement.

Answer

This is a reflective topic. Candidates must provide their views on the given statement and substantiate them by citing examples. They may be for or against the statement or even sit on the fence. A short story cannot be written.

DETAILED PLAN (no full model for this topic)

Thesis / angle. Largely agree: measuring ourselves against others steals contentment because someone is always ahead. Qualify it: comparison with our own past self, or with a model we admire, can inspire growth.

Paragraph-by-paragraph outline

  1. Introduction: the saying explained; a student delighted with 85 marks until he learns his friend scored 92.
  2. Why comparison steals joy: there is always someone richer, cleverer or more popular; the goalposts keep moving.
  3. Social media multiplies it: we compare our ordinary days with others’ carefully chosen best moments.
  4. Its effect on relationships: envy spoils friendships and sibling bonds; parents who compare children with others hurt their confidence.
  5. The qualification: healthy comparison — with our own earlier performance, or with a role model whose example we follow — can motivate.
  6. Conclusion: compare to learn, never to judge one’s worth; gratitude is the defence against the thief.

Opening paragraph

Last year a classmate of mine received his examination results and was overjoyed with a score of eighty-five. Ten minutes later his best friend told him that he had scored ninety-two, and the joy drained from his face as if a switch had been turned off. Nothing about his own marks had changed; only the comparison had. That small scene seems to me a perfect illustration of the saying that comparison is the thief of joy, and I believe that it is largely true.

Forecast 5.1 item 41 (Tier A): reflective slot, a modern maxim; views plus examples, either side or balanced.
(vi)20 marks

Write an original short story that ends with the words: ‘…She folded the note carefully and smiled; some secrets were worth keeping.’

Answer

This has to be an original short story. It must contain all the essential elements of a short story — plot, setting, characters, a conflict and a logical ending. The words given in the question CANNOT be changed, and must appear exactly where the question places them.

MODEL ESSAY (full)

The first note appeared in Tara’s locker in the second week of July, three days after her father lost his job. It was written in blue ink on a page torn from an exercise book, and it said only: “Hard years do not last. Hard workers do. Keep going.” Instead of a signature there was a tiny drawing of a sparrow.

Tara showed it to nobody. At home, her mother was selling her gold bangles to pay the rent, and her father sat for hours by the window with the newspaper open at the advertisements. Tara stopped asking for anything and began to study at the kitchen table long after midnight, because it was the only room where the light did not disturb anyone.

The notes kept coming, one every Monday. When she failed a chemistry test in August, the note said, “One test is a stone, not a wall. Step over it.” When she was too tired to go to the inter-school debate, a note in her locker listed three arguments she had forgotten to use the previous year. Each one ended with the same small sparrow.

She tried to discover the writer. She suspected her friend Nisha, who swore she knew nothing about it. She suspected the librarian, who was kind but had terrible handwriting. She even waited behind the staircase one Monday morning, but the bell rang before anyone came, and when she opened the locker after the first period, the note was already there.

In February her father found work at a printing press, and in May the results were announced. Tara had come first in the school. She went to thank her teachers, and she saved Mr Iyer, the gruff mathematics teacher, for last, because she was a little afraid of him. He was bent over the class register when she knocked, and he did not look up. “Well done,” he said. “I expected nothing less.”

As she turned to leave, Tara glanced at the register. In its margin, beside the names of the students who had been absent, somebody had absent-mindedly drawn a row of tiny sparrows in blue ink.

She looked at him. He went on writing, very busily, and his ears had turned red.

That afternoon she found one last note in her locker. It said, “You never needed me. You only needed to believe that someone was watching.” Tara read it twice. She thought of going back to the staff room, and then she thought of his red ears and his busy pen. She folded the note carefully and smiled; some secrets were worth keeping.

Model essay: 434 words (limit 400–450)

Forecast 5.1 item 46c (Tier A): ‘ends’ shape; the given words close the story unchanged, and the ending must follow logically from the plot.

MODEL ANSWER

Screen Time and the Teenage Mind

by Kavya Menon, Class XII

Check your phone’s screen-time report: the number will probably surprise you. Many of us now spend six to eight hours a day on screens outside school, which is more than a third of our waking hours.

Tired Eyes, Tired Minds

The first casualty is sleep. The blue light of a screen delays the hormone that makes us drowsy, and one more video easily becomes twenty. A tired brain concentrates poorly, and constant notifications train it to jump from task to task, so that reading a single chapter without distraction starts to feel impossible.

Together but Apart

Screens also change the way we relate to people. Families sit at the same dinner table, each member bent over a separate device. Friends message all day but rarely meet, and conversations happen in short bursts rather than in the long talks that build trust.

The Mirror That Lies

Social media adds a further pressure. Its feeds show edited faces and perfect holidays, and teenagers who compare themselves with these images often feel plain, left out or unsuccessful. The pursuit of likes can replace self-respect.

Finding the Balance

Screens are not evil; they connect, teach and entertain us. The answer is balance: keeping phones out of the bedroom at night, switching off unnecessary notifications, setting aside screen-free hours for study and family, and filling free time with sport, music or books.

Adults Have a Part to Play

Parents can set an example by putting their own phones away at meals. Schools can teach digital well-being as seriously as any other subject and offer attractive activities that pull students away from their screens.

The screen is a powerful servant. Our task is to make sure that it never becomes our master.

(ii)10 marks

As the Secretary of the Computer Club of your school, you wish to organise a cyber-safety awareness workshop for students of Classes VI to XII. Write a proposal in not more than 150 words, stating the steps you would take to make it a success.

Answer

The proposal must have a title, a short introduction, at least two objectives written as a paragraph, at least four measures and a one-line concluding request. These should be written in complete sentences and not in phrases. The measures use may, since the final decision rests with the head of the institution.

MODEL PROPOSAL

Proposal for a Cyber-Safety Workshop

Introduction. The Computer Club proposes a one-day workshop to help students use the internet safely and responsibly.

Objectives. The workshop aims to protect students from online dangers such as cyberbullying, fraud and the misuse of personal information. It also seeks to teach them how to report problems and to behave responsibly online.

Measures to be taken.

  1. An officer from the cyber cell of the local police may be invited to address senior students.
  2. Separate sessions may be held for the middle and senior classes, suited to their age.
  3. Club members may demonstrate strong passwords, privacy settings and ways of spotting fake messages.
  4. A helpline number and an anonymous complaint box may be displayed in the school.
  5. A short quiz may be conducted at the end, with prizes for the winners.

Conclusion. We request the Principal to kindly consider and approve this plan.

Model proposal: 140 words (limit 120–150)

Give two examples from the passage to show that Gopal Das was both mean and mistrustful.

He was mean because he charged interest that doubled a debt in two years and dismissed Raghu without paying his wages. He was mistrustful because he trusted no bank, neighbour or relative, and checked his hidden gold with a lantern every night.
