Answer

The prescribed word limit must be adhered to. The essay should neither exceed the prescribed word limit nor fall short of it. Marks are awarded as follows for whichever topic is attempted:

Content and organisation: relevance to every element of the topic, development of ideas, paragraphing, a clear opening and a logical close10

(i) After weeks of scorching heat, the first heavy rain of the season fell on your city one afternoon. Describe what you saw and heard, first from your balcony and then on the street, and how the city changed within an hour. Express your feelings as the rain stopped.

This is a descriptive topic. Candidates must use adjectives to describe what they saw, the sounds they heard and the people around them. They must also express how they felt at the end. Please note that the essay must be written in past tense and in first person.

Full model essay

The Hour the City Changed

For six weeks the sun had pressed down on our city like a hot iron. The pavements shimmered, the leaves of the neem tree outside our building hung limp and grey with dust, and by noon the streets were almost empty. That afternoon I was lying on the floor of our flat, the coolest place I could find, when the light in the room suddenly changed.

I ran out onto the balcony. The sky in the west had turned the colour of a bruise, purple and dark grey, and it was rolling towards us over the rooftops. A hot wind sprang up from nowhere, sending plastic bags and dry leaves whirling down the lane. Somewhere a door banged again and again. Then came a long, low growl of thunder, and the first drops fell, so large and heavy that each one left a dark coin on the dusty balcony floor.

Within a minute the drops became a roar. The rain came down in grey sheets, drumming on the tin roof of the tea-stall below, hissing on the hot road and gurgling out of every drainpipe. The smell rose up to me at once: the rich, earthy smell of wet dust that I had almost forgotten. Across the lane, children burst out of their houses, shrieking with delight, and danced barefoot in the puddles while their mothers called them in, not very seriously.

I could not stay indoors. I went down to the street in my old slippers and stood under the awning of the tea-stall with the rickshaw-pullers and a delivery boy who had taken shelter there. Nobody complained about being wet. The tea-seller was laughing, pouring out cups of steaming tea as fast as he could, and an old man beside me held his palm out into the rain like a child. Water was already swirling ankle-deep at the corner, carrying paper boats that someone had launched.

An hour later it was over. The clouds broke apart, and a pale gold light fell across a city I hardly recognised. The neem tree was a brilliant, shining green. The air was cool and washed clean, the frogs had begun their chorus in the empty plot behind our building, and birds were quarrelling cheerfully on the telephone wires.

As I climbed the stairs, wet to the skin, I felt lighter than I had in weeks. The heat had made everyone irritable and tired, but one afternoon of rain had turned strangers into companions and a dusty city into a garden. I realised how much we depend on something we cannot control, and I was grateful for it.

(ii) A cyclone struck your area and many families lost their homes. Narrate how you and your friends organised a collection of relief material for them, and what you learnt from the experience.

This is a narrative topic. Candidates must begin the essay by referring to the situation given in the question and then narrate what they did, ending with the lesson or impact. Please note that the essay must be written in past tense and in first person.

Plan of a model answer (not a full essay)
  • Opening (situation): the night of the cyclone, the wind and the fallen trees; next morning the news that the fishing hamlet near the river had lost its huts. First person, past tense.
  • The decision: you and three friends decide to act; permission from parents and the resident welfare association; a list of what is needed (dry food, clothes, tarpaulin, medicines).
  • The effort: posters and messages, a collection table outside the housing society gate, sorting and packing late into the night; a difficult moment when donations dry up, and a local shopkeeper donates a truckload of rice.
  • The distribution: travelling with a relief volunteer; the faces of the families, a child clutching a new blanket; the scale of the loss.
  • Lesson and impact: small efforts multiply when people join; gratitude for your own home; the decision to keep a relief cupboard ready every monsoon.
Model opening paragraph only

The cyclone reached our town just after midnight. All night the wind screamed round our building and rattled the windows, and at dawn I looked out at a street strewn with branches, broken signboards and tangled wires. It was only later that morning, when the milkman arrived two hours late, that we heard the worst news: the fishing hamlet by the river, where he lived, had lost almost every one of its huts.

(iii) Online shopping is killing local markets. Argue either FOR or AGAINST the given statement.

This is an argumentative topic. Candidates are to present their arguments either for or against the topic. At least FOUR distinct arguments must be provided. Please note that sitting on the fence is not permitted. Candidates must state their stance clearly and preferably in the first paragraph. Once stated, the stance cannot be changed.

Full model essay

The Empty Shops of My Neighbourhood

When I was a child, the market near my home was the busiest place I knew. Today half of its shops have their shutters down. I believe the reason is clear, and I stand firmly FOR the statement that online shopping is killing local markets.

My first argument is price. Large online companies buy in enormous quantities and can afford to sell at a loss for months in order to win customers. Many even offer free returns, which no small shop can afford. A small shopkeeper who pays rent, electricity and wages cannot match those discounts. Customers who once bought their shoes, phones and books from the neighbourhood now compare prices on a screen and choose the cheapest, and the local shop quietly loses its trade.

Secondly, convenience has changed our habits. Why walk to the market in the heat when a parcel will arrive at the door tomorrow? Even groceries and medicines are now delivered within minutes. Every order placed online is a sale that a local trader has lost.

Thirdly, the markets are losing the young. My parents knew every shopkeeper by name; my friends and I rarely enter a shop at all. A generation that has grown up shopping on its phones will not return to the market when it grows older, and the customers of the future are already gone.

My fourth argument is that the damage spreads beyond the shopkeepers. When a market declines, the porters, tailors, tea-sellers and rickshaw-pullers who depended on its crowds lose their living too. I have seen the cobbler at the corner of our market sit idle for hours, because fewer people pass that way.

Finally, a market is more than a place to buy things. It is where neighbours meet, where a shopkeeper allows a regular customer to pay next week, and where a town’s character lives. On festival evenings our market used to be so crowded that one could barely walk; last Diwali I could see from one end of it to the other. As the shutters come down, that community disappears, and no website can replace it.

Some people argue that online shopping has created new jobs, and that clever shopkeepers can sell online as well. A few have indeed done so. But for every trader who has adapted, many more have closed. The evidence is before our eyes in every neighbourhood: online shopping is slowly but surely killing our local markets.

(iv) Memories

This is a one-word topic. Any type of composition, even a short story, may be written, as long as it is connected with the word. If a short story is written, it should contain all the elements of a short story, that are, plot, setting, characters and a logical ending.

Plan of a model answer (not a full essay)
  • Approach chosen: an essay (a story connected with memories, with plot, setting, characters and a logical ending, would equally be accepted).
  • Opening: memories as the thread that ties our past to our present and makes us who we are.
  • Happy memories: childhood holidays, festivals, a grandparent’s stories; how a smell or a song brings them back in an instant.
  • Painful memories: loss and failure; how they teach and warn; the need not to be imprisoned by them.
  • Shared memory: family photographs and family stories; a nation’s memory in its history and monuments.
  • Memory in the digital age: thousands of photographs taken, few looked at; living a moment versus recording it.
  • Conclusion: we cannot choose all our memories, but we can choose to make good ones for ourselves and for others.
Model opening paragraph only

If someone took away all my memories, I would still be alive, but I am not sure that I would still be myself. My name, my family, the fears I have overcome and the things I love are all held together by memory. It is memory that turns a string of days into a life, and a group of strangers into a family who can say, “Do you remember the time when …?”

(v) ‘It is not the destination but the journey that matters.’ Present your reflections on this statement.

This is a reflective topic. Candidates must provide their views on the given topic and substantiate them by citing examples. They may be for or against the topic or even sit on the fence. Please note that a short story cannot be written.

Plan of a model answer (not a full essay)
  • Opening: explain the statement: the value of an effort lies in the experience of making it, not only in the result. State your view: largely true, with a qualification.
  • Learning happens on the way: the student who fails an examination but learns how to study; mountaineers who remember the climb more than the summit.
  • Joy of the process: a musician practising, a gardener tending seedlings; happiness found in daily effort, not in a single moment of arrival.
  • Danger of living only for goals: anxiety, shortcuts and cheating when only the result counts; the emptiness felt by some after reaching a goal.
  • People met on the journey: friendships and help that outlast any prize.
  • Qualification: a journey without any destination is aimless; goals give direction, the journey gives meaning.
  • Conclusion: aim at a destination, but live the journey.
Model opening paragraph only

When we are young, most of us are taught to think about destinations: the marks we must score, the college we must enter, the career we must reach. The saying, “It is not the destination but the journey that matters,” asks us to look at life differently. It suggests that what we become along the way, the lessons we learn and the people we meet, matters more than the moment of arrival. I believe that this is largely true, although destinations have their own importance.

(vi) Write an original short story that ends with the following words:
… As the train pulled away, I realised I had never even asked her name.

This has to be an original short story. It must contain all the essential elements of a short story such as plot, setting, characters, and the ending must be a logical one. Please note that the words given in the question CANNOT be changed in the essay.

Full model essay

Platform Number Four

The Howrah platform was so crowded that evening that I could hardly see my own feet. I was seventeen, travelling alone for the first time, to an entrance examination in Delhi, and I was more frightened than I would have admitted to anyone. My mother had packed my bag, written my coach number on my hand and made me promise to phone her from the train.

I had just found my coach when I discovered that my wallet was gone. My ticket, my admit card and all my money had been inside it. I stood by the carriage door, staring into my empty bag, my knees weak, while hundreds of people pushed past me. The train was to leave in twenty minutes.

“Is something wrong?” asked a voice. She was a young woman of perhaps twenty-five, in a plain green sari, with a railway employee’s badge on her shoulder bag. I told her everything in a rush, and to my shame my voice shook.

She did not waste a word. She took me to the ticket examiner, explained the situation and showed him the booking message on my phone, which I had been too panicked to remember. Then she marched me to the station police booth to report the theft, and while the constable wrote slowly, she telephoned my examination centre and learnt that a duplicate admit card could be printed there on the morning of the test.

“And money?” she asked. When I shook my head, she pressed two folded notes into my hand. “Send it back when you can,” she said. “Or give it to the next person who needs it.”

The whistle blew. She almost pushed me up the steps into the coach and stood on the platform as the engine gave a long hoot. I leaned out, trying to thank her, but the noise of the engine swallowed my words. She only smiled and waved, the green sari bright against the grey crowd.

I sat down by the window, my heart still racing, holding the two folded notes. The coach was warm and full of voices, but I could not say a word. Then I suddenly remembered the promise I had made my mother. I took out my phone to call her and tell her about the kind woman who had saved me, and then I stopped. I knew the woman’s face, her voice, even the badge on her bag. I did not know how to find her again. As the train pulled away, I realised I had never even asked her name.

QUESTION 2 – DIRECTED WRITING AND PROPOSAL

Question 225 marks
(i)15 marks

Your school magazine is running a series called ‘Lives Worth Reading’. Write a review of a biography or an autobiography that you have read recently, in about 250 – 300 words, based on the following guidelines:

the title, the author and the person the book is about – the period and setting of that life – the main events the book describes – the qualities of the person that stand out – the author’s style, and one passage you remember – your rating and recommendation

Answer

This is an exercise in amplification. As this is a Book Review, it must carry a title and the details of the book (title, author, publisher), and must discuss the content without giving everything away, ending with a rating and a recommendation. All the points given in the question must be included and amplified.

Format the examiner looks for
  • A title for the review, and the reviewer’s name and class.
  • The book’s title, author, genre and publisher stated at the start.
  • Each guideline point in its own paragraph: setting, events, qualities, style.
  • A clear rating and a recommendation saying who should read the book.
Model answer

Lives Worth Reading: Wings of Fire

Reviewed by Ananya Roy, Class XII

Wings of Fire: An Autobiography, written by Dr A. P. J. Abdul Kalam with Arun Tiwari and published by Universities Press in 1999, is the story of the boy from Rameswaram who became one of India’s best-known scientists, and later its President.

The book begins in the 1930s in the small island town of Rameswaram, where Kalam’s father, a boat owner, taught him faith and simplicity. It follows him to college in Tiruchirappalli and to the Madras Institute of Technology, and then into the laboratories and launch sites of India’s space and defence programmes.

Its main events are gripping. As a boy, Kalam sold newspapers to help his family. His dream of becoming an Air Force pilot ended in disappointment, and he began his career at a defence establishment before joining the rocket programme under Vikram Sarabhai. As project director of the SLV-3 rocket, he faced a painful failure in 1979 before the successful launch of 1980 placed a satellite in orbit. Later he led the programme that developed the Agni and Prithvi missiles.

What stands out is Kalam’s humility. He gives credit to his teachers and his team, accepts failure as part of learning, and never forgets his roots.

The style is simple, honest and often moving, with poems and reflections woven into the chapters. The passage I remember best describes his rejection by the Air Force, and the quiet advice he received soon afterwards to accept his destiny and find another path.

I would rate the book four and a half out of five. The technical chapters may seem slow to some readers, but anyone who has ever feared failure should read it.

Word count (body, excluding title and byline): 275

(ii)10 marks

As a member of the Student Council of your school, you wish to start a Wellness Club that would help students manage stress and look after their mental and physical health. Write a proposal in not more than 150 words, stating the steps you would take to make it a success.

Answer

The proposal must have a title, a short introduction of about 10 to 15 words, two objectives and four measures to be taken. These should be written in complete sentences and not in phrases. The objectives are written as a paragraph, not point-wise; the measures use ‘may’, since the final decision rests with the head of the institution; a one-line conclusion politely requests approval.

Model proposal

A Wellness Club for Our School

Introduction

The Student Council proposes to start a Wellness Club to help students stay calm, healthy and confident throughout the year.

Objectives

The first objective is to help students cope with the pressure of examinations and competition by teaching them simple ways to manage stress. The second is to encourage healthy habits of sleep, diet and exercise, so that students learn better and feel happier in school.

List of measures

  1. Weekly sessions of yoga and breathing exercises may be held before school.
  2. The school counsellor may conduct monthly workshops on stress and time management.
  3. A quiet corner in the library may be set aside for students who need a break.
  4. Senior students may be trained as peer listeners for their juniors.
  5. A Wellness Week, with talks by doctors and sports activities, may be organised each term.

Conclusion

I request you kindly to approve this proposal.

Word count (whole proposal, title included): 145

(c)2 marks

Why did the Inspector miss the evening train?

Answer

He had ordered the station clock to be set back two minutes to agree with his own watch, which was slow. The guard started the train by the correct railway time, so when the Inspector rose at “five thirty-two” by his watch, the train had already left.
