Answer

The prescribed word limit must be adhered to. The essay should neither exceed the prescribed word limit nor fall short of it. Marks are awarded as follows for whichever topic is attempted:

Content and organisation: relevance to every element of the topic, development of ideas, paragraphing, a clear opening and a logical close10

(i) A member of your family underwent an operation, and you spent the afternoon waiting in the hospital corridor for news. Describe the sights and sounds around you and the people who waited with you. Express your feelings when the doctor finally came out of the operation theatre.

This is a descriptive topic. Candidates must use adjectives to describe what they saw, the sounds they heard and the people around them. They must also express how they felt at the end. Please note that the essay must be written in past tense and in first person.

Plan of a model answer (not a full essay)
  • Opening (situation): your grandfather taken in for a heart operation; the red light above the theatre door switched on; the long wait begins. First person, past tense.
  • Sights: the pale green walls and the bright tube lights; trolleys, drip stands and wheelchairs; nurses in blue moving quickly; the clock above the door.
  • Sounds and smells: the squeak of rubber soles, the beep of machines, names called over the loudspeaker, whispered prayers; the sharp smell of disinfectant and cold coffee.
  • People: your grandmother telling her beads; a young father pacing; an old woman who offers you a biscuit and tells you about her son; a ward boy who jokes to cheer you up.
  • Feelings through the wait: fear, boredom, guilt at small quarrels, the effort to stay calm for your grandmother.
  • The ending: the light goes off, the doctor removes her mask and smiles; relief, tears, gratitude, and a new respect for the people who work in hospitals.
Model opening paragraph only

The red light above the door of the operation theatre came on at exactly two o’clock, and for the next four hours I watched it as if my own life depended on it. My grandfather had been wheeled in a few minutes earlier, lifting one thin hand to wave at us, and the double doors had swung shut behind him with a soft, final thud. My grandmother and I sat down on the hard steel bench in the corridor to wait.

(ii) Your class was wrongly blamed for damage to costly equipment in the school laboratory. Narrate how the truth came out and what the experience taught you.

This is a narrative topic. Candidates must begin the essay by referring to the situation given in the question and then narrate what they did, ending with the lesson or impact. Please note that the essay must be written in past tense and in first person.

Full model essay

The Broken Microscope

On a Monday morning in October, our class teacher walked into Class Eleven with a face we had never seen before. Over the weekend, she told us, the new digital microscope in the biology laboratory had been knocked to the floor and its lens smashed. Our class had been the last to use the laboratory on Friday. Unless the culprit confessed, the whole class would pay for the damage, and our trip to the science fair would be cancelled.

The silence that followed was terrible. Every one of us knew that we had left the laboratory tidy, and every one of us began to look at everyone else. By lunchtime friends were accusing friends. Someone remembered that Kabir had gone back for his pencil box; someone else said that Rhea had been careless with the slides. I felt sick. I was the class monitor, and I had locked the laboratory door myself.

That evening I could not stop thinking about the door. I was certain I had locked it, yet the teacher had said that there was no sign that it had been forced. How had anyone got in? The next morning I went early to the laboratory with my friend Tanvi. The door was exactly as I had left it. But the window at the back, which overlooked the garden, did not quite close, and on its sill lay a small grey feather.

We waited in the garden during the lunch break. After twenty minutes, a large pigeon landed on the sill, squeezed through the gap and disappeared inside. When we opened the door, we found it perched on the top shelf, beside a tray of slides it had knocked askew. Above the shelf, hidden by a stack of old files, was a nest.

We fetched our teacher at once. She stood looking at the pigeon for a long time, and then, to our astonishment, she laughed. The caretaker confirmed that the window catch had been broken for weeks. The microscope had been knocked off the edge of the bench by a bird, not by a student.

The next day our teacher apologised to the whole class, and the trip to the science fair went ahead. Kabir and Rhea shook hands, rather awkwardly, with those who had accused them.

The experience taught me two things. The first was how quickly suspicion can poison a group of friends when no one stops to look at the facts. The second was that the truth is often found not by arguing but by patient observation. I had wanted to be a scientist for years; that week I learnt what the word really means.

(iii) Homework does more harm than good. Argue either FOR or AGAINST the given statement.

This is an argumentative topic. Candidates are to present their arguments either for or against the topic. At least FOUR distinct arguments must be provided. Please note that sitting on the fence is not permitted. Candidates must state their stance clearly and preferably in the first paragraph. Once stated, the stance cannot be changed.

Plan of a model answer (not a full essay)
  • Stance, in the first paragraph: AGAINST the statement; homework, set sensibly, does more good than harm. (A FOR answer is equally acceptable if held throughout.)
  • Argument 1: practice fixes learning; a concept heard once in class is forgotten unless it is used again the same day.
  • Argument 2: it builds self-discipline, time management and the habit of working alone, which college and working life demand.
  • Argument 3: it shows the teacher, and the student, what has not been understood.
  • Argument 4: it keeps parents in touch with what their children are learning.
  • Argument 5: project and research homework develops curiosity that a forty-minute period cannot.
  • Rebuttal: excessive or mechanical homework is harmful, but that calls for better homework, not none; conclusion restating the stance.
Model opening paragraph only

Few school traditions are as unpopular as homework, and when students are asked, most would happily see it abolished. Yet popularity is not the same as value. I stand firmly AGAINST the statement that homework does more harm than good. Sensible homework, of the right amount and the right kind, is one of the most useful habits a school can build, and the problems that its critics point to are problems of excess, not of homework itself.

(iv) Kindness

This is a one-word topic. Any type of composition, even a short story, may be written, as long as it is connected with the word. If a short story is written, it should contain all the elements of a short story, that are, plot, setting, characters and a logical ending.

Full model essay

Kindness

Kindness is perhaps the simplest of all virtues to understand and the hardest to practise every day. It asks for no special talent, no money and no education. It asks only that we notice another person’s need and act on it, even when nobody is watching and nothing will be given in return.

We often imagine that kindness must be grand to be real: a large donation, a life spent in the service of the poor. Such lives deserve our admiration, but most kindness is small. It is the neighbour who carries an old woman’s shopping up three flights of stairs, the student who explains a sum to a classmate who is too shy to ask the teacher, the passenger who gives up a seat on a crowded bus. Each act takes a minute, and each can change the colour of someone’s whole day. I once saw a small boy at a railway station give half of his biscuits to a stray puppy, and then look round anxiously in case his mother scolded him. Nobody had taught him a lesson on kindness; he simply felt what the puppy felt.

Kindness also has a surprising power over the person who shows it. Doctors and teachers will tell you that those who help others tend to be happier and calmer than those who do not. When we are kind, we stop thinking only about our own worries, and discover that we are part of something larger than ourselves.

Real kindness, however, is not the same as softness. Sometimes the kind thing is to tell a friend a hard truth, to refuse a child something that would harm him, or to stand up for a person who is being mocked when it would be easier to laugh along. Kindness without courage soon turns into mere politeness.

It is also important to be kind to those who cannot repay us, and even to those who have not been kind to us. Anyone can be pleasant to a friend. To be patient with a rude shopkeeper, to forgive a classmate who has hurt us, or to treat a street animal gently requires a deeper kind of goodness.

In an age of hurried lives and angry voices on social media, kindness may seem old-fashioned. I believe it is exactly what our age needs most. Laws can make people behave, but only kindness can make them care. If each of us performed one small, deliberate act of kindness every day, the world would not become perfect, but it would become gentler, and that would be no small achievement.

(v) ‘We make a living by what we get, but we make a life by what we give.’ Present your reflections on this statement.

This is a reflective topic. Candidates must provide their views on the given topic and substantiate them by citing examples. They may be for or against the topic or even sit on the fence. Please note that a short story cannot be written.

Full model essay

Getting and Giving

We spend much of our lives learning how to earn: we study for examinations, compete for places in colleges and work towards careers that will pay us well. The saying, “We make a living by what we get, but we make a life by what we give,” reminds us that earning is only half of the story. I agree with it wholeheartedly.

There is nothing wrong with making a living. Food, shelter, education and security for our families all depend on it, and a person who works honestly to provide them deserves respect. But a living is a means, not an end. A person can have a large salary, a fine house and an expensive car, and still feel that his life is empty.

What fills it is giving. The people we remember with the most love are rarely the richest people we knew. They are the grandmother who gave her time to listen to our troubles, the teacher who stayed after school to help a struggling student, the neighbour who shared his food when a family fell on hard times. None of them became famous, yet each of them made a life that mattered to other people.

History offers larger examples. Mother Teresa owned almost nothing, yet her life of service in the slums of Kolkata touched millions. Scientists who shared their discoveries freely, rather than selling them, gave the world medicines and inventions whose value no salary could measure. Such people are remembered not for what they earned, but for what they left behind.

Giving does not always mean money. We can give time, attention, skill and encouragement. A student who teaches younger children in the evenings, or who spends an hour a week with the elderly in a care home, is making a life as surely as any great philanthropist. In my own school, senior students run a small library for the children of the construction workers who live near our gates, and they say that those afternoons are the best part of their week.

Strangely, giving also enriches the giver. When we give, we feel useful, connected and grateful for what we have. Getting satisfies us only for a moment; the pleasure of a new possession fades within weeks. The pleasure of having helped someone lasts for years.

In the end, a life is judged not by the size of the bank account but by the difference it has made. We must certainly make a living. But if we wish to make a life, we must learn to give.

(vi) Write an original short story that begins with the following words:
The bus had not moved for an hour when the stranger beside me …

This has to be an original short story. It must contain all the essential elements of a short story such as plot, setting, characters, and the ending must be a logical one. Please note that the words given in the question CANNOT be changed in the essay.

Plan of a model answer (not a full essay)
  • Opening: the given words unchanged: a night bus stuck on a flooded highway; the stranger beside the narrator, an elderly man with a cloth bag, begins to speak.
  • Setting and characters: the narrator, a nervous student travelling alone to a college interview in the city; the stranger, calm and humorous; other passengers growing restless.
  • Rising action: the stranger shares his food and his stories of forty years as a schoolteacher in a village; the narrator admits fear of the interview.
  • Complication: news that the road ahead is closed until morning; the narrator fears he will miss the interview.
  • Climax: the stranger organises the passengers to walk to the next village, where he knows a bus that runs on the old road.
  • Ending (logical): the narrator reaches the interview just in time and finds that one of the interview panel is the stranger’s former student; the lesson of calm and kindness.
Model opening paragraph only

The bus had not moved for an hour when the stranger beside me finally closed his book, took off his spectacles and said, “Well, young man, it seems the river has decided that none of us is going anywhere tonight.” Outside, the rain drummed on the roof, and in the yellow glow of the headlights I could see brown water swirling across the highway where the road should have been.

QUESTION 2 – DIRECTED WRITING AND PROPOSAL

Question 225 marks
(i)15 marks

You are a staff reporter for a leading daily newspaper. For over a week a severe heatwave has disrupted normal life in your district. Write a newspaper report in about 250 – 300 words, based on the following guidelines:

the dates and the temperatures recorded – the reasons given by the weather office – the areas and people worst affected – the effect on schools, hospitals and the supply of water and power – relief measures taken by the district administration – the advice issued by doctors and weather experts

Answer

This is an exercise in amplification. As this is a Newspaper Report, it must carry a headline, a byline and a dateline, and be written in the third person and the past tense, without personal opinion. All the points given in the question must be included and amplified.

Format the examiner looks for
  • A short, factual headline; the byline “By Our Staff Reporter”.
  • A dateline (PLACE, date) opening the first paragraph, which answers what, where, when, who, why and how.
  • Third person and past tense throughout; figures and names of officials.
  • At least one direct quotation; facts, not the reporter’s opinions.
Model answer

Heatwave Grips District for Ninth Day; Schools Shut, Hospitals on Alert

By Our Staff Reporter

BANKURA, May 18: A severe heatwave tightened its grip on the district for the ninth consecutive day today, as the mercury touched 46.2 degrees Celsius in Bankura town, the highest May temperature recorded here in fifteen years. Since May 10 the day temperature has not fallen below 43 degrees, and nights have brought little relief.

According to the regional weather office, the heatwave has been caused by hot, dry westerly winds, a clear sky and the delayed arrival of pre-monsoon showers. “There is no significant moisture in the atmosphere, so the land keeps heating up day after day,” said a senior meteorologist.

The worst affected areas are the western blocks, where farm labourers, construction workers, street vendors and rickshaw-pullers have no choice but to work in the open. The district hospital has admitted 138 patients with heatstroke and dehydration this week, most of them elderly people and outdoor workers. Three deaths have been reported.

All schools in the district have been closed until May 25. Several villages are depending on water tankers after wells and ponds dried up, while repeated power cuts of up to six hours have been reported in the evenings as demand for electricity rose sharply.

The District Magistrate has set up drinking-water kiosks at bus stands and markets, ordered additional tankers, and directed that construction work be stopped between noon and four o’clock. Special heatstroke wards with extra beds and oral rehydration salts are now functioning at every block health centre.

Doctors have advised people to stay indoors between eleven and four, to drink plenty of water even when not thirsty, and to wear light cotton clothes. The weather office has advised farmers to finish field work before ten in the morning, and has forecast no relief before the end of the week.

Word count (body, excluding title and byline): 298

(ii)10 marks

As the President of the Quiz Club of your school, you plan to host an inter-school quiz competition on the school premises. Write a proposal in not more than 150 words, stating the steps you would take to make it a success.

Answer

The proposal must have a title, a short introduction of about 10 to 15 words, two objectives and four measures to be taken. These should be written in complete sentences and not in phrases. The objectives are written as a paragraph, not point-wise; the measures use ‘may’, since the final decision rests with the head of the institution; a one-line conclusion politely requests approval.

Model proposal

Inter-School Quiz Competition

Introduction

The Quiz Club proposes to host an inter-school quiz competition in our school auditorium this December.

Objectives

The first objective is to encourage students to read widely and to take an interest in science, history, sport and current affairs beyond the textbook. The second is to build friendly ties with other schools in the city and to bring recognition to our own institution.

List of measures

  1. Invitations may be sent to twelve schools, each entering one team of three students.
  2. A written preliminary round may be held to select six teams for the stage final.
  3. A retired professor may be invited as quizmaster, and questions may be set by club members.
  4. Local businesses may be approached to sponsor the trophies and certificates.
  5. Volunteers may manage registration, scoring, refreshments and the audio-visual system.

Conclusion

I request you kindly to grant permission for this competition.

Word count (whole proposal, title included): 141

Cite any two instances from the passage to show that Haripada’s family was poor but generous.

Answer

Any two of: the narrator’s tea was served in the only unchipped cup in the house, with sugar borrowed from a neighbour; Haripada gave him six eggs that were probably the family’s breakfast; the family depended on a single buffalo, whose milk paid for the boy’s school, yet gave the visitor their best.

Note Question kind: evidence from the text, “cite two instances”.

(b)2 marks

Briefly discuss any two themes of the passage.

Answer

Any two, each briefly explained: (1) devotion to duty: the young vet drives through a storm at night and works until dawn; (2) the dignity and generosity of the poor: the family gives what little it has; (3) the bond between people and animals: the boy comforts the buffalo like a child, and the calf is named as a member of the family; (4) hope and new life born out of the storm.

Note Question kind: theme.

(c)2 marks

Why did the narrator accept the eggs, although he guessed that they were the family’s breakfast?

Answer

He understood that the gift was the family’s way of thanking him with dignity. To refuse it would have hurt their pride and insulted them, as if their gratitude were worth nothing.

Note Question kind: reason / motive.
