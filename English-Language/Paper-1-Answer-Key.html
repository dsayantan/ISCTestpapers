QUESTION 1 – COMPOSITION

Question 120 marks

Write a composition (in approximately 400 – 450 words) on any one of the following subjects.[20]

(You are reminded that you will be rewarded for the orderly and coherent presentation of the matter, use of appropriate style and general accuracy of spelling, punctuation and grammar.)

(i)20 marks

You recently visited a crowded book fair for the first time. Describe the stalls, the sights and sounds around you, the smell of new and old books, and a writer you met there. Describe how you felt as you left the fair.

Answer

This is a descriptive topic. Candidates must use adjectives to describe what they saw, the sounds they heard, the smells and the people they met and interacted with. They must also express how they felt at the end. The essay must be written in the past tense and in the first person.

MODEL ESSAY (full)

A Day Among a Million Books

I had heard about the book fair every winter, but it was only last January that my father finally agreed to take me. We reached the grounds a little after noon, and even from the gate I could see that I had underestimated it. Rows of stalls stretched as far as the eye could follow, their canvas roofs painted blue, red and yellow, and between them moved a slow river of people carrying cloth bags already heavy with books.

The noise struck me first. Loudspeakers announced book launches and lost children in the same cheerful voice. Assistants at the publishers’ counters called out their discounts, a group of college students sang on a small stage near the food court, and beneath it all ran the soft, continuous rustle of pages being turned. It was the sound of thousands of people reading at once, and I had never heard anything like it.

Then there was the smell. Every stall had its own: the sharp, inky smell of freshly printed paperbacks, the dusty sweetness of old second-hand volumes, and, drifting in from the edge of the grounds, the aroma of roasted peanuts and hot tea. I spent almost an hour in a narrow stall of second-hand books where the owner, an old man with spectacles on a string, seemed to know the position of every book on his shelves. When I asked for an adventure novel, he pulled one out without looking and told me that I would not sleep until I had finished it.

The high point of the afternoon came at a small pavilion where a writer of children’s mysteries was signing copies of her new book. The queue was long and full of restless children, yet she spoke to each of them as if nobody else were waiting. When my turn came, she asked me what I liked to read, listened patiently to my rambling answer, and wrote a line in my copy advising me to keep a notebook for my own stories. I bought that notebook on the way out.

By the time we left, the sky had turned orange and the lights of the stalls were coming on one by one. My arms ached from the weight of my bag and my pocket money was gone, but I did not mind in the least. As we walked towards the gate, I felt a curious mixture of tiredness and excitement, as though I had been handed the keys to a great many new worlds. I promised myself that I would return every winter, and it is a promise I mean to keep.

Model essay: 440 words (limit 400–450)

Forecast 5.1 item 1 (Tier A): the event-visit kind (4 of 13 descriptive topics) with the full sensory recipe; ties to the S2025 ‘you become what you read’ thread.
(ii)20 marks

Your younger cousin went missing at a crowded mela while he was in your care. Narrate how you searched for him and found him, and what the experience taught you about responsibility.

Answer

This is a narrative topic. Candidates must begin the essay by referring to the situation given in the question and then narrate what happened and what they did. The lesson learnt or the impact of the experience must be brought out. The essay must be written in the past tense and in the first person.

DETAILED PLAN (no full model for this topic)

Thesis / angle. A first-person, past-tense account of one careless minute, a frightening search and a clear-headed recovery. The lesson is that responsibility means unbroken attention, not merely being present.

Paragraph-by-paragraph outline

  1. The situation, as the question gives it: a winter evening at the town mela; my aunt leaves six-year-old Bablu with me while she shops, and I promise to hold his hand at all times.
  2. The lapse: I stop at a stall of mechanical toys, let go of his hand to try one, and turn round a minute later to find him gone.
  3. The search in panic: shouting his name over the loudspeakers and the giant wheel’s music; running between stalls; the fear and guilt described through physical detail (a dry mouth, shaking hands).
  4. Thinking clearly: I stop running, remember that he had begged for a red balloon, check the balloon seller, and then go to the announcement booth, where a volunteer broadcasts his description.
  5. The reunion: a constable brings him to the booth, sticky with candyfloss and quite unaware that he was lost; I hug him and confess everything to my aunt, who is angry and then relieved.
  6. The lesson and its impact: a promise of care is kept minute by minute; ever since, I agree on a meeting point and write my phone number on a card in the child’s pocket before any outing.

Opening paragraph

My aunt had only one instruction for me that evening at the Sonarpur mela. “Hold his hand,” she said, pressing six-year-old Bablu’s sticky fingers into mine, “and do not let go until I come back.” I laughed and promised, because it seemed the simplest promise in the world. The fair was ablaze with coloured bulbs, the giant wheel creaked above the crowd, and I was sixteen and certain that nothing could go wrong while I was in charge.

Forecast 5.1 item 11 (Tier A): the school/family-responsibility situation is the commonest narrative kind (7 of 13 papers), always with a lesson clause.
(iii)20 marks

Artificial Intelligence tools do students more harm than good. Argue either FOR or AGAINST the given statement.

Answer

This is an argumentative topic. At least FOUR distinct arguments must be provided. Sitting on the fence is not permitted: candidates must state their stance clearly, preferably in the first paragraph, and once stated the stance cannot be changed. Arguments must be supported with examples.

MODEL ESSAY (full)

A Shortcut That Leads Nowhere

I firmly agree that Artificial Intelligence tools, as students actually use them today, do more harm than good. I do not deny that they are clever, or that a disciplined learner can use them well. My argument is that for most students they replace the very effort that education is meant to develop.

The first and most serious harm is to thinking itself. Learning happens in the struggle: in drafting a paragraph that does not quite work, in attempting a problem three times before it yields. When a chatbot produces a finished essay or a complete solution in seconds, that struggle disappears, and so does the learning. A student who has never wrestled with a difficult sentence will not know how to write one when the tool is taken away, as it is in every examination hall.

Secondly, these tools are often wrong, and they are wrong with great confidence. They invent dates, misquote authors and produce calculations that look perfect but are not. A mature researcher checks everything; a fifteen-year-old with a deadline rarely does. Students therefore absorb errors as facts, and they lose the habit of asking where a piece of information came from.

Thirdly, Artificial Intelligence has made dishonesty effortless and almost invisible. Homework and projects exist so that teachers can see what a student understands and help where there are gaps. When the work is produced by a machine, the teacher sees a false picture, the honest student is unfairly outscored, and the one who cheated learns nothing except how to cheat again.

Fourthly, the tools weaken patience and human connection. Why wait to ask a teacher, or argue a question out with a friend, when an instant answer is a click away? Yet it is in those conversations that students learn to explain, to disagree politely and to see a problem from another side. A screen that always agrees with you teaches none of this.

Supporters of these tools say that they are tireless personal tutors, and that a student can ask them to explain rather than to answer. That is true in theory. In practice, a tired student at midnight will almost always choose the answer over the explanation, and the tool will not refuse.

Artificial Intelligence may one day become a genuine aid to learning, once schools have learnt to control its use. Until then, it remains a shortcut, and a shortcut that skips the journey leads nowhere worth reaching.

Model essay: 412 words (limit 400–450)

Forecast 5.1 item 21 (Tier A): technology and youth, the largest argumentative theme (6 of 13 are youth or education); topical for 2027.
(iv)20 marks

Courage.

Answer

This is a one-word topic, the paper’s expository slot. Any type of composition, even a short story, may be written, as long as it is connected with the word. An expository essay explains what the word means, gives its kinds or aspects with examples, and ends with its value. If a short story is written, it should contain all the elements of a short story: plot, setting, characters and a logical ending.

DETAILED PLAN (no full model for this topic)

Thesis / angle. An expository essay: courage is not the absence of fear but the decision to act rightly in spite of it. Explain its three kinds with examples, separate it from recklessness, and end on why ordinary life depends on it.

Paragraph-by-paragraph outline

  1. Introduction: a definition built on a contrast — the soldier who feels no fear is not brave; the one who is afraid and still goes forward is.
  2. Physical courage: firefighters, a fisherman rescuing people in a flood, a mountaineer turning back to help a stranded climber; the body is at risk.
  3. Moral courage: telling an unwelcome truth, refusing to join a crowd that is mocking someone, Gandhi’s non-violent resistance; one’s reputation or comfort is at risk.
  4. Everyday or quiet courage: a patient enduring long treatment cheerfully, a student admitting a mistake to a teacher, a shy child speaking on stage.
  5. Courage and recklessness: courage weighs the danger and has a worthy purpose; recklessness ignores both (a stunt on a motorbike is not bravery).
  6. Conclusion: courage grows through small acts practised daily, and a society without it cannot remain honest or free.

Opening paragraph

We tend to picture courage as a soldier charging forward without a trace of fear. Yet a person who feels no fear at all is not brave; he simply does not understand the danger. Real courage belongs to the one who is afraid, sees clearly what may be lost, and still does what is right. Understood in this way, courage is not the property of a few heroes. It appears in hospitals, classrooms and ordinary homes every day, often where nobody notices it.

Forecast 5.1 item 31 (Tier A): the one-word slot is always an abstract noun (13 of 13); Courage has never been set.
(v)20 marks

Actions speak louder than words. Present your reflections on this statement.

Answer

This is a reflective topic. Candidates must provide their views on the given statement and substantiate them by citing examples. They may be for or against the statement or even sit on the fence. A short story cannot be written.

DETAILED PLAN (no full model for this topic)

Thesis / angle. Largely agree: people and institutions are finally judged by what they do. Qualify it at the end: words matter when they inspire action or when they are themselves an act of courage.

Paragraph-by-paragraph outline

  1. Introduction: the proverb explained in plain terms; why we trust deeds over promises.
  2. Personal and school life: the friend who says he will help versus the one who turns up with notes when you are ill; a class monitor who talks about cleanliness and one who picks up litter.
  3. Public life: leaders are remembered for what they did, not for their speeches; election promises versus completed roads and schools.
  4. Character: a child learns honesty from watching parents return extra change, not from being told to be honest.
  5. The qualification: words can themselves be actions — a speech that moves a nation, an apology that heals a quarrel — but only when deeds follow.
  6. Conclusion: let words announce our intentions and let our actions prove them.

Opening paragraph

Every one of us has been let down, at some time, by a person who promised a great deal and did nothing. Every one of us has also been helped, quietly and without any fuss, by someone who never made a single promise. It is from such experiences that the old proverb draws its strength. Words cost nothing to produce, and anyone can use them; actions demand time, effort and sacrifice, and that is why they tell us who a person really is.

Forecast 5.1 item 39 (Tier A): reflective topics are always a proverb or maxim (13 of 13), with ‘Present your reflections on this statement’ since 2023.
(vi)20 marks

Write an original short story that ends with the words: ‘…and for the first time in years, the old house was filled with laughter.’

Answer

This has to be an original short story. It must contain all the essential elements of a short story — plot, setting, characters, a conflict and a logical ending. The words given in the question CANNOT be changed, and must appear exactly where the question places them.

MODEL ESSAY (full)

Grandfather had lived alone in the house on Hill Road for six years, ever since Grandmother died. He kept it exactly as she had left it: her shawl on the back of the rocking chair, her piano under a white sheet, and a rule that no animal would ever set foot inside, because she had been allergic to them. When my parents suggested that he come and live with us in the city, he refused. “The house would be lonely without me,” he said, although we all knew that it was the other way round.

That summer I was sent to stay with him for a month, and on my second evening a thin brown puppy followed me home from the market. He had one ear up, one ear down and a tail that never stopped moving. I hid him in the shed behind the kitchen and fed him scraps from my own plate. I named him Biscuit, because that was what I had first used to win his trust.

For a week the secret held. Then, one wet afternoon, Biscuit slipped through the kitchen door, shook a storm of muddy water over the hall and raced into the drawing room. Grandfather rose from his chair with a shout. The puppy, delighted by the noise, seized one of his slippers and fled under the piano.

What followed lasted perhaps two minutes, although it felt much longer. Grandfather got down on his knees and reached for the slipper. Biscuit backed away, and the white sheet, caught on his collar, slid off the piano and wrapped itself around Grandfather’s head. He stood up, blind and flapping, walked into the rocking chair and sat down in it heavily, with the sheet over him like a ghost in a school play. Biscuit dropped the slipper, climbed into his lap and licked the sheet where his nose would be.

I waited for the explosion. Instead, a strange sound came from under the sheet. It was very faint at first, then louder, until the whole rocking chair shook with it. When I pulled the sheet away, Grandfather was laughing, tears running down his cheeks, with one arm around the muddy puppy.

“Your grandmother,” he gasped, “would have laughed until she sneezed.” He looked across at the piano, now bare for the first time in six years, and lifted the lid. He played a few wrong notes, and Biscuit howled along with every one of them. I began to laugh as well, and neither of us could stop, and for the first time in years, the old house was filled with laughter.

Model essay: 435 words (limit 400–450)

MODEL ANSWER

Gold, Greed and a Boy Who Grows Up

Treasure Island by Robert Louis Stevenson — Adventure novel — first published by Cassell and Company, 1883

Reviewed by Ananya Roy, Class XII

Few adventure novels have aged as well as Treasure Island. Robert Louis Stevenson’s classic is more than a hundred and forty years old, yet I read its last two hundred pages in one night.

The story moves from a lonely inn on the English coast to a sailing ship and, finally, a wild tropical island. It is narrated by Jim Hawkins, a brave but impulsive boy, and its most memorable character is Long John Silver, the ship’s one-legged cook, who is charming, clever and utterly untrustworthy. Doctor Livesey and Squire Trelawney complete the company.

When an old sea captain dies at the inn and Jim finds among his belongings a map showing where a pirate’s treasure is buried, an expedition is organised. Jim discovers that many of the crew are former pirates planning to seize the treasure and kill everyone else. I will not reveal how the struggle on the island ends, except to say that loyalties change more than once.

The novel explores greed, courage and the difficulty of judging people by appearances. It is also about growing up: Jim learns to act on his own judgement and to live with its consequences.

Stevenson’s style is swift and vivid, and each chapter ends at a moment that makes it impossible to stop. The scene I liked best is the one in which Jim, hidden in an apple barrel, overhears Silver revealing his plans to a young sailor.

I rate the book four and a half stars out of five. Its old-fashioned vocabulary may slow readers at first, but anyone who enjoys a gripping story should read it.

Model answer: 297 words (limit 250–300)

(ii)10 marks

As President of the Student Council of your school, you wish to set up a Reading Club and revive the neglected reading corner of the school library. Write a proposal in not more than 150 words, stating the steps you would take to make it a success.

Answer

The proposal must have a title, a short introduction, at least two objectives written as a paragraph, at least four measures and a one-line concluding request. These should be written in complete sentences and not in phrases. The measures use may, since the final decision rests with the head of the institution.

MODEL PROPOSAL

Proposal for a School Reading Club

Introduction. The Student Council proposes a Reading Club that will bring students back to the library’s forgotten reading corner.

Objectives. The club aims to develop a lasting habit of reading for pleasure among students of all classes. It also seeks to improve their vocabulary, concentration and confidence in discussing ideas.

Measures to be taken.

  1. The reading corner may be repainted and furnished with cushions, lamps and a notice board by student volunteers.
  2. Students and teachers may be invited to donate books, which will be catalogued by the librarian.
  3. The club may meet every Friday during the activity period to share and discuss books.
  4. A monthly book-review competition may be held, with the best reviews printed in the school magazine.
  5. Visiting authors may be invited once a term to speak to members.

Conclusion. We request the Principal to kindly approve this proposal.

Model proposal: 140 words (limit 120–150)

Cite two instances from the passage which show that Brigadier was cleverer than the narrator.

Brigadier kept imitating familiar voices until the narrator stopped responding, and then, while the narrator was in the kitchen, lifted the unlatched door of his cage and escaped.
