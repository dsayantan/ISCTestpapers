Answer

The prescribed word limit must be adhered to. The essay should neither exceed the prescribed word limit nor fall short of it. Marks are awarded as follows for whichever topic is attempted:

Content and organisation: relevance to every element of the topic, development of ideas, paragraphing, a clear opening and a logical close10

(i) You went with your uncle to the wholesale flower market by the river before sunrise. Describe the sights, the sounds and the smells of the market as it came to life, and the people you watched at work. Say what the morning taught you about the way people earn their living.

This is a descriptive topic. Candidates must use adjectives to describe what they saw, the sounds they heard and the people around them. They must also express how they felt at the end. Please note that the essay must be written in past tense and in first person.

Full model essay

Flowers Before Dawn

It was still dark when my uncle knocked on my door. He sells flowers for weddings, and he had promised for years to take me to the wholesale market by the river where he buys them. At four in the morning, rubbing my eyes on the back seat of his old van, I was beginning to wish he had forgotten.

I heard the market before I saw it. Beneath the great iron bridge a low roar rose out of the darkness, made of a thousand voices, the creak of handcarts and the slap of bare feet on wet stone. Then we turned a corner and the whole riverbank opened out in front of us, lit by bulbs hanging from bamboo poles. Everywhere I looked there were flowers: mountains of orange and yellow marigolds, heaped on jute sacks; bundles of tuberoses wrapped in banana leaves; baskets of dark red roses; and long white strings of jasmine coiled like sleeping snakes.

The smell was overpowering. The sweetness of the jasmine mixed with the sharp, green scent of crushed stalks, the muddy breath of the river and the smoke from a tea-stall where a kettle hissed over a coal fire. Porters hurried past with towering baskets balanced on their heads, shouting at us to make way. Sellers squatting behind their heaps called out prices, and buyers answered with prices of their own, until every bargain sounded like a quarrel that ended, suddenly, in laughter.

My uncle moved through it all like a fish through water. He picked up a rose, bent back a petal with his thumb, sniffed it and put it down again without a word. The old woman selling it laughed and dropped her price. Nearby, a boy no older than me was threading marigolds into garlands, his fingers moving so fast that I could not follow them. He told me he had been doing it since he was eight, and that he would finish two hundred garlands before the sun was high.

By the time the sky over the river turned pink, the heaps had shrunk. The porters sat on their empty baskets sipping tea from clay cups, and a few stray petals floated away on the brown water.

On the drive home I sat among our boxes of roses, breathing their scent and thinking about the boy with the garlands. I had always seen flowers as something beautiful that simply appeared at a wedding. That morning I learnt that every one of them had been carried, bargained over and threaded by people who began their work while the rest of the city slept. I have never looked at a garland in the same way since.

(ii) Your class was to present the morning assembly, and at the last minute you were asked to speak on stage in place of a friend who had fallen ill. Narrate what happened and how you overcame your fear.

This is a narrative topic. Candidates must begin the essay by referring to the situation given in the question and then narrate what they did, ending with the lesson or impact. Please note that the essay must be written in past tense and in first person.

Plan of a model answer (not a full essay)
  • Opening (situation): the class assembly on a fixed theme; your friend, the main speaker, absent with fever; the class teacher hands you the speech ten minutes before the bell.
  • The fear: dry mouth, shaking paper, the sight of a thousand faces from the wings; the temptation to refuse.
  • Turning point: a teacher’s or friend’s advice (breathe, look at the back wall, speak to one friendly face); you decide to put the paper aside and speak from what you understood of it.
  • On stage: the first stumbling sentence, a small mistake that makes the audience smile, then growing confidence; the applause at the end.
  • Aftermath and lesson: the friend’s phone call; you learn that fear shrinks once you act, and that preparation and honesty matter more than perfection. Past tense, first person throughout.
Model opening paragraph only

The bell for assembly was ten minutes away when Mrs Dutta found me in the corridor and pressed three typed pages into my hand. “Ritwik has a fever,” she said. “You know the theme better than anyone else in the class. You will speak in his place.” Before I could say a word, she had hurried away, and I was left staring at the pages, which had already begun to tremble in my fingers.

(iii) Zoos should be closed down. Argue either FOR or AGAINST the given statement.

This is an argumentative topic. Candidates are to present their arguments either for or against the topic. At least FOUR distinct arguments must be provided. Please note that sitting on the fence is not permitted. Candidates must state their stance clearly and preferably in the first paragraph. Once stated, the stance cannot be changed.

Full model essay

Keep the Zoos, Change Them

Pictures of a lonely bear pacing its cage have led many people to demand that zoos be closed down. I understand their anger, but I stand firmly AGAINST the statement. A badly run zoo should be reformed; zoos themselves should not be closed.

My first argument is that a good zoo is now one of the last safe places for animals that are disappearing in the wild. Forests are being cleared, rivers poisoned and animals hunted for their skins and horns. Several endangered species survive today because they were bred in zoos and then returned to protected forests. The zoo in Darjeeling, for instance, has long been known for breeding red pandas and snow leopards. Closing zoos would throw away this insurance against extinction.

Secondly, zoos teach. Most city children will never see a tiger in a national park, and a creature seen only on a screen is easy to forget. A child who has stood in front of a real rhinoceros, and heard a keeper explain why its horn makes it a target, is far more likely to grow up caring about wildlife. People protect what they love, and they love what they have seen.

Thirdly, zoos rescue animals that could not otherwise survive. Leopards that stray into villages, elephant calves separated from their herds and birds injured by kite strings during festivals are all brought to zoos and rescue centres. If zoos were closed, where would these animals go? A zoo is often the only place equipped to treat them.

My fourth argument concerns knowledge. Much of what vets know about treating wild animals, their diseases, diet and breeding, has been learnt by caring for them in zoos. That knowledge is used every day to save animals in the wild, and zoo vets regularly pass it on to forest officers and rescue teams.

Finally, closing zoos would not free the animals inside them. Most were born in captivity and have never hunted or escaped a predator. Released into a forest, they would starve or be killed within weeks. Closure would harm the very animals it claims to help.

I do not deny that some zoos keep animals in small, dirty cages, and those zoos must be inspected strictly, improved or shut. But we do not close all hospitals because a few are badly run. Zoos should be given larger, more natural enclosures and trained staff, and made to justify their existence through conservation. Reformed, not closed, they can remain one of the best friends that wild animals have.

(iv) Time

This is a one-word topic. Any type of composition, even a short story, may be written, as long as it is connected with the word. If a short story is written, it should contain all the elements of a short story, that are, plot, setting, characters and a logical ending.

Plan of a model answer (not a full essay)
  • Approach chosen: an essay (a story would also be accepted if it has plot, setting, characters and an ending connected with time).
  • Opening: time as the one resource everyone receives equally, twenty-four hours a day, which cannot be saved, borrowed or bought back.
  • Time as a healer: grief and quarrels softened by time; examples from family life.
  • Time as a test: patience; the farmer who cannot hurry the harvest; long-term effort in study and sport.
  • Time wasted: endless scrolling and procrastination; the student who “will start tomorrow”.
  • Time and value: punctuality as respect for others; time given to parents and grandparents as the truest gift.
  • Conclusion: we cannot manage time, only ourselves within it; use it while it is ours.
Model opening paragraph only

Of all the things we are given in life, time is the only one that is shared out with perfect fairness. The richest man in the world and the poorest child in a village both wake up to exactly twenty-four hours. No one can save an hour today to spend it tomorrow, and no amount of money can buy back yesterday. What makes the difference between one life and another is not how much time we have, but what we choose to do with it.

(v) ‘Simple living, high thinking.’ Present your reflections on this statement.

This is a reflective topic. Candidates must provide their views on the given topic and substantiate them by citing examples. They may be for or against the topic or even sit on the fence. Please note that a short story cannot be written.

Plan of a model answer (not a full essay)
  • Opening: the phrase associated with Gandhiji; meaning: few needs, rich thoughts and values. State your view (agree, with a qualification).
  • Why simple living: fewer wants mean less debt, less anxiety and less damage to the planet; examples of the waste of fast fashion and gadgets replaced every year.
  • Why high thinking: ideas, honesty and service give life dignity; examples of scientists, teachers and reformers who lived plainly.
  • The link between the two: time and attention freed from possessions can be given to learning and to others.
  • Qualification: simplicity is a choice, not poverty; comfort is not a crime, and the poor need more, not less.
  • Conclusion: measure a life by what one thinks and does, not by what one owns.
Model opening paragraph only

Few phrases are quoted as often, or followed as rarely, as “Simple living, high thinking.” We admire it on posters and in speeches, and then go shopping for a newer phone. Yet the idea behind it is not old-fashioned at all. It says that a person needs very little in order to live well, and that the energy we spend on getting and keeping things would be better spent on thinking clearly and acting well. I believe that this is largely true, though it needs to be understood carefully.

(vi) Write an original short story that ends with the following words:
… It was then that he understood why his grandfather had never sold the watch.

This has to be an original short story. It must contain all the essential elements of a short story such as plot, setting, characters, and the ending must be a logical one. Please note that the words given in the question CANNOT be changed in the essay.

Full model essay

The Watch That Stopped

Every evening at six, my grandfather wound his watch. It was a heavy silver thing with a cracked glass and a strap that had been replaced so many times that nobody remembered the original. It lost five minutes a day, and every evening he set it right by the radio.

Raghav, whose smartphone told the time to the second, found this ridiculous. “Dadu, it does not even keep time,” he said one evening. “Mr Mehta at the corner shop says an antique like that could fetch twenty thousand rupees. You could buy a new watch and a new radio.”

His grandfather only smiled and went on winding.

That winter the old man fell ill, and Raghav’s parents took him to the hospital in the city. Raghav was left at home with his aunt, and on the second night the power failed across the whole district. His phone died soon after. The house was silent and dark, and Raghav, lying awake, realised that he had no idea what time it was, or how long the night would last.

Then, from the table beside his grandfather’s empty bed, he heard it: a small, steady ticking. He felt his way across the room and picked up the watch. By the light of a candle he saw that its hands still moved, as they had moved every night of his life, because his grandfather had wound it before he left.

He sat holding it until morning. His aunt found him there at dawn and, seeing the watch in his hand, sat down beside him. “Did he never tell you?” she asked softly. “Your great-grandfather gave it to him the day he left the village to find work. He had nothing else to give. Your grandfather wore it through every hard year: the flood that took the old house, the night your father was born. He always said that as long as it ticked, the family would keep going.”

Raghav thought of Mr Mehta’s twenty thousand rupees and felt his face grow hot.

When his grandfather came home a week later, the watch was on the table beside his bed, wound and set exactly right by the radio. The old man picked it up.

“I wound it every evening at six,” Raghav said. “Someone had to.”

His grandfather held the watch to his ear for a long moment. Then he took his grandson’s hand and fastened the old strap round his wrist. Raghav looked down at the cracked glass and the slow, faithful hands, and the ticking sounded to him like a heartbeat. It was then that he understood why his grandfather had never sold the watch.

QUESTION 2 – DIRECTED WRITING AND PROPOSAL

Question 225 marks
(i)15 marks

You write a blog about the history and everyday life of your city. Last Sunday you joined a guided heritage walk through its oldest quarter. Write a blog post in about 250 – 300 words, based on the following guidelines:

the title of your post and the date – the route of the walk and the landmarks you saw – a story told by the guide – the local food you tried on the way – the condition of the old buildings – your appeal to readers

Answer

This is an exercise in amplification. As this is a Blog post, it must carry a title, the date and the blogger’s name, and be written in the first person in a warm, conversational but correct register, ending with a direct appeal to readers. All the points given in the question must be included and amplified.

Format the examiner looks for
  • A title that catches the eye, the date of posting and the blogger’s name or handle.
  • First person, warm and conversational, but free of slang, abbreviations and contractions.
  • Short paragraphs, one guideline point each, in the order given.
  • A direct appeal to readers and an invitation to comment or share at the end.
Model answer

Walking Through Two Hundred Years in Three Hours

Posted on Monday, 11 January 2027 by CityStories_Aarav

Hello again, fellow city-watchers! Last Sunday I did something I had been putting off for two years: I joined the heritage walk through the old quarter of North Kolkata. It started at seven in the morning, and I promise you it was worth losing my Sunday sleep.

Our group of fifteen met at the Shobhabazar metro station and walked through narrow lanes to Kumartuli, the potters’ quarter. Then we visited the courtyard of an old zamindar’s mansion, where the Durga Puja has been held for over two centuries, and ended at a crumbling mansion with marble statues standing in an overgrown garden.

The best part was a story told by our guide, a retired history teacher. He pointed to a cracked wooden balcony and said that a famous poet had once recited his verses there for a wedding, and had been paid in mangoes because the family had run out of money! I cannot promise that the story is true, but I will never walk past that balcony without smiling.

Of course, no walk in my city is complete without food. We stopped for hot kachoris with spicy potato curry, and finished with syrupy jalebis wrapped in newspaper. My shirt still carries the evidence.

Sadly, the walk was not all happy. Many of the old buildings are in a terrible state. We saw carved doorways hidden behind tangled electric wires, banyan saplings growing out of walls, and one beautiful mansion propped up with bamboo. The guide told us that several have already been pulled down to make way for flats.

So here is my appeal to you. Join a heritage walk, visit these places and talk about them. Buildings survive when people care about them. Have you been on a walk like this? Tell me in the comments below!

(ii)10 marks

As the Secretary of the Eco Club of your school, you wish to make the school campus free of single-use plastic and to set up a composting unit for the waste from the canteen and the gardens. Write a proposal in not more than 150 words, stating the steps you would take to make it a success.

Answer

The proposal must have a title, a short introduction of about 10 to 15 words, two objectives and four measures to be taken. These should be written in complete sentences and not in phrases. The objectives are written as a paragraph, not point-wise; the measures use ‘may’, since the final decision rests with the head of the institution; a one-line conclusion politely requests approval.

Model proposal

A Plastic-Free, Green Campus

Introduction

The Eco Club proposes to make our campus free of single-use plastic and to turn its food and garden waste into compost.

Objectives

The first objective is to reduce the plastic waste that our school sends to the city’s landfills and drains. The second is to teach students, through daily practice, that waste can become a resource, while the compost enriches our own school garden.

List of measures

  1. The canteen may replace plastic plates, straws and spoons with steel and leaf-based items.
  2. Students may be requested to bring steel bottles and cloth bags from home.
  3. Separate bins for dry and wet waste may be placed on every floor.
  4. A composting pit may be dug behind the garden, managed by Eco Club volunteers.
  5. A monthly award may be given to the cleanest, plastic-free classroom.

Conclusion

I humbly request you to approve this proposal at the earliest.
