1 · Composition

ConceptQuestion 1 at a glance

Twenty marks, one topic from six, 400–450 words, about 45 minutes. The six slots come in the same order in every paper since 2019, and picture composition has never been set. CISCE’s key opens with a warning that applies to every slot: “The prescribed word limit must be adhered to. The essay should neither exceed the prescribed word limit nor fall short of it.”

SlotWhat is setWhat the key demandsWhat loses marks
(i) DescriptiveA place, event or scene you were present at: “Describe the sights, the sounds…”, then how you felt.Adjectives for what you saw, heard (and smelt), the people and their interactions, your feelings. First person, past tense.Turning it into a story with a plot; forgetting the feelings the question asks for.
(ii) NarrativeA situation you were in: “Narrate…”, what you did, the lesson or impact.Begin with the situation given; clear sequence; the lesson at the end. First person, past tense.Starting with a long preamble; skipping the lesson clause.
(iii) ArgumentativeA statement: “Argue either FOR or AGAINST…”At least FOUR distinct arguments; stance stated early (first paragraph) and never changed.“Sitting on the fence” is not permitted. Answering both sides equally loses the content marks.
(iv) One-word topicAn abstract noun: Hope, Patience, Gratitude, Magic…Any type of composition, even a story, as long as it is connected with the word. A story must have plot, setting, characters and a logical ending.Losing the word after the first paragraph.
(v) ReflectiveA maxim: “Present your reflections on this statement.”Your views, substantiated by examples. For, against, or on the fence (allowed here, unlike argumentative).A short story cannot be written for this topic.
(vi) Short storyMust begin or end with the given words.Original; plot, setting, characters, a logical ending; the given words appear unchanged, in the given position.Altering even one of the given words (tense, name, punctuation); an ending that does not follow.

The slot decides the rules. Read the verb in the question (describe / narrate / argue / reflect) before you choose.

ConceptWhat examiners reward

The syllabus lists two criteria; the split below is the one the answer keys in this set use.

CriterionMarksWhat it means on the page
Quality of language10Range and appropriateness of vocabulary and sentence structure; syntax; grammatical correctness; punctuation and spelling.
Content and organisation10Relevance to the topic as set; organisation of the whole and of each paragraph; the non-negotiables of the slot (four arguments, the given words, past tense and first person…).
  • Orderly and coherent presentation is named in the question itself: one idea per paragraph, each with a topic sentence, joined by transitions.
  • Appropriate style: descriptive and narrative compositions want concrete, sensory detail; argumentative and reflective ones want a clear, formal voice.
  • Accuracy: a composition full of good ideas and spelling errors is marked down on language, which is half the question.

Worked exampleModel: Descriptive

Topic. Describe the scene backstage during your school’s Annual Day. Describe the sights, the sounds and the people, and how you felt when the curtain closed.

Plan Sights (para 2), sounds (3), people (4), a peak moment (5) and feelings (6): exactly the recipe. Past tense and first person throughout.

Show working

opening transition conclusion — the three moves are marked in every model.

The curtain was still closed, but behind it the whole school seemed to be breathing at once. It was our Annual Day, and I had been given the job of stage manager, which meant that for three hours I would see everything the audience never sees.

The first thing that struck me was the light. The wings were almost dark, lit only by a single blue bulb and the glow from the stage, so that every face looked pale and slightly unreal. Rows of costumes hung on a rope like tired ghosts: paper crowns, a green dragon with one broken wing, twenty white shawls for the chorus. The air smelt of face powder, hot wires and the jasmine that the dancers had pinned in their hair.

Then there were the sounds. From the hall came the steady murmur of eight hundred parents, rising and falling like the sea. Behind me, the junior choir was practising its first line in frantic whispers. Someone was crying softly because a bell had fallen off an anklet, and Mrs Sen, our music teacher, was saying, in the calmest voice I have ever heard, that bells are the easiest things in the world to sew back on.

As the evening went on, I began to notice the people rather than the noise. The boys who moved the scenery worked in silence, carrying a whole painted forest across the stage in forty seconds and grinning at one another when it landed in the right place. The Class Two children, dressed as raindrops, stood in a nervous line holding hands, and when their music began they walked out as bravely as soldiers. Our Principal, who is usually so formal, stood in the corner of the wings and clapped for every item before the audience did.

The most memorable moment came near the end. The lights failed in the middle of the senior play. For a few terrible seconds there was total darkness and absolute silence. Then, from somewhere in the hall, a small child began to sing the school song, and the whole audience took it up while our electrician found the fault. When the lights returned, the actors simply carried on, and the applause at the end was the loudest of the night.

When the curtain finally closed, I sat down on a box of props, too tired to stand and too happy to go home. I had not been seen by a single person in the audience, yet I felt that I had been part of everything. I understood that evening that a performance belongs as much to the people in the dark as to the people in the light.

Topic. You lost your bag at a railway station in an unfamiliar city and a stranger helped you. Narrate what happened, what you did and what you learnt from the experience.

I had never felt so completely alone as I did on the platform at Vijayawada, at nine in the evening, with my bag gone. I was travelling to Chennai for a science camp, I had got off the train for five minutes to buy water, and when I turned round, the bag that held my wallet, my phone and my ticket was no longer on the bench.

At first I refused to believe it. I walked up and down the platform three times, looking under benches and behind pillars, as if the bag might be hiding from me. My train had already left. I did not speak Telugu, I had eleven rupees in my pocket, and I could not remember a single phone number except my mother’s, which I had never needed to dial because it was saved in my phone.

It was then that a tea-seller named Ravi noticed me. He was about sixty, with a steel kettle in one hand and a stack of clay cups in the other. He had seen me searching and, in slow Hindi mixed with English, asked what had happened. When I told him, he did not waste time on sympathy. He took me to the railway police post, stood beside me while I filed a complaint, and argued patiently with the officer until the complaint was actually written down.

Next, he solved the problem I had not even thought of. He lent me his own phone, and when I said I could not remember my father’s number, he suggested I call my school, whose number was printed on the identity card still hanging round my neck. The school’s night guard found my father’s number in the register. Within an hour my father had booked me on the early morning train and arranged for a relative to meet me in Chennai.

Ravi would not let me sit alone in the waiting room. He gave me two cups of tea and a plate of idli, refused to take my eleven rupees, and sat on the next bench until my train arrived at four in the morning. When I tried to thank him, he only said that his own grandson travelled alone too, and he hoped someone would do the same for him.

I never recovered the bag, but I think I came home with something more valuable. I had always been told not to trust strangers, and it is still sensible advice. That night taught me that it is not the whole truth. Since then I have tried, whenever I see someone lost in a crowd, to be the stranger who stops.

Worked exampleModel: Argumentative

Topic. “Joint families are better than nuclear families.” Argue either FOR or AGAINST the given statement.

Plan Stance in the first paragraph; four numbered arguments (care, shared burdens, values, the elderly); a concession answered without changing sides; stance restated.

Show working

opening transition conclusion — the three moves are marked in every model.

A family is the first school any child attends, and I firmly believe that the joint family is the better school. I therefore argue FOR the statement that joint families are better than nuclear families, and I shall support that stand with four reasons.

The first reason is care. In a nuclear family where both parents work, a child often returns to an empty house or to a paid helper. In a joint family there is almost always someone at home: a grandmother who checks the homework, an uncle who takes the child to the doctor, a cousin who shares a secret. Children who grow up surrounded by several adults learn early that they are never without help.

Secondly, the joint family shares burdens that crush a small household. When a parent falls ill or loses a job, a nuclear family must face the crisis alone. A joint family pools its income, divides the housework and nurses its sick members in turn. The same sharing makes daily life cheaper, since one kitchen, one house and one set of bills serve many people.

A third advantage is the education in values that no textbook can give. Living with grandparents, children hear the family’s stories, festivals and recipes from the people who lived them. More importantly, they learn to adjust: to wait their turn for the bathroom, to lower their voice when an elder is resting, to share the last sweet. Such daily lessons in patience and compromise prepare them for the classroom, the office and marriage.

Finally, the joint family is kinder to the old. Our cities are filling with elderly people who live alone, or in homes for the aged, waiting for a weekly telephone call. In a joint family the grandparents remain at the centre of life, respected for their experience and useful in a hundred small ways. A society that keeps its old people close is a more humane society.

It is true that a joint family can limit privacy and that quarrels happen. Yet these are problems of management, not of the system itself, and a family that talks openly can solve them. The loneliness of a small, busy household is much harder to cure.

For these reasons I remain convinced that the joint family is the better way to live. It offers care, security, values and dignity in a single home, and in an age of loneliness those are gifts we cannot afford to lose.

Worked exampleModel: One-word topic

Topic. Freedom.

Plan Treated as an essay with four facets (political, social, inner, responsible). A story would also be allowed, provided it is about freedom.

Show working

opening transition conclusion — the three moves are marked in every model.

When my grandfather was nineteen, he spent four months in prison for carrying a flag. When I was nineteen months old, according to my mother, I screamed for an hour because I was not allowed to climb the stairs alone. Both of us, in very different ways, were demanding freedom.

Freedom is perhaps the one word that every human being understands without a dictionary. At its simplest it means the absence of chains: the right to move, to speak, to worship and to choose without fear. For my grandfather’s generation it had a very concrete meaning. It meant a country that governed itself, and they were prepared to lose their jobs, their health and sometimes their lives to win it.

Yet freedom is not only political. A country can be free while many of its citizens are not. A girl who is taken out of school to work at home, a labourer who owes more than he can ever repay, and a young man who cannot choose his own career are all living without real freedom, even though no soldier stands at their door. Poverty, ignorance and fear build prisons that are harder to see than stone walls.

There is also an inner freedom, which may be the hardest of all to achieve. We are slaves to many things we have chosen ourselves: to our phones, to the opinions of others, to anger and to habit. The student who cannot study for twenty minutes without checking a screen is not quite free. On the other hand, history is full of prisoners who kept their minds free in the smallest cell, writing books and poems that outlived their jailers.

Most importantly, freedom is not the same as doing whatever one likes. My right to play loud music ends where my neighbour’s right to sleep begins. A free society survives only when its members accept limits willingly: when they obey laws they helped to make and respect the freedom of people they disagree with. Freedom without responsibility soon turns into its opposite.

My grandfather is ninety now, and he still stands up when the national anthem plays on television. He once told me that freedom was not a gift his generation handed to mine, but a lamp we must keep filling with oil. I think he is right. Every generation has to earn freedom again, in the way it treats others and in the way it governs itself.

Worked exampleModel: Reflective

Topic. “Every cloud has a silver lining.” Present your reflections on this statement.

Plan Views substantiated with personal, historical and community examples; a qualified position (allowed in this slot); no story.

Show working

opening transition conclusion — the three moves are marked in every model.

“Every cloud has a silver lining” is one of the first proverbs children learn, and one of the last they come to believe. It says that even the darkest misfortune carries some hidden good. I think the statement is largely true, provided we understand it correctly.

Consider, first, what adversity teaches. A student who fails a test discovers, often for the first time, exactly what she does not know. A player who is dropped from the team learns more about his weaknesses in one week than in a season of easy victories. Pain is a harsh teacher, but it is an honest one, and the lessons it gives are rarely forgotten.

History offers larger examples. Many great inventions were born of hardship. Wartime shortages pushed scientists to develop new medicines and materials, and the pandemic of 2020, which closed our schools, also forced millions of teachers to learn new ways of teaching that are still useful today. Communities that have survived floods often rebuild with stronger houses and closer bonds than before.

Misfortune also reveals people. When my father was in hospital for a month, I learnt which of our neighbours would cook for us without being asked, and I learnt that I could manage the house and my studies at the same time. I would never have chosen that month, but I came out of it knowing both my friends and myself better. The same is true of whole societies: a country that suffers a drought learns to save water, and a town that survives an earthquake writes better building laws. The cloud does not disappear, but it leaves behind a wiser people.

However, the proverb can be misused. It is cruel to tell a family that has lost its home to look for the silver lining while the house is still burning. The lining is usually visible only later, and it does not cancel the loss. Nor does the proverb mean that we should wait passively for good things to arrive. The silver lining is often something we make ourselves, by the way we respond to what has happened.

In the end, I believe the proverb is less a promise than an instruction. It does not guarantee that every trouble will bring a reward. It asks us to look for the lesson, the strength or the friendship that trouble can uncover, and to use it. Understood that way, it is one of the wisest sentences a child can learn.

Topic. Write an original short story that ends with the words: “…and that was the last time anyone in our street laughed at old Mr Dutta’s umbrella.”

Show working

opening transition conclusion — the three moves are marked in every model.

Mr Dutta’s umbrella was the most famous object on Lake Road. It was black, enormous and patched in three places with blue cloth, and he carried it every single day, rain or shine, on his slow walk to the market and back. The boys at the corner tea stall called it “the tent”, and whenever he passed, one of them would shout, “Is the monsoon coming today, Dadu?” Mr Dutta would only smile and lift the umbrella a little in greeting.

I was eleven that summer and, I am ashamed to say, I laughed as loudly as anyone. My mother told me that Mr Dutta had once been a schoolmaster and that he had lived alone since his wife died, but to me he was simply the funny old man with the tent.

Everything changed on a Saturday in June. The sky had been heavy all afternoon, and by five o’clock the first storm of the monsoon broke over the city. I was coming home from tuition with my little sister Mitu when the wind struck. Within minutes the road was a river, a hoarding came crashing down across the pavement, and a live electric wire fell into the water barely ten metres ahead of us, spitting blue sparks.

I froze. Mitu was screaming, and I did not know whether to run forward or back. It was then that a huge black shape appeared out of the rain. Mr Dutta planted the umbrella in front of us like a shield, and I saw that its handle and spokes were not metal at all but thick wood and bamboo. “Hold on to the handle, both of you,” he said calmly, “and walk exactly where I walk.” Step by step he led us along the wall, far away from the wire, testing the water with the tip of the umbrella, until we reached the steps of the post office.

He waited with us until the electricity board switched off the supply, and then he walked us home. When my mother thanked him, he told us that he had carried a wooden umbrella for forty years, ever since a boy in his class had been hurt by a fallen wire. “One umbrella,” he said, “is a small price for peace of mind.”

The next morning I went to the tea stall and told the boys what had happened. Nobody said anything for a while. When Mr Dutta passed that afternoon, three of them stood up and greeted him properly for the first time, and that was the last time anyone in our street laughed at old Mr Dutta’s umbrella.

Common errorsCommon errors

  • Writing 350 or 520 words. Count as you go: about 70 words per paragraph, six paragraphs.
  • Present tense in a descriptive or narrative composition. The key says past tense and first person.
  • Argumentative: three arguments, or two for and two against. Four distinct arguments, one side.
  • Short story: changing “laughed at old Mr Dutta’s umbrella” to “laughed at Mr Dutta’s umbrella”. The words must be exactly as given, including where they are placed.
  • Reflective: telling a story. Examples are welcome, but the answer is a set of views.
  • Headings and bullet points. A composition is continuous prose.

Why Our Grandparents Are Our Best Teachers

by Ananya Rao, Class XII

My grandmother cannot use a calculator, but she can tell you the price of rice in 1965, the name of every tree on her village road and the recipe for a pickle that has never failed. Grandparents are libraries that do not appear in any catalogue, and we are forgetting to borrow from them.

Textbooks tell us what happened; grandparents tell us what it felt like. A chapter on Partition becomes real when a grandfather describes the train he travelled on at the age of nine.

Beyond memories, they carry skills. Knitting, gardening, repairing a bicycle, telling a story without a book: these are crafts that vanish in a single generation unless somebody learns them. Just as valuable are the quieter lessons of patience, thrift and respect, which they teach by example rather than by lecture.

Sadly, the contact is shrinking. Families move to cities, both parents work, and a weekly video call replaces the evening on the veranda. Many of us know our grandparents only as voices on a phone.

Yet technology can also be the bridge. A grandchild who teaches a grandparent to send messages opens a daily channel; a recorded interview can preserve a family’s history for ever.

Schools can help too. A Grandparents’ Day, an oral-history project for the history class, or a hobby club where elders teach their crafts would cost little and give much. Students can simply spend one hour a week listening.

The next time your grandfather begins, “When I was your age…”, put the phone down. You are about to attend the best class of the week.

A Rocket Scientist’s Journey

Book: Wings of Fire: An Autobiography · Author: A. P. J. Abdul Kalam with Arun Tiwari · Genre: autobiography · Publisher: Universities Press

Reviewed by Kabir Sethi, Class XII

Few autobiographies make a reader want to study harder, but this one does. Wings of Fire is Dr A. P. J. Abdul Kalam’s account of his life up to the early 1990s, written with Arun Tiwari, and it is as much a history of Indian rocketry as the story of one man.

The book opens in Rameswaram, where the young Kalam grows up in a modest home, the son of a boat owner, and helps his family by distributing newspapers. We meet the teachers, relatives and friends who shaped him, and later the scientists he worked with, above all Dr Vikram Sarabhai and Professor Satish Dhawan.

The story follows his journey from school in Ramanathapuram and college in Tiruchirappalli to engineering in Madras, and then into India’s space and defence laboratories. He leads the team that builds the country’s first satellite launch vehicle, suffers a public failure, and learns from it before the launch finally succeeds. The later chapters describe the missile programme he directed.

Two themes stand out. One is that humble beginnings need not limit a dream. The other is leadership: Kalam repeatedly shows how a good leader stands by the team in failure and shares the credit for success.

The style is simple and sincere, full of gratitude and occasional poetry. The part I liked most describes the failed launch and its aftermath, when his seniors stood by him and the team was allowed to try again.

I rate the book four and a half out of five. A few technical passages are heavy going, but every student who has ever lacked confidence should read it.

Unplugged Teens

Seven Days Without My Phone

Posted on 14 November 2026 · by Meera Iyer (@meera_writes)

Confession: last month my phone told me I had spent six hours a day on it. Six hours! That is a working day. So I switched it off, handed it to my mother and asked her not to return it for seven days.

The first two days were honestly awful. I reached for my pocket every few minutes, like someone feeling for a lost tooth. I was sure I was missing important messages, and I checked the clock so often that my brother started timing me.

By day three, something changed. I had time on my hands, so I used it. I finished a novel that had sat on my shelf for a year, cycled to the lake with a friend, and helped my father repair the kitchen tap. I even rediscovered board games, and discovered that my grandmother is ruthless at carrom.

The biggest surprise was sleep. Without a screen at midnight, I fell asleep an hour earlier and woke up without an alarm. In class I could concentrate through a whole chemistry period, and my homework took half the usual time.

Did I miss anything? I missed the family group photographs and a few jokes from my friends. I did not miss the endless scrolling, the arguments in comment sections or the feeling of being slightly anxious all the time.

So here is my challenge to you. You do not have to give up your phone for a week. Try one phone-free evening, and tell me in the comments what you did with it. I think you will be surprised.

Speeding Car Injures Two Students Outside School

By Our Staff Reporter

PUNE, 12 November: Two Class VIII students were injured this morning when a speeding car struck them outside Green Park Academy on Baner Road, minutes before the school gates opened. The accident took place at about 7.40 a.m., when the road is crowded with pupils, parents and school buses.

According to the police, the car was travelling well above the speed limit when the driver lost control while overtaking a bus. It mounted the footpath and hit the two boys, aged thirteen, before crashing into a lamp post. The driver, a 24-year-old resident of Aundh, was detained at the spot.

The injured students were rushed to Sahyadri Hospital by teachers. One suffered a fractured leg; the other was treated for head injuries and is under observation. Doctors described both as stable.

Eyewitnesses said the accident had been waiting to happen. “Cars race down this stretch every morning. There is no speed-breaker, no zebra crossing and no traffic police,” said Mr Anil Kulkarni, who runs a stationery shop opposite the school.

Within an hour, more than two hundred parents and residents gathered at the gate and blocked the road for forty minutes. They demanded speed-breakers, a pedestrian crossing and a traffic warden during school hours, and submitted a memorandum to the ward office.

Worked exampleTime: No sooner / Hardly / Scarcely ↔ As soon as

rank 1 · 8 of 9 boards No sooner takes than; Hardly and Scarcely take when. All three invert the auxiliary. For a past event use had + past participle (with No sooner, did + base form is also correct); for a present habit use does / do + base form.

FromTo
As soon as + past, clauseNo sooner had + S + pp … than …
As soon as + past, clauseHardly / Scarcely had + S + pp … when …
As soon as + present, clauseNo sooner does + S + base … than …

Try these five.

  1. As soon as the guests arrived, the lights went out. (Begin: No sooner)
  2. As soon as the bell rings, the canteen fills up. (Begin: No sooner)
  3. The match had just begun when it started to rain. (Begin: Hardly)
  4. As soon as she opened the letter, she burst into tears. (Begin: Scarcely)
  5. No sooner had the thief seen the police than he ran away. (Begin: As soon as)

Traps.

  • Hardly … than or No sooner … when: the partners are fixed.
  • No sooner the guests had arrived: the auxiliary must come before the subject.
  • No sooner does the bell rings: after does the verb is the base form.
Show working
  1. No sooner had the guests arrived than the lights went out. Also accepted: No sooner did the guests arrive than …
  2. No sooner does the bell ring than the canteen fills up. (present habit: does + base)
  3. Hardly had the match begun when it started to rain.
  4. Scarcely had she opened the letter when she burst into tears.
  5. As soon as the thief saw the police, he ran away. (undo the inversion; past simple)

FromTo

ConceptSequence of tenses

  • After a past main verb, a subordinate verb is normally past: He said that he was busy.
  • An earlier past action takes the past perfect: When we arrived, the film had begun.
  • A universal truth keeps the present: The teacher said that the earth moves round the sun.
  • A clause of comparison may take any tense: He liked her more than he likes me.
  • since (time) is followed by a past simple, the main verb by a present perfect: I have lived here since I was five.

(2)By the summer that Anjali turned fourteen, however, the pond was dying. The fish had disappeared, and the number of herons that came each winter had dwindled to two or three. Most villagers now drew water from a tanker that came twice a week, and they had begun to talk of the pond as a thing of the past.

(3)Anjali’s grandfather, Master Ramdas, who had taught in the village school for thirty years, refused to accept this. “A pond is not lost,” he told the panchayat, “until the people who need it stop caring.” The members listened politely and did nothing. They were reluctant to spend money on what they considered a lost cause.

(4)So Master Ramdas began without them. On the first Sunday of April he walked down the steps with a spade and a basket, and Anjali, somewhat embarrassed, followed with a second basket. For two hours they dug out silt and carried it to the fields, where, he explained to anyone who stopped to stare, it would make excellent manure. By the end of the morning a few boys had joined them, mostly out of curiosity.

(5)The next Sunday there were eleven volunteers, and the Sunday after that more than forty. Master Ramdas divided them into teams. One team removed the plastic and burned nothing, sorting it instead for a recycling dealer in the town. Another hauled out the weeds and the rotting logs. The farmers, who had seen the silt enrich their fields, lent their bullock carts to carry it away.

(6)Then he turned his attention to the spring. With two old men who remembered where it had flowed, he traced the channel up the hill and found it blocked by a landslide of stones and roots. It took the volunteers three weekends to clear it, and on the evening the water first trickled down into the pond, half the village came to watch in silence.

(7)Master Ramdas knew that cleaning a pond once was not enough. He persuaded the school to make Anjali’s class the guardians of the pond, with a rota for checking the banks each week. He wrote to the district office, enclosing photographs of the work, and was granted funds to repair the stone steps and fence the spring. Finally, he asked the panchayat to fine anyone who threw waste into the water, and this time, with the whole village watching, the members agreed at once.

(8)The monsoon that year was generous. By September the pond was deep and clear again, and in December Anjali counted nineteen herons standing at its edge. Her grandfather did not say anything; he simply sat on the bank until it was dark, as though he were listening to the water.
