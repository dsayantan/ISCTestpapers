In Act III of the play, Pygmalion, what does Clara’s eager imitation of Eliza’s talk reveal about her? (Interpret)

Answer

Clara is desperate to seem fashionable. When Higgins passes off Eliza’s shocking remarks as the new small talk, she copies them at once and even leaves using Eliza’s oath. It shows that she has no judgement of her own, and that fashionable manners are mere imitation.

In Act III of the play, Pygmalion, what does Mrs Higgins mean when she calls Eliza a triumph of Higgins’s art and of her dressmaker’s? (Interpret)

Answer

She grants that Eliza looks right and pronounces perfectly, thanks to Higgins’s teaching and her fine clothes. But she insists that Eliza gives herself away in every sentence she speaks. The surface of a lady is finished; the substance is not.

In the poem, Refugee Blues, why does the speaker mention the poodle in a jacket and the cat let in through a door? (Interpret)

Answer

The pampered poodle and the cat enjoy comfort, shelter and welcome, all of which are refused to the German Jews. The contrast shows a society that values its pets above human beings in danger, and so exposes the cruelty and absurdity of the refugees’ rejection.

Question 320 marks
(i)5 marks

In Act V, how does Eliza react when her father suddenly appears? What does the moment show about her new self?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • Her reaction
  • What it reveals about her transformation
Answer

Eliza has just been talking to Pickering with complete self-possession, explaining that she can no longer utter her old sounds. At that moment Doolittle steps in from the balcony in his wedding clothes. Taken utterly by surprise, she lets out her old flower-girl cry, and for an instant the poised young lady vanishes. The moment shows that her transformation is real but not complete: her new manner is a learnt habit that a sudden shock can break. Higgins crows in triumph, but Eliza quickly recovers her dignity, which proves how far she has come.

Model answer: 94 words (limit 80–100)

Forecast #55 (Tier C) raised to a paragraph. Act V; the one moment that undoes Eliza’s claim, so it tests judgement as well as recall.
(ii)5 marks

In Act V, Eliza tells Higgins that she may marry Freddy. What does Freddy offer her that Higgins does not?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • What Eliza says about Freddy
  • What Freddy gives her
Answer

In the final quarrel Higgins mocks Freddy as a poor fool, but Eliza defends him. Freddy is weak and has no money, she admits, yet he loves her and needs her, and he might make her happier than her so-called betters, who bully her and do not want her. His love, she says, makes him king enough for her. Higgins offers only his genius and his even-handed indifference, and would keep her fetching his slippers. Freddy gives her what Higgins never does: admiration, tenderness and the sense that she matters for herself, not as someone else’s achievement.

(iii)10 marks

(a) How does Shaw explore the relationship between speech and class in Acts III to V of the play?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates should build their answer on the following areas:

  • Class in England as largely a matter of speech
  • The at-home: flawless vowels, gutter matter; she gives herself away in every sentence
  • What one says matters as much as how
  • Clara copies the ‘new small talk’: fashionable speech is imitation
  • Act V: the new speech has become part of Eliza, though a shock still breaks it
  • Doolittle raised by money alone: income as a second route into a class
  • Beyond phonetics: a lady is made by how she is treated

Model answer

Shaw’s central idea is that class in England is largely a matter of how people speak, and Acts III to V test that idea.

The at-home in Act III shows how far speech alone can carry a person. Eliza’s pronunciation is flawless, yet her subject matter, her aunt’s death and the gin, belongs to the gutter, and Mrs Higgins sees that she gives herself away in every sentence. Correct vowels are not enough; what one says matters as much as how.

The play also shows how easily fashionable speech is imitated. Clara takes Eliza’s slang for the new small talk and adopts it at once: upper-class manners are performance.

By Act V the change in Eliza’s speech has become part of her. She tells Pickering that she can no longer utter her old sounds, although the shock of her father’s arrival still brings one out. Doolittle, by contrast, is lifted into the middle class by money alone, which shows that income can change class as surely as speech.

Shaw’s conclusion goes beyond phonetics. A new accent may open doors, but, as Eliza says, a lady is really made by how she is treated.

Model answer: 200 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Pygmalion themes (class and language); the play’s central idea, and not set in the specimen.

(b) What does Higgins’s relationship with his mother reveal about him? Refer to Acts III and V of the play.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to elaborate on the following ideas:

  • Act III: arrives on her at-home day against her wishes; scolded for his manners
  • Shows off the experiment like a boy with a toy; ‘a pretty pair of babies’
  • Mrs Higgins as his conscience: the problem of what becomes of Eliza
  • Act V: rebukes him for calling the police; shelters Eliza; makes him behave
  • His ideal woman is modelled on his mother, so he stays a bachelor
  • The sequel: his devotion to his mother is one reason Eliza will not marry him

Model answer

Higgins’s visits to his mother show the child inside the brilliant professor. In Act III he bursts in on her at-home day, when she has asked him to stay away, and is at once scolded for his manners. He brings her his experiment as a boy shows off a new toy, and she treats him accordingly, calling the two men a pair of babies playing with a live doll.

Mrs Higgins is also his conscience. She sees at once what he cannot: that Eliza has been given a lady’s manners without a lady’s means. In Act V, when he rushes in because Eliza has vanished, she rebukes him for going to the police, shelters Eliza upstairs, and makes him promise to behave before he may see her.

The relationship also explains why he remains a confirmed bachelor. He admits that his idea of a lovable woman is someone as like his mother as possible, and no younger woman measures up. Shaw’s sequel says so directly: his devotion to his mother is one reason Eliza could never marry him. Higgins is a genius, but emotionally he has never grown up.

Model answer: 193 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast #119 (Tier E). Mrs Higgins is the moral voice of Acts III and V.

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Interpret)

  • The wife’s behaviour during the game
  • What it shows about the narrator

(iii)10 marks

(a) In the short story, The Father-Thing, Charles proves braver and wiser than the adults around him. Do you agree? Give reasons.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates should build their answer on the following areas:

  • A clear judgement: the adults fail to see; the boy sees, acts, endures
  • Wisdom: trusting his own perception in the garage and at dinner; the mother disbelieves
  • Courage: the trash barrel and the skin; going to Peretti, who had beaten him
  • Tracking the creature; the half-grown copies of himself and his mother
  • Not giving up until the boys burn the creature
  • Courage as fear overcome, not fearlessness

Model answer

I agree. The adults fail to see what is in front of them, while Charles, a frightened boy, sees, acts and endures.

His wisdom lies first in trusting his own perception. In the garage he sees two identical fathers, and at dinner he knows at once that the thing at the table is not his father. His mother notices nothing, and when he tries to tell her, she does not believe him.

His courage shows in what he does next. He goes to the garage and finds his father’s dried, hollow skin in the trash barrel. He then turns to Tony Peretti, a boy who has beaten him before, because he needs someone strong enough to believe him and help.

With Peretti and Bobby Daniels he tracks the creature to its burrow and finds the half-grown copies of himself and his mother. Knowing that he is next, he does not give up, and the boys finally burn the creature.

His courage is not fearlessness. He is terrified throughout and weeps over the trash barrel, yet he goes on, which makes him braver than adults who never face anything at all.

Model answer: 191 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Father-Thing themes (a child’s perception disbelieved by adults). The specimen’s omitted story: 9 of 10 omitted texts reached the board’s Section B.

(b) How does Anita Desai present the world of children in the short story, Games at Twilight, as a small society with its own power and cruelty?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are required to focus on the following areas in their answers:

  • Power belongs to the strongest: Raghu as ‘It’; the smaller children scatter
  • Quarrels before the game; the game as a contest the weak hope to survive
  • Ravi near the bottom: his dream in the shed is a dream of status
  • The group’s cruelty: forgotten, brushed aside, the funeral game
  • Cruelty through indifference rather than intent
  • Childhood has its hierarchies; Ravi learns his place in them

Model answer

Desai’s children are not innocent playmates but a small society with its own rulers and victims.

Power belongs to the strongest. Raghu, older, bigger and athletic, is ‘It’, and he enjoys the fear he causes; the smaller children scatter before him. The children quarrel noisily before they settle on hide-and-seek, and the game itself is a contest in which the weak hope only to avoid capture.

Ravi is near the bottom of this order. Hiding in the dark shed, he is frightened but also thrilled, because he imagines for the first time a victory over Raghu that would make him the champion. His dream is a dream of status.

The cruelty of the group appears at the end. When Ravi at last races to the den and claims his victory, nobody cares. The others have forgotten him and moved on to a solemn funeral game in a ring, and they brush him aside. No one means to hurt him; the cruelty lies in indifference.

Desai shows that childhood has its hierarchies, just as adult life does. Ravi’s discovery of his own insignificance is the price of learning how that society works.

Model answer: 193 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast brief (the loneliness and cruelty of peer groups); spec 4(iii)(b) on a child’s thoughts and fears.

In the poem, My Grandmother’s House, why does the poet wish to return to the house? Why can she never truly go back?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Interpret)

  • Her reasons for wanting to return
  • What makes a real return impossible
Answer

The poet wishes to return because her grandmother’s house was the one place where she was loved without condition. Her present life lacks such love, and she wants to stand in that house again, look through its windows and listen to its silence. Yet a real return is impossible. The grandmother is dead, and the love has gone with her; the house has withdrawn into silence and decay, its windows blind and its air frozen. All she could bring back is darkness, a memory to keep behind her bedroom door. The past survives only in her mind.

  • The final scene in the snow

(iii)10 marks

(a) Comment on the title of the poem, Urban. Suggest an alternative title and give reasons for your choice.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates should build their answer on the following areas:

  • A single abstract adjective: a condition, not a place
  • No city is named: the experience of every modern city-dweller
  • The poem fills in the condition: far hills, unseen sky, the circles in his head
  • The city inside him: the traffic of the mind; longing without action
  • The impersonal title matches the detached ‘he’
  • A reasoned alternative (e.g. ‘Circles’, ‘The Far Hills’), weighed against the original

Model answer

‘Urban’ is a single, flat adjective, and its bareness suits the poem. It names not a place but a condition: the state of a man whom the city has shaped so completely that it has become his nature. By naming no city, Ezekiel suggests that the experience belongs to every modern city-dweller.

The poem fills out what that condition means. For this man the hills are always far away; he sees no sky, no breeze reaches him, his river is dry, and he knows only the broken roads and the circles traced inside his head. The city is not merely around him but inside him, in the traffic of his mind. He dreams of solitary morning walks, yet never takes them, and is drawn back to the crowd. The impersonal title matches the detached ‘he’ of the poem: he is a type as much as a person.

An alternative might be ‘Circles’, which captures both his routine and his mind’s endless repetitions, or ‘The Far Hills’, which points to the longing for nature he never acts on. Neither, however, has the cold generality of ‘Urban’, which is why the original title works best.

Model answer: 194 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast #111 (Tier D); booklet 10(b). Spec 5(ii) examined Urban’s claustrophobia; the title question reaches the same idea from a new angle.

(b) The poem, Ode to Tomatoes, celebrates community and the shared meal rather than the tomato alone. Do you agree? Give reasons.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates have to address the following areas in their answers:

  • A clear judgement: the tomato as the means, shared life as the subject
  • Summer: the tomato fills the streets; abundance no one household owns
  • December: the kitchen, glasses, butter dishes, saltcellars; ordinary family life
  • The wedding with onion, oil, salt and pepper as a communal ritual; parsley, potatoes, the roast
  • Generosity: no husk or thorns; a gift to every table
  • Odes to common things for common people: the Chilean kitchen and the family meal

Model answer

I agree. The tomato is the poem’s hero, but what Neruda celebrates through it is the life people share around food.

From the start the tomato belongs to everyone. In summer it fills the streets, and its juice seems to run through the whole town, an abundance that no single household owns. In December it enters the kitchen and settles among the glasses, butter dishes and saltcellars, the ordinary objects of family life.

The central episode is a social ritual. The tomato is cut and then wedded to onion, oil, salt and pepper. A wedding is the most communal of celebrations, and the language turns the making of a salad into a festive occasion. Parsley, potatoes and the roast join in, as if guests were arriving at a feast.

The tomato’s generosity completes the picture. It has no husk or thorns, gives itself entirely and offers its colour and freshness to every table.

Neruda wrote his odes to common things for common people, and the poem honours the Chilean kitchen and the family meal. The tomato matters because it brings people together, which is why the poem is finally a celebration of community.

Model answer: 195 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast brief (the shared meal, Chilean kitchen culture); booklet MCQ on how we know the tomato matters to Chilean culture.
