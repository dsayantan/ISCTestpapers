Question 210 marks

Answer any five of the following subparts in 40-50 words.

(i)2 marks

In Act III of Pygmalion, why does Mrs Higgins warn the two men about what is to be done with Eliza afterwards? (Understand)

Answer

Mrs Higgins sees what the men ignore. Higgins has given Eliza the manners and speech of a lady but no income or place in society. Once the experiment ends she cannot return to the gutter or live as a lady. What is to become of her afterwards is the problem.

Model answer: 50 words (limit 40–50)

Forecast Tier C 51; the ethics of the experiment is the play’s central question.
(ii)2 marks

In Act IV of Pygmalion, why does Eliza hand back the ring, and how does Higgins react? (Understand)

Answer

Eliza returns the ring and the jewellery so that she cannot be accused of taking what is not hers; she will keep nothing that makes her his property. Stung, Higgins flings the ring into the fireplace and loses his temper, for once genuinely hurt by her.

Model answer: 46 words (limit 40–50)

Forecast Tier C 49 / Tier B 40: the ring is one of the play’s key symbols.
(iii)2 marks

In the short story, The Dreamer, why was Cyprian not wearing a hat? (Recall)

Answer

Cyprian explains that a hat is a nuisance while shopping. If you meet someone you know, you must raise it, which is awkward when your hands are full of parcels. The explanation sounds harmless, but being hatless later lets him pass as a shop assistant.

Model answer: 45 words (limit 40–50)

Forecast Tier C 75; booklet SA (ii).
(iv)2 marks

In the short story, Games at Twilight, why is the shed frightening to Ravi? (Understand)

Answer

The shed behind the garage is dark, forbidden and full of discarded junk. It smells of rats, ant hills, dust and spider webs, and Ravi fears less definable horrors lurking there. Being small and alone, he is terrified, though he stays to avoid being caught.

Model answer: 45 words (limit 40–50)

Forecast Tier C 80.
(v)2 marks

In the poem, Ode to Tomatoes, what does the poet mean by saying that the summer light is ‘halved like a tomato’? (Interpret)

Answer

At midday in summer the light is split, like a tomato cut in two, and spills over the street like juice. The simile fuses the sun and the tomato, suggesting abundance, heat and colour, and shows how completely the fruit fills the season.

Model answer: 43 words (limit 40–50)

Forecast Tier C 89; booklet 5-mark prompt on the same simile.
(vi)2 marks

In the poem, Urban, what is meant by saying that the man moves in circles ‘tracked within his head’? (Interpret)

Answer

His life is a closed routine. He follows the same mental paths again and again, trapped by habit and anxiety rather than by the city alone. The circles suggest futility: he moves constantly but never arrives anywhere new or free.

Model answer: 40 words (limit 40–50)

Forecast Tier C 86; booklet SA.

SECTION B – 60 MARKS
PYGMALION: GEORGE BERNARD SHAW

Question 320 marks
(i)5 marks

In Act IV of Pygmalion, Eliza quarrels bitterly with Higgins after the reception. Why does she lose her temper, and what does her question about her future reveal?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • Eliza’s anger and the slippers
  • Her question: what is to become of her?
Answer

Back at Wimpole Street, Higgins and Pickering congratulate themselves on the night’s triumph as if Eliza were not there. When Higgins asks for his slippers, her pent-up hurt explodes and she flings them at him, crying that she won his bet and is nothing to him. Her question, what is to become of her, is the heart of the play. She has been lifted out of her old life and fitted for none. Higgins’s offhand suggestions of marriage or a shop only show that he never thought about her as a person.

Model answer: 94 words (limit 80–100)

Forecast Tier B 35; specimen 3(ii) examined the same night, so it is examined here from Eliza’s side.
(ii)5 marks

Describe Alfred Doolittle’s appearance and complaint in Act V of Pygmalion. What does Shaw satirise through him?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Interpret)

  • His new condition and whom he blames
  • The satire on middle-class morality
Answer

Doolittle arrives dressed splendidly for a wedding, yet he is furious, not grateful. Higgins’s joking letter to the American millionaire Wannafeller has brought him a large yearly income on condition that he lectures for a moral reform league. Once a carefree member of the undeserving poor, he is now burdened with respectability, besieged by relatives, and must even marry. Through him Shaw mocks middle-class morality as a set of anxieties and duties bought with money, suggesting that class is a matter of income, not of virtue.

Model answer: 87 words (limit 80–100)

Forecast Tier B 34 / Tier C 54: Doolittle’s transformation parallels Eliza’s.
(iii)10 marks

(a) Eliza says that the difference between a lady and a flower girl lies not in how she behaves but in how she is treated. Discuss this statement with close reference to Higgins and Pickering in Acts III to V.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates should build their answer on the following areas:

  • Eliza’s claim in Act V: identity depends on treatment
  • Pickering: courtesy, ‘Miss Doolittle’, standing up, opening doors
  • Pickering’s manners as the source of her self-respect
  • Higgins: rudeness, neglect after the reception, the slippers
  • Higgins’s defence of ‘the same manner for all’
  • Conclusion: she will always be a flower girl to Higgins, a lady to Pickering

Model answer

In Act V Eliza tells Pickering that she learned to be a lady from him, not from Higgins. Phonetics gave her the accent, but self-respect came from the way she was treated.

Pickering always behaved to her as a gentleman behaves to a lady. He called her Miss Doolittle from the first, stood up when she entered, took off his hat and opened doors. Such small courtesies, she says, came naturally to him, and they taught her that she was worth respecting.

Higgins, by contrast, treats her as material for an experiment. After the reception he talks across her, thanks God the business is over and calls for his slippers, never thanking the person who won his bet. He defends himself by saying he treats a duchess and a flower girl alike, but that only means he is equally rude to everyone.

Eliza’s conclusion is exact: to Higgins she will always be a flower girl, because he treats her as one; to Pickering she can be a lady. Shaw’s point is that class is a social performance sustained by others’ behaviour, and dignity is conferred by respect.

Model answer: 189 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Tier B 31: the thesis of Act V; specimen key 3(iii)(a) bullets Pickering’s courtesy.
OR

(b) Shaw calls Pygmalion ‘a romance’, yet refuses to let Eliza marry Higgins. Is the play a romance? Give reasons for your answer with reference to the ending and to Shaw’s sequel.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to elaborate on the following ideas:

  • Title and myth: Pygmalion loves his statue; Higgins does not
  • ‘Romance’ in the older sense: a transformation, like Cinderella
  • The ending: errands, Higgins laughing at the idea of Freddy
  • Eliza’s independence: kindness, not love-making; teaching phonetics
  • The sequel: Eliza marries Freddy; the flower shop; Higgins’s mother-fixation
  • Evaluation: an anti-romance that is romantic only about a woman’s freedom

Model answer

The title promises a love story. In the myth, the sculptor falls in love with the statue he has made. Shaw deliberately refuses that ending: Higgins creates a lady but never falls in love with her.

The play is a romance only in the older sense of a marvellous transformation, a Cinderella story in which a flower girl passes for a duchess. Its emotional climax, however, is not a proposal but Eliza’s declaration of independence. In Act V she asks for kindness, not love-making, and threatens to marry Freddy and to teach phonetics herself. Higgins admires her new strength, yet the play ends with him handing her errands and laughing at the idea of Freddy, a comic and unresolved close.

Shaw’s sequel removes any doubt. Eliza marries Freddy, and they open a flower shop with help from Pickering. She cannot marry Higgins, Shaw explains, because he is a confirmed bachelor devoted to his mother and indifferent to her needs.

In my view, therefore, Pygmalion is an anti-romance. It uses romantic expectations only to overturn them, and its real romance is Eliza’s discovery of her own worth.

Model answer: 189 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Tier E 118; specimen 3(iii)(b) (why Freddy) and its key cite the sequel.

Question 420 marks
(i)5 marks

In the short story, The Father-Thing, what does Charles see in the garage, and how does he know that the man at the dinner table is not his father?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • The scene in the garage
  • The signs at the dinner table
Answer

Sent by his mother to call his father to dinner, Charles finds two identical fathers in the garage: his father is talking to an exact replica of himself. Only one comes in to eat. At the table this ‘father’ looks right, but his eyes are cold and empty, his voice lacks feeling and his manner is subtly wrong, like an imitation. Charles cannot convince his mother, but he knows the real father has gone, and he bolts from the table. The horror lies in the familiar becoming strange.

In the short story, The Doll’s House, why are the Kelvey sisters shunned, and how do they respond when Lena Logan taunts them?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • Reasons for their exclusion
  • Lil’s and Else’s response
Answer

The Kelveys are shunned because of class. Their mother is a hard-working washerwoman and their father is rumoured to be in prison, so the respectable families forbid their daughters to speak to them. Even the teacher has a special voice for them, and their odd cast-off clothes mark them out. When Lena Logan, egged on by the other girls, asks Lil whether she will be a servant, Lil only gives a silly, shamefaced smile, and little Else stays close to her. Their silence shows long, patient endurance of cruelty.

(iii)10 marks

(a) In the short story, Games at Twilight, why is Ravi forgotten by the other children, and what lies at the centre of his heartbreak?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are required to focus on the following areas in their answers:

  • Ravi hides in the dark shed; overcomes fear; dreams of beating Raghu
  • He waits too long, lost in fantasies of victory
  • The others move on; twilight; the funeral ring-game
  • He touches the den; no one cares; they brush him aside
  • The centre of his grief: not losing but being insignificant
  • The ending as an epiphany about the indifference of the world

Model answer

Ravi, small and usually ignored, hides from Raghu in the dark lumber shed behind the garage. After his first terror of the dark and its unseen horrors, he feels a growing sense of triumph: he will not merely escape being caught, he will defeat the big bully and be the champion.

That dream is his undoing. He stays hidden so long, imagining glory, that the game ends without him. The others move on, and as twilight falls the children begin a solemn funeral ring-game. Nobody thinks of Ravi, because to them he was never important enough to miss.

When he finally bursts out and touches the den, shouting his victory, the others are merely puzzled. They had quite forgotten him, and his triumph means nothing to them.

Ravi weeps, but not over the game. At the centre of his heartbreak is the discovery that he does not matter. The funeral game ironically marks the death of his childish hope. Desai shows how a child’s inner world, intense and dramatic, can be crushed by the casual indifference of others. Ravi falls silent at last, overwhelmed by a sense of his own insignificance.

Model answer: 192 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Tier B 25; specimen 4(iii)(b) asked about a child’s thoughts and fears, so this is its companion question.
OR

(b) Referring closely to the short story, Death by Scrabble, explain the ending. In your opinion, is the narrator’s death caused by the game, by coincidence or by his own mind?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates have to address the following areas in their answers:

  • The narrator’s hatred and violent fantasies
  • Words on the board seem to come true (ZAPS, EXPLODES, FLY, QUAKE)
  • His belief that he can kill his wife with a word
  • The twist: he dies himself, choking on a tile he chews
  • Readings: supernatural, coincidence, self-made (unreliable narrator)
  • A reasoned personal conclusion, with poetic justice

Model answer

Throughout the game the narrator broods on his hatred for his wife. Irritated by the heat and by her smug concentration, he chews the tiles and imagines killing her.

Then the words on the board seem to come true. After ZAPS, EXPLODES, FLY and QUAKE, events around the house appear to echo the words. He becomes convinced that the game controls reality and plans a word that will kill his wife. The ending turns the plot on him: it is the narrator who dies, choking on the tile in his mouth.

A supernatural reading is possible, and the story’s magic realism invites it. Each ‘event’ could, however, be a coincidence, magnified by a heated, resentful mind. The first-person narrator is unreliable; we see only what his obsession lets him see.

In my opinion the death is self-made. His nervous habit of chewing tiles, fed by rage, kills him, and his belief in the words is his own delusion. The ending is poetic justice: the man who wished to murder with words is destroyed by his own malice.

Model answer: 178 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Tier B 24; booklet 10(a); specimen 4(iii)(a) was on the marriage, so the ending is the likeliest board shift.

Question 520 marks
(i)5 marks

In the poem, My Grandmother’s House, describe the condition of the house after the grandmother’s death. What does it reveal about the speaker’s feelings?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • The physical state of the house
  • Its atmosphere and the speaker’s feelings
Answer

After the grandmother dies, the house falls into silence and neglect. Snakes move among the books the speaker was then too young to read, the windows are ‘blind’, and the air seems frozen. The house, once full of love, becomes a dead place, personified as withdrawing into itself. Its decay mirrors the speaker’s own loss: her blood turns cold, and warmth leaves her life. The description reveals deep grief and nostalgia. The house stands for the only unconditional love she has known, and its ruin is her inner desolation.

(ii)5 marks

In the poem, The Black Santa, how is the Black Santa different from the Santa Claus the child knows from television and advertisements?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • His physical appearance
  • His clothing, manner and what he means to the child
Answer

The Santa of television is fat, white and endlessly merry. The Black Santa the child meets is thin and shaky, with a bony lap, and his Santa suit and beard are plainly a costume rather than the plush perfection of the advertisements. He is no perfect figure from a card or a store window but an ordinary, flawed man from a poor neighbourhood. Yet the child still trusts him with a whole wish-list. His imperfection makes him real, and his colour makes him belong to her community. The poem thus questions whose image defines a shared festival.

(iii)10 marks

(a) How does the poem, Refugee Blues, convey the refugees’ loss of home, identity and dignity?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to use the following approach to the question:

  • A city of ten million with no place for them; the lost homeland
  • The passport and the consul: ‘officially dead’; identity erased
  • The committee, the public speaker: indifference and hostility
  • Animals and nature free (poodle, cat, fish, birds) while they are not
  • The dream building and the soldiers in the snow: exclusion, persecution
  • The refrain ‘my dear’: intimacy and quiet despair

Model answer

Auden’s speaker is a German Jewish refugee addressing a companion in an unnamed city in 1939. The poem is a catalogue of losses.

The first is home. In a city of ten million there is no room for them, and the country they once called theirs is closed to them. Even the old yew in the churchyard renews itself each spring, but their old passports cannot be renewed.

The second is identity. A consul thumps the table and declares that, without a passport, they are officially dead. A committee politely tells them to come back next year; a public speaker claims they will steal the nation’s bread. Bureaucracy and prejudice have turned people into problems.

The third is dignity. A poodle in a jacket and a cat are let in while German Jews are not; fish swim and birds sing freely. The contrast shows them valued below animals. The dream of a building with a thousand doors, none of them theirs, and the soldiers searching the snowy plain complete the picture of exclusion and hunted fear.

The weary refrain, ‘my dear’, keeps a small human tenderness alive amid despair, making the sorrow personal.

Model answer: 195 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Tier A 5; specimen 5(iii)(a) on the sorrow of refugees; poetry specimen foci recur at 75%.

(b) Examine the devices Nissim Ezekiel uses in the poem, Urban, to portray the alienation of the modern city-dweller.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates should build their answer on the following areas:

  • Detached third-person ‘he’: clinical distance
  • Imagery of absence: distant hills, dry river, no sky, no breeze
  • Metaphor: circles tracked in the head; the traffic of the mind
  • Simile: the city burns like a passion
  • Irony: dreams of solitary walks but turns back to the clamour
  • Regular stanza form mirroring routine; the one-word title

Model answer

Ezekiel never enters the man’s mind directly. He describes ‘he’ from the outside in a cool, clinical third person, and this detachment is itself the first sign of alienation: the man is a type, not an individual.

The poem is built on imagery of absence. The hills are always far away, the river is dry and no breeze reaches him. He sees no sky, and neither sun nor rain enters his life. Nature exists only as something missing.

Metaphor carries the inner prison. He moves in circles tracked within his head, and his mind turns its own ‘traffic’, so the city’s congestion has entered his thoughts. The city is said to burn like a passion, a simile suggesting both fever and consumption.

Irony completes the portrait. He dreams of morning walks alone, yet his mind always returns to the familiar clamour close at hand. He longs for escape but does not act. Even the neat, regular stanzas mirror his fixed routine, and the abstract one-word title, Urban, suggests a condition rather than a place. The poem thus shows alienation as both an outer and an inner state.

Model answer: 189 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Tier A 12; specimen 5(ii) (claustrophobia); booklet 10(a).
