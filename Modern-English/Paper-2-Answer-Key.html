Question 210 marks

Answer any five of the following subparts in 40-50 words.

(i)2 marks

In Act V of Pygmalion, why does Eliza suddenly let out her old flower-girl cry on seeing her father? (Interpret)

Answer

She has just claimed that she can no longer make her old sounds. The shock of seeing Doolittle, dressed like a gentleman, breaks her control and the old habit returns. It shows that her new self is still fragile, and Higgins triumphantly seizes on it.

In Act V of Pygmalion, what does Higgins mean when he calls Eliza a ‘consort battleship’? (Interpret)

Answer

When Eliza defies him, Higgins is delighted. He sees that she is no longer a clinging dependant but an independent, strong woman, fit to sail beside him and Pickering as an equal. The naval image shows admiration, although it still reveals his impersonal way of seeing her.

(iii)2 marks

In the short story, Death by Scrabble, why does the narrator feel such hatred for his wife? (Understand)

Answer

The narrator is bored, frustrated and trapped in a stale marriage. On a sweltering day everything his wife does irritates him: her smug concentration, her gestures and her skill at the game. His hatred grows from resentment and boredom rather than any real wrong she has done.

In the short story, The Doll’s House, what does Else’s final remark about the lamp reveal? (Interpret)

Answer

After being chased away, Else smiles and softly says that she saw the little lamp. Humiliation cannot spoil her joy. The lamp links her with Kezia across the barrier of class, showing that beauty and human feeling belong to everyone, however poor.

In the poem, The Black Santa, why does the Black Santa matter to the child? (Understand)

Answer

He looks like the child and the people of the neighbourhood, unlike the white Santa of television. Though thin, shaky and far from perfect, he is theirs. He lets the child believe that the magic of Christmas includes Black children, which gives a sense of belonging.

(vi)2 marks

In the poem, Ode to Tomatoes, why is the tomato called a star, and what makes it a gift? (Understand)

Answer

The tomato is a star of the earth because it is round, bright and returns fruitfully every season. It is a gift because it has no pit, husk, leaves or thorns: nothing is wasted. It freely offers its fiery colour and cool, complete goodness to everyone.

SECTION B – 60 MARKS
PYGMALION: GEORGE BERNARD SHAW

Question 320 marks
(i)5 marks

In Act III of Pygmalion, after the guests leave, how do Higgins and Pickering describe their work with Eliza? How does Mrs Higgins respond?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • What the two men describe
  • Mrs Higgins’s response
Answer

Once the visitors have gone, Higgins and Pickering talk over each other with boyish excitement about ‘their’ Eliza. They describe her lessons, the clothes they choose, the concerts they take her to and how quickly she learns. Neither mentions her feelings. Mrs Higgins is not impressed. She tells them they are like a pair of babies playing with a live doll, and she raises the real problem: what is to be done with Eliza afterwards? The men brush her warning aside, which prepares for the crisis of Act IV.

Model answer: 89 words (limit 80–100)

Forecast Tier E 121 / Tier B 37: Mrs Higgins’s warning is the moral centre of Act III.
(ii)5 marks

Describe the episode of the ring in Act IV of Pygmalion. What does it show about Eliza and Higgins?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Interpret)

  • What Eliza does
  • What it shows about both characters
Answer

Before leaving Wimpole Street, Eliza asks which clothes belong to her and hands back her jewellery, including the ring Higgins bought her, so that nobody can call her a thief. Higgins, stung for once, flings the ring into the fireplace and loses his temper. Eliza is glad she has hurt him a little; later she looks for the ring again. The episode shows her new pride and her refusal to be anyone’s possession. It also shows that Higgins, for all his coldness, has feelings he cannot admit.

Model answer: 88 words (limit 80–100)

Forecast Tier B 40.
(iii)10 marks

(a) Trace Eliza’s growth from an object in an experiment into an independent woman in Acts III to V of Pygmalion.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to elaborate on the following ideas:

  • Act III: a mechanical doll; perfect vowels, wrong content
  • The reception: her triumph, credited to Higgins
  • Act IV: the slippers, ‘what is to become of me’, the ring, leaving
  • Act V: poise; self-respect through Pickering’s treatment
  • She asks for kindness; threatens to teach phonetics
  • Higgins’s ‘consort battleship’; she refuses to be his servant

Model answer

At Mrs Higgins’s ‘at home’ in Act III, Eliza is still an object. She speaks with perfect vowels but mechanically, like a doll wound up, and her story of her aunt’s death shows that only her sound has been changed. The men discuss her as their creation.

Act IV is the turning point. After her triumph Higgins ignores her, and her anger bursts out as she flings the slippers at him. Her question about what is to become of her shows that she has begun to see herself as a person with a future. Returning the ring and walking out of the house are her first independent acts.

In Act V she is poised and self-possessed. She explains that her self-respect came from Pickering’s courtesy, not from phonetics. She asks Higgins for kindness, not love-making, and declares she can earn her own living by teaching phonetics. Higgins himself admires the change, calling her a tower of strength.

Her growth is complete when she refuses to run his errands and leaves. The girl who was once handed from man to man now makes her own choices.

Model answer: 188 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Tier B 39.
OR

(b) How does Shaw use humour to criticise the English class system in Acts III to V of Pygmalion?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are required to focus on the following areas in their answers:

  • The at-home: perfect accent, gutter content; ‘new small talk’
  • Clara’s imitation and the Eynsford Hills’ shabby gentility
  • Higgins: a gentleman with the manners of a boor
  • Doolittle: wealth brings ‘middle-class morality’ and misery
  • Eliza’s point: a lady is made by how she is treated
  • Evaluation: laughter exposes class as performance, not worth

Model answer

The comedy of the ‘at home’ depends on the gap between sound and sense. Eliza speaks in a flawless accent about her aunt being ‘done in’ and her father pouring gin down the old woman’s throat. The genteel guests cannot tell what is refined and what is not. Clara, eager to be fashionable, adopts it as the new small talk. Society’s standards prove to be empty fashions.

Higgins, a gentleman, behaves with the manners of a spoilt boy, rude to his mother’s guests and careless of their feelings, while the flower girl is the model of politeness. Breeding clearly does not produce good behaviour.

The funniest reversal belongs to Doolittle. Made rich by a legacy, he complains that he has been ruined, trapped by middle-class morality, respectability and relatives wanting money. Class is shown to be a matter of income.

Finally, Eliza’s remark that a lady is made by how she is treated turns the joke into argument. In my opinion Shaw’s humour succeeds because it lets the audience laugh first and think afterwards: class is a performance, not a measure of worth.

Model answer: 188 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Tier E 120; Shaw’s comedy of manners is a standard board focus.

With reference to the short story, The Dreamer, what kind of woman is Adela Chemping? Show how her words and her conduct differ.

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Interpret)

  • What she says about herself
  • Her conduct at the sale
Answer

Adela Chemping declares that she is no bargain hunter, yet she loves to go wherever bargains are, and Saki slyly calls this her human weakness. At the winter sales she eagerly buys things she has no real use for. She is also self-important and conventional. She takes her nephew Cyprian only as a male escort and parcel carrier, and is scandalised that he has come without a hat. Yet she is easily overwhelmed: when she sees him selling to a stranger she nearly faints. The gap between her words and deeds makes her a comic, satirical figure.

In the short story, Games at Twilight, describe Ravi’s experience in the shed. How do his feelings change while he is hiding?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • His fear in the shed
  • His growing sense of triumph
Answer

Escaping Raghu, Ravi slips into the dark, forbidden shed behind the garage. It is full of junk and smells of rats, ant hills, dust and spider webs; he fears less definable horrors around him and is terrified. Slowly, however, fear gives way to excitement. Nobody has found him, and he begins to dream of beating Raghu, the big bully, and of being the champion whom everyone admires. He stays hidden, savouring his coming victory. This change reveals a small child’s longing for recognition and prepares for the bitter disappointment of the ending.

(iii)10 marks

(a) How does Philip K. Dick build horror out of an ordinary suburban family in the short story, The Father-Thing?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates should build their answer on the following areas:

  • An ordinary evening: mother, dinner, father in the garage
  • The two fathers; the familiar made strange
  • Dinner table: cold eyes, wrong manner; the mother does not see
  • The dried skin in the trash barrel
  • The creature under the garden; replicas of Charles and his mother
  • Dramatic irony: a child’s knowledge disbelieved; what makes us human

Model answer

The story begins with the most ordinary moment in suburban life: a mother sending her son to call his father in for dinner. Dick’s horror grows precisely because everything around it is so familiar.

In the garage, a homely place of tools and rubbish, Charles sees two identical fathers: his father is talking to a replica of himself. The thing that sits at the table looks exactly right, yet its eyes are cold and empty and its manner is subtly wrong. Its plan to punish Charles for leaving the table sounds like normal parental discipline, which makes it more chilling. His mother notices nothing, and this dramatic irony isolates the child.

The horror then becomes physical: in the trash barrel lies his real father’s dried, empty skin, thrown out like waste. Beneath the neat garden an insect-like creature is growing copies of Charles and his mother, so the whole family is to be replaced.

By setting nightmare inside the kitchen, the garage and the garden, Dick makes us ask how we know that the people closest to us are really themselves. What makes a father human is what lies inside, not his shell.

Model answer: 195 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Tier A 3; the text was omitted from the specimen’s Section B, which predicted the board’s long answer in 9 of 10 cases.

(b) How does Katherine Mansfield use the doll’s house and its little lamp to comment on class and on human connection in the short story, The Doll’s House?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates have to address the following areas in their answers:

  • The house as a model of society: handsome outside, smelly paint
  • Its perfect rooms and stiff dolls: respectability without life
  • Who may see it: Isabel’s privilege; the Kelveys excluded
  • Kezia and the lamp: what seems real to her
  • Aunt Beryl’s cruelty; the Kelveys shooed away
  • Else’s ‘I seen the little lamp’: shared inner light across class

Model answer

The doll’s house is a small model of the society in the story. From outside it is handsome, but its paint smells so strongly that it must stand in the courtyard, just as the town’s respectability hides an unpleasant snobbery. Inside, the rooms are perfect and the dolls stiff and lifeless.

The house also becomes a measure of privilege. Isabel boasts about it at school, and girls court her to be invited. The Kelveys, daughters of a washerwoman, are the only ones never allowed to see it, since the adults have taught every child that they must be shunned.

The lamp stands for something different. Kezia loves it most because it seems real, and it becomes a symbol of genuine feeling. She breaks the rule and invites the Kelveys in, until Aunt Beryl drives them out like chickens.

In the last scene Else, resting by the road, smiles and says softly that she saw the little lamp. That moment unites the richest and poorest children. Mansfield suggests that class is an adult construction, while the capacity to respond to beauty, the true inner light, belongs to everyone.

Model answer: 189 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Tier B 23; specimen 4(ii) described the house, so the board is likely to move to its meaning.

Question 520 marks
(i)5 marks

Referring closely to the poem, Ode to Tomatoes, trace the tomato’s journey from the summer streets into the kitchen. What mood does the poet create?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • The streets and the kitchen
  • The mood of the poem
Answer

Neruda begins with streets overflowing with tomatoes at midday in summer, when even the light seems halved like a tomato and its juice runs through the roads. Then, in December, the tomato ‘invades’ the kitchen. Like a conquering monarch it settles on the counters among glasses, butter dishes and saltcellars, shining with its own light and gentle majesty. The mood is joyful, playful and reverent. By treating a humble vegetable as a hero, the poet celebrates everyday abundance and the shared pleasure of food.

(ii)5 marks

Show how the world is indifferent to the refugees in the poem, Refugee Blues.

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • The consul, the committee and the public speaker
  • The freedom enjoyed by animals
Answer

Every official the refugees meet shuts them out. The consul thumps the table and says that without a passport they are officially dead. The committee politely asks them to come back next year, which is a refusal in disguise. A speaker at a public meeting warns that, if let in, they will steal the nation’s daily bread. Meanwhile a pampered poodle and a cat are admitted, fish swim freely in the harbour and birds sing without politicians. The contrast shows that the world grants animals more freedom and kindness than persecuted people.

(iii)10 marks

(a) Discuss the imagery and figures of speech in the poem, My Grandmother’s House, and show how they convey the speaker’s sense of loss.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to use the following approach to the question:

  • The house as a place of love; personified, withdrawing into silence
  • Snakes among books: decay, danger, a childhood cut short
  • Blood ‘cold like the moon’: grief as emotional death
  • Blind windows, frozen air: a dead house
  • Armful of darkness ‘like a brooding dog’: clinging to loss
  • Begging for love ‘in small change’: present poverty of love

Model answer

Kamala Das makes the house the centre of her imagery. It was once where she received love, and after her grandmother’s death it is personified as withdrawing into silence, as if it too were grieving.

Images of decay show what was lost. Snakes move among the books she was then too young to read, suggesting neglect, danger and a childhood cut off too soon. The simile of her blood turning cold like the moon turns grief into emotional death: warmth has gone out of her.

She longs to peer through the blind eyes of the windows and to listen to the frozen air. The house is a dead body that can neither see nor breathe, yet she still wants to reach it. Her wish to bring back an armful of darkness, to lie behind her bedroom door like a brooding dog, is startling: even the gloom of the past is a faithful companion compared with her present.

The final metaphor completes the contrast. The woman who was once proud and loved now begs at strangers’ doors for love, if only in small change. The imagery moves from warmth to cold and from plenty to beggary.

Model answer: 196 words (limit 150–200)

(b) Do you agree that the man in the poem, Urban, is imprisoned as much by his own mind as by the city? Give reasons for your answer.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to elaborate on the following ideas:

  • The city: broken roads, no sky, dry river, no breeze
  • Nature (the hills) always far away
  • Circles tracked within his head: habit, routine
  • He dreams of solitary walks but never goes
  • His mind’s traffic turns back to the noise close at hand
  • Personal judgement: the inner prison is the stronger

Model answer

The city certainly confines him. He knows only its broken roads; the hills are always far away; he sees no sky; his river is dry and no breeze reaches him. The city burns around him like a passion, crowding out nature and quiet.

Yet Ezekiel suggests that the deeper prison is inside. The man moves in circles tracked within his head: his routine is a mental habit, not a physical wall. His longings are real but idle. He dreams of morning walks alone and of floating free, yet he never takes those walks, although nothing actually prevents him.

Most tellingly, his mind turns its own traffic away from the natural world and back to the familiar noise close at hand. He is drawn to the noise that oppresses him. The city has become part of his thinking, so he could not escape even if the hills were near.

I therefore agree with the statement, and would go further: the mind is the stronger prison. The city explains how his alienation began, but his inertia keeps it in place. The poem warns that modern people often carry their confinement with them.

Model answer: 190 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Tier A 6 / 12: specimen 5(ii) on claustrophobia and longing, reframed as an evaluation.
