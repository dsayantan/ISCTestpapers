When Higgins suggests in Act IV of the play, Pygmalion, that Eliza might marry, why does she reject the idea? (Understand)

(iii)1 mark

Question 210 marks

Answer any five of the following subparts in 40-50 words.

(i)2 marks

In Act V of the play, Pygmalion, what does Higgins mean when he tells Eliza that he cannot turn her soul on? (Interpret)

Answer

Eliza mocks him that he can play her recorded voice when he feels lonely. Higgins replies that a recording is not enough: what he values is her living spirit, which no machine can reproduce. It is a rare admission that she matters to him as a person.

Spec 1(iv) (the gramophone line) answered from Higgins’s side.
(ii)2 marks

In Act V of the play, Pygmalion, why does Doolittle say that he was happier as one of the undeserving poor? (Understand)

Answer

As a dustman he lived from hand to mouth, owed nothing to anyone and enjoyed himself without worrying about respectability. Now that he is rich he must support relatives, pay doctors and lawyers and behave respectably. He was freer and happier when he was poor.

Forecast #126 (Tier E).
(iii)2 marks

In the short story, The Doll’s House, why does Kezia invite the Kelveys to see the doll’s house? (Understand)

Answer

She sees two girls left out when everyone else has been invited, and her natural kindness is stronger than the rule her mother has made. Kezia has not yet learnt the adults’ prejudice, so to her the Kelveys are simply children who would like to see the house.

(iv)2 marks

In the short story, The Dreamer, why does Adela Chemping nearly faint in the store? (Recall)

Answer

She sees Cyprian, mistaken for a shop assistant, calmly sell a handbag to a stranger and take her money. To Adela this is a shocking breach of respectability, and she dreads the scandal of her nephew being caught cheating a customer. The shock is too much.

Forecast #76 (Tier C).
(v)2 marks

In the poem, Refugee Blues, what does the speaker say about the country the refugees once had? (Understand)

Answer

They once had a country that they thought fair, and it can still be found in the atlas. But they cannot go there now. Their homeland exists on the map yet is closed to them, because those who rule it have turned against them.

(vi)2 marks

In the poem, My Grandmother’s House, why does the poet tell the person she calls darling that he cannot believe she once lived in such a house? (Understand)

Answer

She speaks to someone close to her in her present life. The woman he knows is lonely and starved of love, so it seems incredible that she was once proud and loved in her grandmother’s house. The remark measures how far she has fallen from that past.

SECTION B – 60 MARKS
PYGMALION: GEORGE BERNARD SHAW

Question 320 marks
(i)5 marks

How does Higgins behave at his mother’s ‘at home’ in Act III? What does his behaviour show about him?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • His manners with his mother and her guests
  • What it shows about him
Answer

Higgins arrives at his mother’s at-home uninvited, in his careless everyday manner, and she at once tells him to go home because he offends her friends. With the guests he is no better. He is abrupt with Mrs Eynsford Hill, wanders restlessly about the room, and lectures the company on how little any of them really knows. He treats Clara and Freddy with the same rough frankness. His behaviour shows a brilliant man with no social sense at all. The teacher making a lady of Eliza has no drawing-room manners himself, which is one of Shaw’s best ironies.

Model answer: 100 words (limit 80–100)

Act III at-home (spec 3(i)) seen through Higgins; Mrs Higgins’ exasperation with her son (forecast #119 area).
(ii)5 marks

What does Shaw’s sequel to the play tell us about Eliza’s life after the final scene?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • Her marriage and the flower shop
  • Her continuing relationship with Higgins
Answer

Shaw’s prose sequel settles what the play leaves open. Eliza marries Freddy, and Pickering helps them set up a flower shop. At first it struggles, because neither of them knows anything about business, and they have to take lessons in book-keeping before it prospers. Eliza does not marry Higgins. Shaw explains that he is a confirmed bachelor devoted to his mother, and that she instinctively knows he would never give her the warmth she wants. Yet she never quite leaves his world. She goes on meddling in his household and quarrelling with him, as independent as ever.

Model answer: 98 words (limit 80–100)

The spec key to 3(iii)(b) draws on the sequel; forecast #118 (romance and the sequel).
(iii)10 marks

(a) How does Shaw use and overturn the myth of Pygmalion in Acts III to V of the play?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are required to focus on the following areas in their answers:

  • The myth: a sculptor falls in love with his statue, which is brought to life
  • Higgins as sculptor: speech and manners shaped until she passes for a lady
  • Eliza treated as a creation: the men’s project; the ‘live doll’
  • Galatea comes alive on her own terms: slippers, ‘what is to become of me?’, leaving
  • Act V: she credits Pickering’s courtesy, not Higgins
  • The ending reverses the myth: no marriage; Freddy and independence; the sequel

Model answer

In the Greek myth the sculptor Pygmalion carves a statue so beautiful that he falls in love with it, and the goddess brings it to life. Shaw borrows the outline and then reverses almost every detail.

Higgins is the sculptor. He takes the raw material of a flower girl and shapes her speech and manners until, after the trial run of the at-home, she passes for a lady at the ambassador’s party. Like the statue, Eliza is treated as his creation: in Act III the men discuss her as their project, and Mrs Higgins calls her their live doll.

But this statue comes to life on her own terms. In Act IV, ignored after her triumph, she flings the slippers, demands to know what is to become of her and walks out. In Act V she tells Higgins that he did not make her a lady; Pickering’s courtesy did.

The greatest reversal is the ending. The myth ends in the union of creator and creation; Shaw refuses it. Higgins does not fall in love, Eliza chooses Freddy and independence. The play keeps the myth’s idea of transformation but turns a love story into a declaration of independence.

Model answer: 200 words (limit 150–200)

The title’s myth is set out in every edition’s introduction; forecast #118 (romance) and #39 (Eliza’s growth) from a new angle.
OR

(b) Were Higgins and Pickering right to carry out their experiment on Eliza? Give your opinion with reference to Acts III to V of the play.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to use the following approach to the question:

  • A clear judgement, argued from the text
  • Gains: poise, confidence, a living by teaching phonetics; self-respect from Pickering
  • Mrs Higgins’ warning in Act III waved away
  • Act IV: the men celebrate and forget her; ‘what is to become of me?’
  • Unfit for the gutter, no place above it: Higgins treats her as material
  • Her independence is her own achievement, not theirs

Model answer

The experiment does Eliza real good, but on balance I believe the men were wrong to begin it without a thought for her future.

On the credit side, Eliza gains a great deal. She wanted to speak well enough to work in a flower shop, and she ends the play able to hold her own anywhere, poised, confident and able to earn her living by teaching phonetics. Pickering’s courtesy gives her self-respect.

Yet the men never ask what the change will cost her. In Act III Mrs Higgins warns that they are giving her a fine lady’s manners without a fine lady’s income, and they wave the problem away. In Act IV, after her triumph, they celebrate their own success and forget her entirely, so that she is left asking what is to become of her. She cannot return to the gutter and has no place above it. Higgins treats her as material, not as a person with feelings.

Eliza finds her own way out, but that is to her credit, not theirs. The experiment was justified by its results only by chance; in its conduct it was careless and selfish.

Model answer: 194 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Pygmalion themes (the ethics of the experiment); Mrs Higgins’ question is the play’s moral centre.

(i)5 marks

In the short story, The Father-Thing, how is the father-thing different from Charles’s real father?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Interpret)

  • How it looks and behaves
  • What is missing in it
Answer

On the surface the father-thing is a perfect copy. It has Ted Walton’s face, body and clothes, and it sits in his place at dinner. Yet Charles feels at once that something is wrong. Its eyes are hard and dark, its voice is without emotion, and it watches him in a way that frightens him. What is missing is everything that made his father human: warmth, love and a self of its own. It is only a shell worked by the creature in the garden, and when the creature dies it collapses like a lifeless puppet.

(ii)5 marks

In the short story, Games at Twilight, how does Ravi see Raghu? Why does he want so badly to beat him?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Interpret)

  • Raghu’s nature
  • Why Ravi wants to beat him
Answer

To Ravi, Raghu is the enemy. He is older, bigger and stronger than the others, an athletic boy who enjoys his power and bullies the little ones. As ‘It’ he hunts through the garden with noisy energy, and Ravi, hiding in the shed, pictures him as a dangerous hunter. Raghu is contemptuous of smaller children and makes them feel weak. That is exactly why Ravi longs to beat him. For once the smallest boy could defeat the biggest and be champion, admired instead of ignored. Victory would prove his worth, which is why being forgotten hurts so much.

(iii)10 marks

(a) Is the narrator of the short story, Death by Scrabble, a reliable guide to what happens? Examine how the first-person point of view shapes the story.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates should build their answer on the following areas:

  • First person, present tense: everything filtered through the husband’s resentment
  • His hatred colours every detail of the wife; we never hear her side
  • Coincidences read as proof that the game controls reality
  • A heated, frustrated mind turning chance into pattern; the plan to kill with a word
  • No calmer voice to correct him; the twist turns his plan against him
  • Effect: darkly comic and chilling

Model answer

The story is told entirely by the husband, in the first person and the present tense, so everything we know is filtered through his resentment. He is a gripping but unreliable guide.

He tells us at once that he hates his wife, and every detail of her that follows is coloured by that hatred. Her concentration looks smug to him, her scoring looks like gloating, her small gestures designed to irritate. Yet nothing she actually does is cruel; we never hear her side and cannot check his judgement.

His reading of events is less reliable still. As they play, he notices that happenings around him seem to echo the words on the board, and he concludes that the game controls reality. A reader can see these as coincidences that a hot, frustrated mind has turned into a pattern. His belief grows into a plan to kill his wife with a word.

The present tense adds to the effect: we live each moment inside his head, with no calmer voice to correct him, and the twist that turns his plan against him shows how little he deserved trust. The point of view makes the story darkly funny and chilling.

Model answer: 198 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast #103 (Tier D); spec 4(iii)(a) on the marriage. Every judgement is the husband’s, which the key can reward.
OR

(b) Bring out the comic element in the short story, The Dreamer, and show how Saki satirises the craze for bargains.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates have to address the following areas in their answers:

  • Adela’s self-deceiving declaration: not a bargain hunter, only likes to be near bargains
  • The winter sales at Walpurgis and Nettlepink’s: useless purchases, crowd madness
  • The planted detail: the missing hat and Cyprian’s perfect logic
  • The handbag sale; Adela nearly faints
  • The final twist: devotional books sold to a Canon; the dreamy look deeper than ever
  • Irony and reversal: the dreamer beats the bargain hunters at their own game

Model answer

Saki’s comedy rests on reversal and irony, and his target is the fashionable passion for bargains.

The satire begins with Adela Chemping. She declares that she is not a bargain hunter but merely likes to go where bargains are, a distinction that is pure self-deception. At the winter sales at Walpurgis and Nettlepink’s she plunges into the crowd and buys things of no use to anyone, and the crush of shoppers is presented as a kind of madness.

The comedy grows from a planted detail. Adela is scandalised that her nephew has come without a hat, and Cyprian explains, with perfect logic, that a hat is a nuisance when one’s hands are full of parcels. Left alone, the hatless youth is taken for a shop assistant, and instead of correcting the mistake he calmly sells a lady a handbag at a special reduction and takes her money. Adela nearly faints.

The final twist doubles the joke. She finds him in the book department selling devotional books to an elderly Canon, his dreamy look deeper than ever. The quiet dreamer has beaten the bargain hunters at their own game.

Model answer: 191 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast #96 (Tier D); booklet 10(a). Spec 4(i) examined Cyprian’s dress, the detail on which the comedy turns.

Question 520 marks
(i)5 marks

In the poem, Urban, what do the distant hills symbolise?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Interpret)

  • No nearness to nature
  • No escape from the city’s chaos
Answer

The hills that are always far away stand first for nature. He lives among broken roads and traffic; he sees no sky, no breeze reaches him and his river is dry; the hills are the open, green world from which he is cut off. They also mean escape and peace. He dreams of solitary morning walks, but the hills remain at a distance he never crosses, because the city’s crowds hold him and his own mind moves in the same closed circles. The distant hills thus measure both his longing and his helplessness.

Forecast #110 (Tier D); booklet 5(ii). Spec 5(ii) examined Urban’s claustrophobia.
(ii)5 marks

In the poem, Ode to Tomatoes, how is the cut tomato presented as a gift?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • The tomato cut open
  • Nothing is wasted
Answer

When the knife enters the tomato, the poet sees not destruction but an offering. The halves open to reveal red insides, full of juice and seeds, as bright as a small sun, and they are laid on the table to be shared. The tomato gives itself generously to the salad and to everyone who eats. What makes it a true gift is that nothing is wasted: it has no husk to peel and no thorns to avoid. It offers its fiery colour and cool freshness completely, so that the humble vegetable becomes a model of generosity.

(iii)10 marks

(a) How does the poem, My Grandmother’s House, present memory as both a comfort and a source of pain?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to elaborate on the following ideas:

  • Memory as the only way back to lost love
  • Comfort: the house where she was proud and loved; the wish to return
  • The armful of darkness like a brooding dog: even gloom as companion
  • Pain: silence, snakes among books, blind windows, frozen air; blood cold as the moon
  • Memory sharpens the present: begging for love in small change
  • Judgement: a refuge she cannot live in

Model answer

For Kamala Das memory is the only way back to the love she has lost, and it both comforts and wounds her.

Memory comforts because it keeps alive a time when she was truly loved. The house far away was the place where she received love, where she was proud and secure. Even now she wants to return, to look through its windows and listen to its air, and to bring back an armful of its darkness to lie behind her bedroom door like a brooding dog. Even the gloom of that house would be a companion, guarding her like a faithful animal.

Yet the same memory brings pain. The house she remembers is now silent, decayed and cold: snakes move among the books, the windows are blind and the air is frozen. Her grandmother’s death turned her blood cold like the moon. Remembering also sharpens her sense of what she lacks now, when she must beg at strangers’ doors for love and receive it only in small change.

Memory therefore offers her no cure. It is a refuge she cannot live in, and each return to it measures how much she has lost.

Model answer: 195 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast #1 (Tier A: loss and nostalgia) as an Evaluate question. The specimen’s omitted poem, weight 0.9.

(b) What does the poem, The Black Santa, suggest about a child’s faith and imagination?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are required to focus on the following areas in their answers:

  • A child’s faith sees past poverty and imperfection
  • This Santa: thin, shaky, bony lap, breath smelling of drink
  • The child tells him the wishes in earnest; imagination supplies what he lacks
  • Wishes learnt from television commercials: advertising reaches a poor neighbourhood
  • Belonging: a Black Santa the child can claim as the community’s own
  • The poem’s recognition: the imperfect Santa is more real

Model answer

The poem shows that a child’s faith is generous enough to see past poverty and imperfection.

The Santa the child meets is nothing like the figure of cards and commercials. He is thin and shaky, with a bony lap and breath that smells of drink. An adult would see at once that he is a poor man doing a seasonal job. The child, however, sits on his lap and tells him the wishes in all seriousness. Imagination supplies what is missing, and belief turns a struggling man into Santa Claus.

This faith is also shaped by the child’s world. The wishes, learnt from television commercials, are for bright plastic things the family can probably not afford, which shows how advertising reaches even a poor neighbourhood.

What makes the faith moving is its sense of belonging. Because this Santa is Black, the child can claim him as one of their own, as the white Santa of television never could be. The poem seems to recognise that this imperfect Santa is more real than the perfect one. Childhood faith, the poem suggests, needs recognition, not perfection.

Model answer: 186 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast Black Santa themes (childhood faith, the stereotype against the real); spec 5(iii)(b) on overturning traditional ideas of Santa.
