Question 210 marks

Answer any five of the following subparts in 40-50 words.

(i)2 marks

In Act III of the play, Pygmalion, why does Higgins bring Eliza to his mother’s ‘at home’? (Recall)

Answer

He wants to test her in real society before the far greater trial of the ambassador’s party. His mother’s drawing room offers a small, safe company, and he is confident that her pronunciation is ready, so the at-home is a trial run.

Act III opening; spec 2(i) is the same visit from the other side (his reassurance).
(ii)2 marks

In Act V of the play, Pygmalion, what does Eliza mean when she says she wants a little kindness from Higgins? (Interpret)

Answer

She is not asking for romance. She wants to be treated as a person whose feelings count, noticed and valued for what she has done. Higgins offers only his genius and his indifference, while she wants ordinary warmth and respect.

Model answer: 40 words (limit 40–50)

Act V, the final confrontation (forecast #38/#39 area).
(iii)2 marks

In the short story, Death by Scrabble, what is ironic about the way the story ends? (Understand)

Answer

The narrator believes that the words on the board make things happen, and he plans to use one to kill his wife. Instead it is he who dies as the game ends, while the wife he hates lives on. His murderous scheme turns back on him: irony and poetic justice.

Model answer: 50 words (limit 40–50)

Forecast brief (irony, poetic justice, twist ending); booklet 10(a) on the ending, at short-answer level.
(iv)2 marks

In the short story, The Doll’s House, why do the girls at school suddenly become so friendly with Isabel? (Understand)

Answer

Everyone has heard about the Burnells’ wonderful doll’s house, and every girl longs to be asked to see it. Since Isabel decides who may come, the girls crowd round her and compete to be her special friend. Their sudden friendship is self-interest, not affection.

Model answer: 45 words (limit 40–50)

Forecast #130 (Tier E); booklet MCQ.
(v)2 marks

In the poem, Ode to Tomatoes, why do you think the poet writes in such short lines? (Interpret)

Answer

The very short lines, some a single word, fall quickly down the page like slices from a knife. They give the poem speed and simplicity, suiting an everyday subject, and make the reader pause on each small detail, as if every part of the tomato deserved attention.

Model answer: 47 words (limit 40–50)

Forecast #109 (Tier D; booklet 10(b) on form), at short-answer level.
(vi)2 marks

In the poem, The Black Santa, why do you think the Santa is shaky and thin? (Interpret)

Answer

He seems to be a poor, perhaps elderly man who has taken a seasonal job for a little money. His thinness and unsteadiness suggest hardship and poor health. The poet does not hide this: he is a real, struggling man, not a glossy figure from an advertisement.

Model answer: 47 words (limit 40–50)

Forecast #92 (Tier C); booklet short answer.

Question 320 marks
(i)5 marks

What do the Eynsford Hills reveal about themselves at Mrs Higgins’ ‘at home’ in Act III?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • Mrs Eynsford Hill
  • Clara
Answer

The Eynsford Hills are genteel but poor, and the at-home shows them struggling to keep up appearances. Mrs Eynsford Hill is refined, anxious and old-fashioned. She is shocked by Eliza’s language and by the new manners, yet she is too polite and too insecure to object openly. Clara is desperate to be modern. Taking Eliza’s strange talk for the latest fashion, she copies it eagerly and leaves using Eliza’s oath herself, to her mother’s distress. Together they are Shaw’s study of shabby gentility: upper-class manners without upper-class money.

Model answer: 93 words (limit 80–100)

Forecast #116 (Tier E). The at-home again (spec 3(i)), through the guests.
(ii)5 marks

How does the play end? What does the ending leave unresolved?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Interpret)

  • Higgins’s errands and Eliza’s reply
  • What the ending leaves open
Answer

As Eliza sets out for her father’s wedding, Higgins calls after her a list of errands: a ham, a Stilton cheese, a pair of gloves and a tie. Eliza answers him with disdain and sweeps out. Higgins is untroubled: he is sure she will do his shopping all the same, and he treats the idea of her marrying Freddy with scorn. Shaw leaves the ending open on purpose. Higgins still assumes she will serve him, while Eliza has just declared her independence. Her choice is not shown on stage; only the sequel settles it, and against Higgins.

Model answer: 98 words (limit 80–100)

Forecast #48 (Tier C) raised to a paragraph; the ending is always central (forecast Tier B note on #30).
(iii)10 marks

(a) Compare Higgins and Pickering in their treatment of Eliza in Acts III to V of the play.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to elaborate on the following ideas:

  • Pickering as Higgins’s foil: shared experiment, opposite attitude
  • Higgins treats her as material: discussed as a project; ignored after the party
  • ‘What is to become of me?’ answered with casual talk of marriage or a shop
  • His defence in Act V: the same manners for everyone, which still ignores her feelings
  • Pickering: Miss Doolittle, standing when she enters, never making her feel inferior
  • Yet he too forgets to thank her in Act IV
  • Eliza’s verdict: phonetics from Higgins, self-respect from Pickering

Model answer

Higgins and Pickering share the experiment but not the attitude, and Shaw uses each as the other’s foil.

Higgins treats Eliza as material. After the at-home he and Pickering discuss her as a project. After the party in Act IV he ignores her, calls for his slippers and thanks God the business is over. When she asks what is to become of her, he suggests marriage or a shop as casually as he would advise a stranger. In Act V he defends himself by saying he treats everyone alike, but his even-handed rudeness still ignores her feelings.

Pickering is a gentleman to her from first to last. He calls her Miss Doolittle, stands when she enters and never makes her feel inferior. Yet he is not blameless: in Act IV he too celebrates the triumph and forgets to thank her, which shows that kindness without attention can also wound.

In Act V Eliza sums up the difference. Higgins taught her to speak, but Pickering’s courtesy taught her self-respect, since a lady is made by how she is treated. The contrast carries the play’s argument about class and manners.

Model answer: 191 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast #36 (Tier B). The spec key calls Pickering Higgins’s foil; this uses that idea across all three acts.
OR

(b) Doolittle’s transformation in Act V runs parallel to Eliza’s. How does Shaw use it to deepen the play’s ideas about class and morality?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to use the following approach to the question:

  • Both raised out of their class by Higgins, almost by accident
  • Eliza through phonetics; Doolittle through a joking letter and a millionaire’s will
  • Both lose as much as they gain: no return to flower-selling; no return to the undeserving poor
  • Eliza’s change earned and inward: self-respect and independence
  • Doolittle’s change only money: wedding finery, relatives, duty to others
  • Satire of bought respectability; class as income as well as speech
  • Judgement: a new accent or fortune changes position; only self-respect changes the person

Model answer

Doolittle’s story mirrors his daughter’s, and the likeness sharpens Shaw’s point.

Both are raised out of their class by Higgins, almost by accident. Eliza is changed by phonetics; Doolittle by a joke in a letter to an American millionaire, whose will leaves him three thousand a year on condition that he lectures on morality. Both find that the change brings loss as well as gain. Eliza cannot go back to selling flowers, and Doolittle can no longer be one of the undeserving poor, living cheerfully from hand to mouth.

The difference is instructive. Eliza’s change is earned and inward: through hard work and Pickering’s courtesy she gains self-respect and independence. Doolittle’s is purely a matter of money. He arrives in wedding finery, besieged by relatives, obliged to marry and to live for others, and he blames middle-class morality for his misery.

Through him Shaw satirises a respectability that is bought rather than grown, and shows that class is a matter of income as much as of speech. Set beside Eliza, Doolittle proves that a new accent or fortune changes a person’s position, but only self-respect changes the person.

Model answer: 195 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast #34 (Tier B): the Doolittle subplot as a parallel transformation, a classic board focus the specimen leaves out.

Why is the short story called The Dreamer? Is the title apt?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Interpret)

  • What he actually does
Answer

The title seems to describe him perfectly, and then the story overturns it. Hatless, he is taken for a shop assistant and calmly sells a lady a handbag at a special reduction. (iii)10 marks

(a) Tony Peretti calls what has happened to Charles’s family “a bad business”. Is he right? Refer to three incidents in the short story, The Father-Thing.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to elaborate on the following ideas:

  • An understatement that the story bears out
  • The replacement: two fathers in the garage; the dried skin in the trash barrel
  • The spreading danger: the creature in its burrow; half-formed copies of Charles and his mother
  • No adult help: the mother accepts the thing; three boys must fight it
  • Three boys must find and burn the creature themselves
  • Peretti’s calm words make the horror practical and believable

Model answer

Peretti’s phrase is an understatement, and the story shows that he is right in at least three ways.

The first is the replacement itself. Charles sees his father in the garage talking to an exact replica of himself, and later finds his real father’s dried, hollow skin in the trash barrel. A loved parent has been used up and thrown away, and a copy sits at the dinner table in his place.

The second is that the danger is spreading. When the boys trace the source, they find a large insect-like creature in a burrow in the garden, and near it half-formed copies of Charles and his mother. The whole family is to be replaced, and Charles is next.

The third is that no adult will help. Charles’s mother accepts the thing as her husband, so the fight falls to three boys, who must find the creature and burn it themselves.

Peretti, older and tougher than Charles, sees what the adults do not: this is a real and deadly threat. His flat, calm words make the horror sound practical, and so all the more believable.

Model answer: 188 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast #15 (Tier A); booklet 10(b). The specimen’s omitted story, weight 0.9.

(b) How does Anita Desai use setting in the short story, Games at Twilight, to reflect the children’s feelings? Refer to the afternoon heat, the garden and the shed.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates have to address the following areas in their answers:

  • The heat: shuttered house, burning veranda, drooping plants, stilled creatures
  • The children’s restlessness and their burst into the open
  • The garden: arena of the game and Raghu’s territory
  • The shed: dark, forbidden, dusty; fear turning to excitement and dreams of victory
  • Twilight: fading light, the funeral game, fading hopes
  • Setting as a mirror of inner feeling throughout

Model answer

Desai’s setting is never mere background; it mirrors what the children feel.

The story opens in oppressive heat. The house is shuttered, the veranda burns, the plants droop and the birds and animals lie still in the glare. The children, shut indoors, are as restless as the heat is heavy, and when the door opens they burst out as though released from prison.

The garden becomes the arena of the game, its hedges, bushes and garage offering hiding places. It is also the territory of Raghu, the big boy who hunts the others, so for Ravi it is a place of danger and competition.

The shed is the most powerful setting. It is dark, forbidden and full of old junk, dust and spider webs; it smells of rats and ant hills. Ravi’s fear of it turns slowly into excitement as he realises that he has not been found, and he begins to dream of victory.

Finally twilight falls. The light fades as Ravi emerges to find himself forgotten, and the mournful funeral game completes the change of mood. The fading day matches his fading hopes, and the story ends in darkness outside him and within.

Model answer: 197 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast #99 (Tier D) raised to an essay; spec 4(iii)(b) (a child’s thoughts and fears) approached through setting.

In the poem, My Grandmother’s House, how does the poet contrast her past with her present life?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Interpret)

  • Her life in her grandmother’s house
  • Her present condition
Answer

In the past the poet lived in her grandmother’s house, where she received love freely and fully. There she was proud and loved, secure in the old woman’s affection. Her present is the opposite. Her grandmother is dead, the house has fallen silent, and the poet, now far away, tells the person she addresses that he can hardly believe she once lived like that. She has lost her way and must beg at strangers’ doors for love, receiving it only in small change. The contrast shows how poor in affection her adult life has become.

In what ways are the refugees in the poem, Refugee Blues, denied their basic rights?

Incorporate the following details and write your answer in one paragraph of 80 - 100 words. (Understand)

  • Material needs: home and shelter
  • Social and legal identity
Answer

The refugees are denied the most basic needs. In a city of ten million there is no room for them: others live in mansions or even holes, but they have nowhere. They are refused shelter, and at a public meeting they are accused of wanting to steal the city’s daily bread. They are also stripped of legal and social identity. Without a valid passport the consul declares them officially dead, and the committee puts them off until next year. Their homeland is closed to them. They have lost not only a home but their existence as citizens.

(iii)10 marks

(a) How does Pablo Neruda turn an ordinary vegetable into a subject worthy of an ode in the poem, Ode to Tomatoes?

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Analyse)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates should build their answer on the following areas:

  • The ode as a form of praise for the noble, given to a humble tomato
  • Size and power: overflowing streets; the halved light; the December invasion
  • Royal and military imagery: majesty, invasion
  • A story: murder leading to a wedding with onion, oil, salt and pepper
  • Cosmic grandeur and generosity: a star of the earth; no husk or thorns
  • Form: short tumbling lines, a playful tone
  • The tomato as a symbol of abundance, sharing and daily life

Model answer

An ode is traditionally a formal poem in praise of something noble. Neruda gives that honour to a tomato, and the surprise is the point: ordinary things deserve celebration.

He first gives the tomato size and power. In summer the streets overflow with it, and the midday light is halved like a tomato. In December it invades the kitchen and takes its place among the glasses and butter dishes with its own light and majesty. Royal and military imagery raises it above its humble station.

He then gives it a story. The tomato must be murdered, the knife sinking into its living pulp, and the murder leads to a wedding with onion, oil, salt and pepper. Violence, ceremony and celebration turn a salad into a small drama.

He also gives it grandeur and generosity. The tomato is a star of the earth, and it has no husk or thorns, giving itself entirely.

Finally, the form suits the subject: the very short lines tumble down the page like slices and keep the tone light.

By the end the tomato has become a symbol of abundance, sharing and daily life, and the poem honours everyone who cooks and eats it.

Model answer: 198 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast #109 (booklet 10(b): other celebratory techniques) and the ode’s central idea; spec 5(i) examined the invasion of the kitchen.

(b) Why does the poet use a Black Santa in the poem, The Black Santa, to speak about race and representation? Comment on the title.

Answer should be written in 150 - 200 words. (Evaluate)

Answer

Marking points. Candidates are expected to use the following approach to the question:

  • The Santa of television, stores and cards: fat, white, jolly, prosperous
  • What that image tells Black children about who owns Christmas
  • This Santa: thin, shaky, bony lap, breath smelling of drink; not idealised
  • The child’s trust claims him as the community’s own: inclusion
  • The wish-list from television: the media shape desire
  • The title: one added word overturns a stereotype

Model answer

Santa Claus as the child knows him from television, stores and cards is fat, white, jolly and prosperous. That image tells Black children, without saying so, that the magic of Christmas belongs to others. By choosing a Black Santa, Allison Joseph brings the question of race into the most innocent of childhood rituals.

The Santa of the poem is thin and shaky, with a bony lap and breath that smells of drink. He seems to be a poor man from the neighbourhood doing a seasonal job, and the poet does not idealise him. Yet the child trusts him and pours out a list of wishes, and in doing so claims him as the neighbourhood’s own. A Santa who looks like the people around the child makes the child feel included.

At the same time the wishes, learnt from television commercials, show how strongly the media shape desire.

The title is a quiet challenge. One added word overturns the stereotype and asks why the figure was ever assumed to be white. It asserts that representation matters, and that a flawed Santa who belongs to the community is more real than a perfect one who does not.

Model answer: 196 words (limit 150–200)

Forecast #113 (Tier D); booklet 10(a) and 10(b). Spec 5(iii)(b) asked how the poem overturns traditional ideas of Santa.
