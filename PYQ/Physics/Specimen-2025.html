SECTION A – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q1(A)(i)
Question 1(A)(i)[1]
Figure 1 shows a circuit containing an ammeter A, a galvanometer G and a plug key K. When the key is closed:
(a)both G and A show deflections.
(b)neither G nor A shows deflection.
(c)G shows deflection but A does not.
(d)A shows deflection but G does not.
  1. The four resistors form a Wheatstone bridge: 2 Ω : 4 Ω = 5 Ω : 10 Ω (= 1 : 2), so the bridge is balanced. The points Q and S are at the same potential and no current flows through G.
    [½]
  2. The cell still drives current through the bridge as a whole: Req = (7 × 14)/(7 + 14) = 4·67 Ω and I = 2/4·67 = 0·43 A, and all of it passes through the ammeter A, so A is deflected.
    [½]
Answer
(d) A shows deflection but G does not.

Concepts to recall

  • Wheatstone bridge: balanced when P/Q = R/S; the galvanometer in the diagonal then reads zero.
  • Test the ratio of the two resistors on each side of the galvanometer, not just their sum.
  • The ammeter is in the main circuit, so it always carries the cell’s current whatever the bridge does.
SECTION A – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q1(A)(ii)
Question 1(A)(ii)[1]
A circular coil having N turns of radius R carrying a current I is used to produce a magnetic field B at its centre O. If this coil is opened and rewound such that the radius of the newly formed coil is 2R, carrying the same current I, what will be the magnetic field at the centre O?
(a)2B
(b)B
(c)B/2
(d)B/4
  1. The length of wire is fixed: N(2πR) = N′(2π·2R), so the new number of turns is N′ = N/2.
    [½]
  2. B = μ0NI/2R, so B′ = μ0(N/2)I/(2 × 2R) = B/4.
    [½]
Answer
(d) B/4
Common slip Doubling R and forgetting that N halves gives B/2, option (c).

Concepts to recall

  • Field at the centre of a coil: B = μ0NI/2R (N turns, radius R).
  • Rewinding the same wire into a bigger loop reduces the number of turns: N ∝ 1/R for a fixed length.
SECTION A – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q1(A)(iii)
Question 1(A)(iii)[1]
Magnetic susceptibility of a diamagnetic substance:
(a)decreases with the increase in its temperature.
(b)is not affected by the change in its temperature.
(c)increases with increase in its temperature.
(d)first increases then decreases with increase in its temperature.
  1. Diamagnetism comes from the induced orbital magnetic moments in every atom, which do not depend on thermal agitation; so χ (small and negative) is independent of temperature.
    [1]
Answer
(b) is not affected by the change in its temperature.

Concepts to recall

  • Diamagnetic: χ small, negative, independent of T. Paramagnetic: χ = C/T (Curie law). Ferromagnetic: χ = C/(T − Tc) above the Curie point.
  • The temperature test is the quickest way to separate the three classes in an MCQ.
SECTION A – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q1(A)(iv)
Question 1(A)(iv)[1]
The de Broglie wavelength of an electron in the first Bohr’s orbit of hydrogen atom is equal to:
(a)diameter of the first orbit.
(b)circumference of the first orbit.
(c)radius of the second orbit.
(d)h/2π.
  1. Bohr’s quantum condition mvrn = nh/2π gives 2πrn = n(h/mv) = nλ.
    [½]
  2. For n = 1: λ = 2πr1, the circumference of the first orbit (one complete standing wave).
    [½]
Answer
(b) circumference of the first orbit.

Concepts to recall

  • de Broglie: λ = h/mv. Bohr’s orbit is allowed when the circumference holds a whole number of wavelengths: 2πr = nλ.
  • The quantisation of angular momentum L = nh/2π is the same statement.
SECTION A – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q1(A)(v)
Question 1(A)(v)[1]
An ideal PN junction diode offers:
(a)zero resistance in forward as well as reverse bias.
(b)infinite resistance in forward as well as reverse bias.
(c)zero resistance in forward, but infinite resistance in reverse bias.
(d)infinite resistance in forward, but zero resistance in reverse bias.
  1. An ideal diode is a perfect one-way valve: forward biased it conducts with no resistance (a closed switch), reverse biased it blocks completely (an open switch).
    [1]
Answer
(c) zero resistance in forward, but infinite resistance in reverse bias.

Concepts to recall

  • Ideal diode: Rf = 0, Rr = ∞. A real Si diode: knee at about 0·7 V, small forward resistance, reverse current of a few μA.
  • Use the ideal model in circuit questions (see Q20(iv)): reverse-biased diode = open circuit.
SECTION A – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q1(A)(vi)
Question 1(A)(vi)[1]
Assertion: An astronomical telescope has an objective lens having large focal length.
Reason: Magnifying power of an astronomical telescope varies directly with focal length of the objective lens.
(a)Both Assertion and Reason are true and Reason is the correct explanation for Assertion.
(b)Both Assertion and Reason are true but Reason is not the correct explanation for Assertion.
(c)Assertion is false and Reason is true.
(d)Both Assertion and Reason are false.
  1. Magnifying power (normal adjustment) m = fo/fe, so m ∝ fo: the Reason is true.
    [½]
  2. To get a large magnification the objective is therefore chosen with a large focal length (and a large aperture), so the Assertion is true and the Reason is exactly why: option (a).
    [½]
Answer
(a) Both Assertion and Reason are true and Reason is the correct explanation for Assertion.

Concepts to recall

  • Astronomical telescope: m = fo/fe, tube length L = fo + fe in normal adjustment.
  • Microscope is the reverse: both focal lengths are small, fo < fe.
  • Assertion–reason: decide each statement alone, then ask whether the Reason explains the Assertion.
SECTION A – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q1(A)(vii)
Question 1(A)(vii)[1]
Assertion: If critical angle of glass–air pair (μg = 3/2) is θ1 and that of water–air pair (μw = 4/3) is θ2, then the critical angle for the water–glass pair will lie between θ1 and θ2.
Reason: A medium is optically denser if its refractive index is greater.
(a)Both Assertion and Reason are true and Reason is the correct explanation for Assertion.
(b)Both Assertion and Reason are true but Reason is not the correct explanation for Assertion.
(c)Assertion is false and Reason is true.
(d)Both Assertion and Reason are false.
  1. sin θc = 1/μ for a medium against air: sin θ1 = 2/3, θ1 = 41·8°; sin θ2 = 3/4, θ2 = 48·6°.
    [½]
  2. For the water–glass pair (light going from glass to water): sin θc = μw/μg = (4/3)/(3/2) = 8/9, θc = 62·7°, which is greater than both, not between them: the Assertion is false. The Reason (denser = larger μ) is true.
    [½]
Answer
(c) Assertion is false and Reason is true.

Concepts to recall

  • Critical angle at the boundary from denser (1) to rarer (2): sin θc = μ2/μ1 = μrarer/μdenser.
  • Two media with nearly equal indices have a critical angle close to 90°; air against a medium gives a much smaller one.
  • Total internal reflection needs light going from the denser to the rarer medium and an angle of incidence above θc.

Question 1(B)(i)[1]
Current I flowing through a metallic wire is gradually increased. Show graphically how heating power (P) developed in it varies with the current (I).
  1. Heating power P = I2R, so P ∝ I2.
    [½]
  2. Graph: a parabola through the origin with I on the x-axis and P on the y-axis, both axes labelled.
    [½]
Answer
Parabola through the origin, P ∝ I2 (see the graph).
Marking note A wire warms up as I grows and its resistance rises a little, so the true curve bends up slightly more steeply; CISCE’s key accepts the parabola.

Concepts to recall

  • Joule heating: P = VI = I2R = V2/R.
  • Know the shape for each pair of variables: P–I parabola, P–V parabola, P–R a rectangular hyperbola at fixed V, a straight line at fixed I.
SECTION A – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q1(B)(ii)
Question 1(B)(ii)[1]
Explain why core of a transformer is always laminated.
  1. The changing flux induces eddy currents in the iron core. A core built of thin insulated sheets (laminations) breaks up the paths of these currents and raises the resistance of each loop, so the eddy currents are small and the loss of energy as heat (I2R) is reduced.
    [1]
Answer
To reduce the loss of power (heat) due to eddy currents.

Concepts to recall

  • Eddy currents: induced circulating currents in a bulk conductor in a changing flux (Lenz’s law: they oppose the change).
  • Laminate the core to cut eddy-current loss; use soft iron (narrow hysteresis loop) to cut hysteresis loss; thick copper wire cuts the I2R loss in the windings.
SECTION A – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q1(B)(iii)
Question 1(B)(iii)[1]
Why does a car driver use a convex mirror as a rear-view mirror?
  1. A convex mirror always forms an erect, virtual and diminished image of objects behind the car, so a much larger region (a wider field of view) is seen in a small mirror.
    [1]
Answer
It gives an erect, diminished image and hence a wide field of view.

Concepts to recall

  • Convex mirror: image always virtual, erect, smaller than the object and between P and F, for every object position; f is positive in the sign convention used for mirrors by ISC.
  • Wide field of view; the speed of vehicles looks smaller than it is, so one judges distance with care.

Question 1(B)(iv)[1]
Represent diagrammatically how the incident planar wavefronts of wavelength λ pass through an aperture of size d, when d is approximately equal to λ.
  1. Plane wavefronts approach the aperture and, because d ≈ λ, emerge as (nearly) circular/spherical diverging wavefronts that spread into the region behind the barrier (diffraction). Wavefronts labelled, direction of propagation shown.
    [1]
Answer
Diverging (circular) wavefronts beyond the aperture, as drawn.
Marking note CISCE’s key asks for a “diverging spherical wavefront”; the incident plane wavefronts should be shown as well.

Concepts to recall

  • Diffraction is marked when the aperture or obstacle is of the order of the wavelength (d ≈ λ); for d ≫ λ the wave goes nearly straight on.

Question 1(B)(v)[1]
What is meant by “Dual nature of matter”?
  1. Matter (e.g. an electron, a proton, a neutron) exhibits both particle and wave behaviour. A moving particle of momentum p is associated with a matter wave of wavelength λ = h/p (de Broglie).
    [1]
Answer
Matter shows both particle nature and wave nature; λ = h/p.

Concepts to recall

  • de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p = h/mv; for a charge accelerated through V: λ = h/√(2mqV).

SECTION A – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q1(B)(vi)
Question 1(B)(vi)[1]
What happens when an electron collides with a positron?
  1. The electron and the positron (its antiparticle) annihilate each other and their mass is converted into energy: two gamma-ray photons are produced, each of energy mec2 = 0·51 MeV, moving in opposite directions.
    [1]
Answer
They annihilate each other, producing gamma-ray photons (two, each of about 0·51 MeV).
Marking note Full mark for “annihilate and produce gamma-ray photons”, as the key says. The 0·51 MeV figure is an extra.

Concepts to recall

  • Pair annihilation e− + e+ → 2γ; the reverse, pair production, needs a photon of at least 2mec2 = 1·02 MeV.
  • Two photons are needed so that momentum is conserved. E = mc2; 1 u = 931·5 MeV.

Question 1(B)(vii)[1]
What energy conversion takes place in a solar cell?
  1. A solar cell (a p–n junction in which photons create electron–hole pairs that the junction field separates) converts light (solar) energy into electrical energy.
    [1]
Answer
Light (solar) energy → electrical energy.

Concepts to recall

  • Solar cell works in the photovoltaic mode, no external bias: photons with hν ≥ Eg generate electron–hole pairs; the junction field drives them to opposite sides, giving an emf.

SECTION B – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q2
Question 2[2]
(i) A dielectric slab of dielectric constant K and thickness t is introduced between the two plates of a capacitor of plate-separation d (> t) and common area A. The capacitance of this system is given as C = ε0A / [(d − t) + t/K]. How does the capacitance C modify in each of the following cases?
(a) The dielectric slab covers half the distance of separation between the two plates.
(b) The whole space between the plates is filled with the dielectric.
OR
(ii) In Figure 2, the current–voltage graphs for a conductor are given at two different temperatures T1 and T2.
(a) At which temperature T1 or T2 is the resistance higher?
(b) Which temperature (T1 or T2) is higher?

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a) Put t = d/2 in the given formula: C = ε0A / [(d − d/2) + d/2K]
    [½]
  2. = ε0A / [d(1/2 + 1/2K)] = 2Kε0A / [d(K + 1)]
    [½]
  3. (b) The whole space is filled: t = d, so C = ε0A / (d/K) = Kε0A/d, i.e. the capacitance becomes K times that of the empty capacitor.
    [1]
Answer
(a) C = 2Kε0A/[d(K + 1)]; (b) C = Kε0A/d.
Common slip Writing the (a) answer as KC0/2: that is wrong, because the half of the gap that stays air is not scaled by K.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (a) The slope of an I–V graph is I/V = 1/R = tan θ. The line of T1 makes the larger angle (θ1 > θ2), so tan θ1 > tan θ2 and R1 < R2.
    [1]
  2. The resistance is higher at T2.
  3. (b) The resistance of a metallic conductor increases with temperature. R2 > R1, so T2 > T1.
    [1]
Answer
(a) the resistance is higher at T2; (b) T2 is the higher temperature.
Common slip Reading the steeper line as the higher resistance (it is the lower one).

Concepts to recall

  • Empty parallel-plate capacitor C0 = ε0A/d; with a full dielectric C = KC0.
  • Slab of thickness t: the air gap d − t and the slab act like two capacitors in series, giving the effective separation d − t + t/K.
  • Check limits: K = 1 gives C0 in both cases; t = 0 gives C0.
  • I–V graph: slope = 1/R (conductance), not R. A steeper line means a smaller resistance.
  • For a metal, R = R0(1 + αΔT) with α > 0: higher temperature, higher resistance (more frequent collisions, shorter relaxation time τ).
SECTION B – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q3
Question 3[2]
Arrangement of an oxygen ion and two hydrogen ions in a water molecule is shown in Figure 3. Calculate the electric dipole moment of the water molecule. Express your answer in terms of e (charge on hydrogen ion), l and θ.
  1. Each O–H pair is a dipole of moment p1 = p2 = e × l, directed from the negative O ion towards the positive H ion.
    [½]
  2. The two dipole moments are inclined at an angle θ, so each makes an angle θ/2 with the bisector. Their resultant: p = 2p1 cos(θ/2) = 2el cos(θ/2).
    [1]
  3. Direction: along the bisector of angle θ, from the oxygen ion towards the midpoint of the two hydrogen ions.
    [½]
Answer
p = 2el cos(θ/2), along the bisector of θ.

Concepts to recall

  • Dipole moment p = q × (2a), a vector from −q to +q; dipole moments add as vectors.
  • Resultant of two equal vectors at angle θ: 2p cos(θ/2). Checks: θ = 0 gives 2p, θ = 180° gives 0.
  • Water’s bent shape (HOH about 104·5°) is why it is a polar molecule; a linear molecule (θ = 180°) would have no net moment.
SECTION B – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q4
Question 4[2]
(i) Figure 4 shows a part of an electric circuit.
(a) Apply Kirchhoff’s junction rule to the junction A to find current flowing through the 5 Ω resistor.
(b) Apply Kirchhoff’s loop rule to the loop ABDA to find current flowing through the 7 Ω resistor.
OR
(ii) In a potentiometer, a cell is balanced against 110 cm when the circuit is open. A cell is balanced at 100 cm when short-circuited through a resistance of 10 Ω. Find the internal resistance of the cell.

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a) Junction rule at A: current in = current out: I = I1 + I2, so 5 = 2 + I2 and I2 = 3 A.
    [1]
  2. (b) Loop rule round A → B → D → A (the 4 Ω and 7 Ω resistors are traversed along their currents, the 5 Ω one against its current): 4I1 + 7I3 − 5I2 = 0, i.e. 4(2) + 7I3 = 5(3)
    [½]
  3. 7I3 = 15 − 8 = 7, so I3 = 1 A (from B to D).
    [½]
Answer
(a) I2 = 3 A; (b) I3 = 1 A.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. Open circuit: the potential difference across the cell is its emf E, balanced at l1 = 110 cm, so E = kl1 (k = potential gradient).
    [½]
  2. With R across the cell: terminal voltage V = ER/(R + r) = kl2, balanced at l2 = 100 cm.
    [½]
  3. Dividing: E/V = (R + r)/R = l1/l2, so r = R(l1/l2 − 1)
    [½]
  4. = 10(110/100 − 1) = 1 Ω
    [½]
Answer
r = 1 Ω
Common slip Using l2/l1 instead of l1/l2 (it would give a negative r).

Concepts to recall

  • Junction rule (conservation of charge): ΣIin = ΣIout.
  • Loop rule (conservation of energy): ΣIR round a closed loop = Σemf (here no cells in the loop, so 0). Traversing along the current is a potential drop IR; against it a rise.
  • Pick the direction first and keep it: the answer’s sign then tells you the true direction.
  • Potentiometer: potential difference across a length l of wire = kl (k = gradient, V m−1); a null reading draws no current from the cell.
  • Internal resistance by the potentiometer: r = R(l1 − l2)/l2. Open circuit gives E; closed gives the terminal voltage V = E − Ir.
SECTION B – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q5
Question 5[2]
Figure 5 shows two long, parallel wires carrying current in the same direction such that I1 < I2.
(i) In which direction will wire I1 move?
(ii) If the direction of the current I2 is reversed, in which direction will the wire I1 move now?
  1. (i) Parallel conductors carrying currents in the same direction attract. Wire I (carrying I1) is pulled towards wire II (carrying I2).
    [1]
  2. (ii) With I2 reversed the currents are opposite, so the wires repel: wire I moves away from wire II.
    [1]
Answer
(i) wire I moves towards wire II (attraction); (ii) wire I moves away from wire II (repulsion).
Marking note CISCE’s key says wire I “will move towards I1” and “away from I1”. That is a slip: it is wire I1 that moves, towards (or away from) the other wire carrying I2. Answers that name the direction relative to the other wire earn the marks.

Concepts to recall

  • Force per unit length between parallel wires: F/l = μ0I1I2/2πd: attractive for parallel currents, repulsive for antiparallel (this defines the ampere).
  • The two forces are equal and opposite (Newton’s third law) even though I1 ≠ I2; the condition I1 < I2 only decides who has the stronger field.

Question 6[2]
(i) The focal length of a double convex lens is equal to the radius of curvature of either surface. What is the refractive index of its material?
(ii) What is meant by a thin prism?
  1. (i) Lens-maker’s formula: 1/f = (n − 1)(1/R1 − 1/R2) with R1 = +R, R2 = −R and R = f:
    [½]
  2. 1/f = (n − 1)(1/f + 1/f) = 2(n − 1)/f, so n − 1 = 1/2 and n = 1·5.
    [½]
  3. (ii) A thin prism is a prism whose refracting angle is small (less than about 4°, i.e. a few degrees), so that the deviation δ = (n − 1)A for rays of small incidence.
    [1]
Answer
(i) n = 1·5; (ii) a prism of very small refracting angle (A < 4°).
Common slip Using 1/R1 + 1/R2 without the sign convention is fine for magnitudes, but it must still give n − 1 = 1/2, not 2.

Concepts to recall

  • Lens-maker’s formula: 1/f = (n − 1)(1/R1 − 1/R2); for a symmetric double convex lens f = R/2(n − 1); f = R exactly when n = 1·5.
  • Thin prism: angle of minimum deviation δm ≈ (n − 1)A for small A.
SECTION B – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q7
Question 7[2]
(i) Name the electromagnetic radiation that has been used in obtaining the image of a chest (a chest radiograph is shown in the paper).
(ii) What is the wavelength range of electromagnetic radiation used in radio broadcast?
  1. (i) X-rays: they pass through soft tissue but are absorbed by bone, so a radiograph (shadow image of the ribs and spine) is obtained.
    [1]
  2. (ii) Radio waves, with wavelength greater than 1 m (CISCE’s key): FM broadcast is about 3 m, AM broadcast about 200–600 m.
    [1]
Answer
(i) X-rays; (ii) greater than 1 m.
Marking note The wavelength of radio waves is quoted differently in books (0·1 m upwards in NCERT, > 1 m in CISCE’s key); either is credited for broadcast bands, which lie from about 3 m (FM) to several hundred metres (AM).

  • X-rays (0·01–10 nm): medical imaging, crystallography, airport scanners. Radio waves: communication; microwaves: radar and ovens.
SECTION B – 14 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q8
Question 8[2]
How does stopping potential in photoelectric emission vary if
(i) the intensity of the incident radiation increases?
(ii) the frequency of incident radiation decreases?
  1. (i) Einstein: eVs = hν − φ = Kmax. Intensity only changes the number of photons (hence the photocurrent), not the energy of each photon, so the stopping potential is unchanged (independent of intensity).
    [1]
  2. (ii) Vs = (h/e)(ν − ν0): it decreases linearly as ν decreases, and becomes zero at the threshold frequency ν0 (below ν0 there is no emission).
    [1]
Answer
(i) no change; (ii) Vs decreases (linearly with ν).
Marking note CISCE’s key says only that Vs “will change with the frequency”. The direction (it decreases) is what the question asks and is what is marked here.

Concepts to recall

  • Einstein’s equation: hf = φ + Kmax, Kmax = eVs; φ = hν0.
  • Intensity affects the saturation current, not Vs; frequency affects Vs, not the saturation current.
  • Vs–ν graph: a straight line, slope h/e, intercept on the ν axis = ν0.
SECTION C – 27 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q9
Question 9[3]
“A uniformly charged conducting spherical shell for the points outside the shell behaves as if the entire charge of the shell is concentrated at its centre.” Show this with the help of a proper diagram and verify this statement.
  1. Diagram: shell of radius R with surface charge density σ (total charge q = σ × 4πR2); a concentric Gaussian sphere of radius r > R through the point P; centre O marked.
    [1]
  2. By spherical symmetry E at every point of the Gaussian surface is radial, i.e. parallel to dS, and has the same magnitude. The flux through the surface: Φ = ∮E·dS = E × 4πr2.
    [1]
  3. Gauss’s law: E · 4πr2 = q/ε0 = σ · 4πR2/ε0, so E = σR2/(ε0r2) = q/(4πε0r2). This is exactly the field of a point charge q placed at O (Coulomb’s law): the statement is verified.
    [1]
Answer
E = q/(4πε0r2) = σR2/(ε0r2) for r ≥ R, as for a point charge q at the centre.
Marking note CISCE’s key writes the flux as E × 4πR2 (with R) in one line and 4πr2 in the next; the flux through the Gaussian sphere must use r, as written here. Diagram, symmetry argument and Gauss’s law are what the three marks follow.

Concepts to recall

  • Gauss’s law: ∮E·dS = qenc/ε0. Choose a Gaussian surface on which E is constant and perpendicular to the surface.
  • Shell results: outside (r ≥ R) E = q/4πε0r2; just outside E = σ/ε0; inside E = 0; potential inside = constant = q/4πε0R.
  • The surface area of a sphere is 4πr2, with the radius of the Gaussian sphere, and the enclosed charge uses the shell’s own area 4πR2.
SECTION C – 27 MARKSISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q10
Question 10[3]
(i) Study the two circuits shown in Figure 6. The cells in the two circuits are identical to each other. The resistance of the load resistor R is the same in both circuits. Question 20[5]
Sanya performed an experiment of obtaining characteristic curves of a junction diode. When she forward biased it, she found that beyond forward voltage V = Vk, the conductivity is very high. When she reverse biased the diode she found that a very small current (of about a few microamperes) flows in the diode. It remained constant even though she varied the voltage.
(i) In Figure 11, which one of the diodes is forward biased?
(ii) What is meant by a saturation current?
(iii) When applied voltage during forward bias is small, why does no current flow in the diode?
(iv) The circuit shown in the Figure 12 contains two diodes, each with a forward resistance of 50 Ω and with infinite resistance during reverse bias. If the battery voltage is 6 V, then calculate the current through the 100 Ω resistance.
  1. (i) A diode is forward biased when its anode (the triangle’s flat side) is at the higher potential. The −5 V end is higher than the −10 V end. Diode D2 has its anode towards the −5 V side, so D2 is forward biased; D1 has its anode towards the −10 V side and is reverse biased.
    [1]
  2. (ii) The saturation current is the small (a few μA) current in reverse bias, due to minority carriers, which stays constant (independent of the applied reverse voltage) until breakdown.
    [1]
  3. (iii) The potential barrier (barrier potential, about 0·7 V for Si) at the junction opposes the flow of majority carriers; until the applied forward voltage exceeds it, carriers cannot cross the depletion region and no appreciable current flows.
    [1]
  4. (iv) The cell’s + plate (long plate, left) feeds the diode in the upper branch (anode on the left): it is forward biased and conducts, with resistance 50 Ω. The diode in the middle branch is reverse biased: infinite resistance, no current (its 50 Ω resistor carries nothing).
    [1]
  5. The loop contains the cell, the upper diode (50 Ω), the 150 Ω resistor and the 100 Ω resistor in series: R = 50 + 150 + 100 = 300 Ω, so I = 6/300 = 0·02 A, the current through the 100 Ω resistor.
    [1]
Answer
(i) D2; (ii) the constant reverse current of a few μA; (iii) the potential barrier; (iv) 0·02 A (20 mA).
Marking note CISCE’s key: D2; “current that remains constant even on increasing the p.d.”; “due to potential barrier”; and 6/(50 + 150 + 100) = 0·02 A. All agree. The 50 in the sum is the diode’s forward resistance, not the middle 50 Ω resistor, which sits in the blocked branch. Strictly, in Figure 11 the series diode D1 is reverse biased, so no current flows in that circuit at all; the question asks only about the polarity of each diode.

Concepts to recall

  • Forward bias: p side (anode) at the higher potential; current flows only beyond the knee Vk (about 0·7 V Si, 0·3 V Ge). Reverse bias: small minority-carrier current, the saturation (leakage) current, until breakdown.
  • Circuit problems with ideal diodes: first decide which diode is forward biased, replace it by its forward resistance (here 50 Ω) and the reverse one by an open circuit; then use I = V/Rtotal.
  • The knee voltage is the barrier potential: the applied voltage must first cancel it.
