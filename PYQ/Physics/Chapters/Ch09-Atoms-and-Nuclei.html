When can a gamma ray photon produce a proton and an antiproton pair?

    [1]
Answer
When the photon energy is at least 2mpc2 = 1876·6 MeV.

What happens when an electron collides with a positron?
  1. The electron and positron annihilate each other; their mass is converted into energy emitted as two gamma-ray photons (each 0·51 MeV when they are at rest).
    [1]
Answer
They annihilate, producing gamma-ray photons.

Concepts to recall

The de Broglie wavelength of an electron in the first Bohr’s orbit of hydrogen atom is equal to:
(a)diameter of the first orbit.
(b)circumference of the first orbit.
(c)radius of the second orbit.
(d)h/2π.

What happens when an electron collides with a positron?
  1. The electron and the positron (its antiparticle) annihilate each other and their mass is converted into energy: two gamma-ray photons are produced, each of energy mec2 = 0·51 MeV, moving in opposite directions.

They annihilate each other, producing gamma-ray photons (two, each of about 0·51 MeV).

What is ‘Pair production’?
  1. The conversion of a high-energy gamma-ray photon into an electron and a positron (a particle–antiparticle pair), γ → e− + e+.
    [½]
  2. It takes place in the strong electric field close to a nucleus (which takes up the momentum) and needs photon energy of at least 2mec2 = 1·02 MeV.
    [½]
Answer
A gamma-ray photon of energy ≥ 1·02 MeV, near a nucleus, changes into an electron–positron pair.

TWO-MARK QUESTIONS (1)ISC 2024 Physics Paper 1 (861A), Q7
Question 7 · Board 2024[2]
(i) Figure 1 above is the energy level diagram for hydrogen atom. Study the transitions shown and answer the following questions.
(a) State the type of spectrum obtained.
(b) Name the series of spectrum obtained.
OR
(ii) In a nuclear reactor, state the use of the following:
(a) Graphite rods
(b) Cadmium rods

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a) The arrows point downwards: the electron falls from a higher level (n = 3 and n = 4) to a lower one and the atom emits photons of definite energies, 1·89 eV and 2·55 eV, so the spectrum is a line emission spectrum.
    [1]
  2. (b) Both transitions end on the second level (n = 2): 3 → 2 and 4 → 2. Transitions to n = 2 form the Balmer series (in the visible region).
    [1]
Answer
(a) Emission (line) spectrum. (b) Balmer series.
Marking note The printed level energies agree with −13·6/n2 (−3·4, −1·51, −0·85 eV).

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (a) Graphite rods act as the moderator: they slow down the fast neutrons produced in fission to thermal energies (about 0·025 eV) by elastic collisions, without absorbing them, so that they can cause further fission of 235U.
    [1]
  2. (b) Cadmium rods are the control rods: cadmium absorbs neutrons strongly, so pushing the rods in or out controls the number of neutrons and hence the rate of the chain reaction (they can also shut the reactor down).
    [1]
Answer
(a) Graphite: moderator (slows down fast neutrons). (b) Cadmium: control rods (absorb neutrons to control the chain reaction).

Concepts to recall

  • Emission: electron falls to a lower level, hν = Ei − Ef. Absorption: upward transitions.
  • Series by final level: Lyman n = 1 (UV), Balmer n = 2 (visible), Paschen n = 3 (IR), Brackett 4, Pfund 5. 1/λ = R(1/nf2 − 1/ni2).
  • Energy of level: En = −13·6/n2 eV.
  • Reactor parts: fuel (enriched U), moderator (graphite, heavy water), control rods (Cd, B), coolant, shielding.
  • Multiplication factor k: k = 1 steady (controlled), k > 1 growing (bomb-like), k < 1 dies out.
  • Thermal neutrons have much larger fission cross-section for 235U than fast ones.
FIVE-MARK QUESTIONS (7)ISC Specimen 2027 Physics Paper 1, Q19
Question 19 · Specimen 2027[5]
(i) (a) Write Bohr’s equations for circular motion of an electron around the nucleus and conservation of angular momentum. Using them, obtain an expression for orbital velocity of an electron.
(b) Prove that 1 u = 931·5 MeV where the terms have their usual meaning.
OR
(ii) (a) Assuming that nuclei are spherical in shape, whose radius R is given by R = R0A1/3 where R0 is a constant, show that the nuclear density (i.e. density of nuclear matter) is independent of mass number (A) of the nucleus / atom.
(b) What are isotones?
(c) Name the series of lines in hydrogen spectrum which lies in (1) Visible region (2) UV region

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a) Coulomb force provides the centripetal force (nucleus of charge +Ze): mv2/r = (1/4πε0)(Ze)(e)/r2, i.e. mv2r = Ze2/4πε0. (1)
    [1]
  2. Bohr’s quantisation of angular momentum: mvr = nh/2π, so r = nh/(2πmv). (2)
    [1]
  3. Substitute r from (2) in (1): mv2 · nh/(2πmv) = Ze2/4πε0, i.e. vnh/2π = Ze2/4πε0, so v = Ze2/(2ε0nh).
    [1]
  4. (b) Einstein’s relation E = mc2 for m = 1 u = 1·66 × 10−27 kg (the energy equivalent of one atomic mass unit), c = 3 × 108 m s−1: E = 1·66 × 10−27 × (3 × 108)2 = 1·494 × 10−10 J.
    [1]
  5. Convert with 1 eV = 1·6 × 10−19 J: E = 9·338 × 108 eV ≈ 933·8 MeV; with the more exact values (1 u = 1·6605 × 10−27 kg, c = 2·998 × 108 m s−1, 1 eV = 1·602 × 10−19 J) this is 931·5 MeV.
    [1]
Answer
(a) v = Ze2/(2ε0nh); (b) 1 u ≈ 931·5 MeV.
Marking note Slip in the printed key: it writes 1·66 × 9 = 14·929 (it is 14·94) and then states 931·5 MeV. With the constants of the paper’s table (1·66 × 10−27 kg, 3 × 108 m s−1, 1·6 × 10−19 J) the result is 933·8 MeV; 931·5 MeV needs the more precise constants (931·49 MeV). Full marks for either with the substitution shown.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (a) Let m be the average mass of a nucleon; the mass of the nucleus is mA and its volume is (4/3)πR3.
    [1]
  2. Density ρ = mA/[(4/3)πR03A] = 3m/(4πR03), in which A has cancelled: the density is independent of A (about 2·3 × 1017 kg m−3).
    [1]
  3. (b) Isotones are nuclei (atoms) with the same number of neutrons (N) but different numbers of protons (Z) (and so different mass numbers).
    [1]
  4. (c)(1) Visible region: Balmer series.
    [1]
  5. (c)(2) Ultraviolet region: Lyman series.
    [1]
Answer
(a) ρ = 3m/(4πR03), independent of A; (b) same N, different Z; (c) (1) Balmer, (2) Lyman.

Concepts to recall

  • Bohr postulates: stationary orbits, mvr = nh/2π, hν = Ei − Ef on a jump.
  • Consequences: vn = Ze2/2ε0nh ∝ 1/n (2·19 × 106 m s−1 for n = 1, hydrogen); rn ∝ n2; En = −13·6Z2/n2 eV.
  • Mass–energy equivalence: 1 u = 931·5 MeV/c2; useful in binding-energy and Q-value problems.
  • Nuclear radius R = R0A1/3, R0 ≈ 1·2 fm; volume ∝ A, so density is the same for all nuclei.
  • Isotopes: same Z; isobars: same A; isotones: same N = A − Z (e.g. 14C and 15N).
  • Hydrogen series: Lyman (to n = 1, UV), Balmer (to 2, visible), Paschen (to 3), Brackett, Pfund (infrared).
FIVE-MARK QUESTIONS (7)ISC 2026 Physics Paper 1 (861A), Q19
Question 19 · Board 2026[5]
Answer either subparts (i) to (iii) or (iv) to (vii).
(i) Draw a labelled graph showing the variation of binding energy per nucleon with the mass number (A) of the nucleus. On it, mark the nucleus that is most stable.
(ii) What is meant by the following statement? ‘Angular momentum of an orbiting electron is quantised.’
(iii) Calculate the shortest wavelength of the Balmer series.
OR
(iv) Calculate the minimum amount of energy which a gamma ray photon should possess in order to produce a proton and an anti-proton pair.
(v) Why is a nuclear fusion reaction also known as a thermonuclear reaction?
(vi) What is the physical significance of the fact that the total energy of an orbiting electron is negative?
(vii) State any one limitation of Bohr’s theory of the hydrogen atom.

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (i) Graph of binding energy per nucleon (MeV, y-axis) against mass number A (x-axis): rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks at about 8·8 MeV near A = 56 (56Fe), then falls slowly to about 7·6 MeV at A = 238. Axes labelled [1]; peak (56Fe, most stable) marked [1].
    [2]
  2. (ii) The orbital angular momentum of the electron can take only the discrete values L = nh/2π (n = 1, 2, 3, …), i.e. integral multiples of h/2π; it cannot have any other value.
    [1]
  3. (iii) The shortest wavelength is the series limit n = ∞ → 2: 1/λ = RH(1/22 − 1/∞2) = RH/4
    [1]
  4. λ = 4/RH = 4/(1·097 × 107) = 3·65 × 10-7 m (about 364·6 nm)
    [1]
Answer
(iii) λmin = 3·65 × 10-7 m ≈ 365 nm.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (iv) Pair production needs at least the rest energy of the pair: Emin = 2mpc2 = 2 × 1·67 × 10−27 × (3 × 108)2 = 3·01 × 10-10 J
    [1]
  2. = 3·01 × 10-10/(1·6 × 10−19) = 1·88 × 109 eV ≈ 1·88 GeV (about 1880 MeV).
    [1]
  3. (v) Fusion needs the nuclei to overcome the Coulomb repulsion, which requires extremely high temperatures (about 107 to 109 K), so it is brought about by heat: thermonuclear.
    [1]
  4. (vi) The negative total energy means the electron is bound to the nucleus: energy must be supplied (the ionisation energy) to free it.
    [1]
  5. (vii) Any one: it applies only to hydrogen-like (single-electron) atoms; it cannot explain fine structure/relative intensities of spectral lines, the Zeeman effect, or the splitting of lines; it ignores the wave nature of the electron.
    [1]
Answer
(iv) Emin = 3·01 × 10-10 J ≈ 1·88 GeV; (v) very high temperature needed; (vi) electron is bound; (vii) fails for multi-electron atoms.

Concepts to recall

  • Binding energy per nucleon: low for light nuclei, maximum (~8·8 MeV) near 56Fe, slowly decreasing for heavy nuclei: fusion of light and fission of heavy nuclei both release energy.
  • Bohr’s postulate: mvr = nh/2π.
  • Balmer series (to n = 2): 1/λ = RH(1/4 − 1/n2), n = 3, 4, …; longest λ at n = 3 (656 nm), shortest at n = ∞ (365 nm).
  • Pair production: γ → particle + antiparticle, threshold E = 2mc2 (for e−e+ it is 1·02 MeV).
  • Energy levels of hydrogen: En = −13·6/n2 eV; E < 0 means a bound state; E = 0 is the ionised atom.
  • Mass–energy: E = mc2; 1 u = 931 MeV.

Question 19 · Specimen 2026[5]
(i) (a) Study the graph shown (binding energy per nucleon against mass number) and answer: indicate which region corresponds to: (1) nuclei prone to fission, (2) nuclei prone to fusion, (3) the most stable nuclei.
(b) In Rutherford’s scattering experiment, when an alpha particle (charge +2e, mass 4mp) approaches a gold nucleus (Z = 79) it is continuously repelled, so it loses its kinetic energy K and its potential energy increases. Finally the α-particle comes to rest momentarily when the whole of the kinetic energy has changed into the potential energy of the charge at that distance r0 from the nucleus, after which it returns. Using the above information, derive an expression for r0.
OR
(ii) (a) In an atom X, electrons absorb energy from an external source. This energy excites the electrons from a lower energy level to a higher one. When electrons return to the ground state they emit photons. Figure 15 is the energy-level diagram of atom X with three levels, E1 = 0·00 eV, E2 = 1·78 eV and E3 = 2·95 eV (the ground state is taken as 0 eV). What wavelength of radiation is needed to excite the atom to level E2 from E1?
(b) According to Bohr’s theory of the hydrogen atom, calculate (1) the angular momentum of the electron in the second Bohr orbit; (2) the radius of the third Bohr orbit.

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a)(1) Nuclei prone to fission (heavy, A > about 180: binding energy per nucleon falls with A, so splitting releases energy): Region C.
    [1]
  2. (a)(2) Nuclei prone to fusion (light, A < about 50: binding energy per nucleon rises, so joining releases energy): Region A.
    [1]
  3. (a)(3) Most stable nuclei (maximum binding energy per nucleon, about 8·8 MeV near 56Fe): Region B.
    [1]
  4. (b) At closest approach the total energy is wholly potential: K = U = (1/4πε0)(2e)(Ze)/r0 = (1/4πε0)(2 × 79 e2)/r0.
    [1]
  5. r0 = (1/4πε0) · 158 e2/K (= 2Ze2/(4πε0K) with Z = 79).
    [1]
Answer
(a) (1) C, (2) A, (3) B; (b) r0 = 158e2/(4πε0K)
Marking note Key: C, A, B and r0 = (1/4πε0)(158e2/K). The regions are those marked on the printed graph: A to about A = 50, B to about 180, C beyond.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (a) Energy needed = E2 − E1 = 1·78 eV = 1·78 × 1·6 × 10−19 J = 2·85 × 10-19 J = hc/λ.
    [1]
  2. λ = hc/(E2 − E1) = (6·6 × 10−34 × 3 × 108)/(2·85 × 10-19) = 6·95 × 10-7 m (about 695 nm, red light).
    [1]
  3. (b)(1) Bohr’s quantisation: L = nh/2π = 2h/2π = h/π = 6·6 × 10−34/3·14 = 2·10 × 10-34 J s.
    [1½]
  4. (b)(2) rn = n2a0: r3 = 32 × 5·3 × 10−11 = 4·77 × 10-10 m (= 4·77 × 10−10 m).
    [1½]
Answer
(a) λ = 6·95 × 10-7 m; (b)(1) L = 2·10 × 10-34 J s; (b)(2) r3 = 4·77 × 10-10 m.
Marking note Key: λ = 6·952 × 10−7 m, L = 2·1 × 10−34 J s and r3 = 47·7 × 10−11 m. Key’s intermediate “l2 = 2h =” omits the 1/2π, but the number is right. π = 3·14 is the usual ISC value.

Concepts to recall

  • Binding energy per nucleon: rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks (about 8·8 MeV) near 56Fe, falls slowly for heavy nuclei. Fusion of light and fission of heavy nuclei move towards the peak and release energy.
  • Conservation of energy in Rutherford scattering: K = kq1q2/r0 at the distance of closest approach (head-on); r0 ∝ 1/K, a measure of the nuclear size.
  • Charge of the gold nucleus is +79e, of the alpha particle +2e.
  • Excitation needs a photon of energy exactly equal to the level difference: hc/λ = ΔE; λ(nm) ≈ 1240/ΔE(eV) (here 1240/1·78 = 697 nm with h = 6·63).
  • Bohr: angular momentum mvr = nh/2π; orbit radius rn = n2a0 for hydrogen (a0 = 5·3 × 10−11 m); En = −13·6/n2 eV.
  • Use the paper’s own constants: h = 6·6 × 10−34 J s, c = 3 × 108 m s−1, 1 eV = 1·6 × 10−19 J.
FIVE-MARK QUESTIONS (7)ISC 2025 Improvement Physics Paper 1 (861A), Q19
Question 19 · Board 2025 Imp.[5]
(i) (a) State Bohr’s postulate of quantisation of angular momentum.
(b) Show how it can be obtained from wave nature of orbiting electrons.
(c) Plot a labelled graph showing variation of binding energy per nucleon of a nucleus versus its mass number.
OR
(ii) (a) Write a balanced equation of a nuclear fission reaction taking place in a nuclear reactor.
(b) By performing necessary calculations, determine which one of the following nuclei is more stable: 42He nucleus having binding energy of 28·8 MeV or 94Be nucleus having binding energy of 63 MeV.
(c) (1) Draw energy level diagram of hydrogen atom showing at least four (lowest) energy levels.
(2) Show any one transition on this diagram which results in the emission of light energy.

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a) Of all the possible circular orbits only those are allowed in which the angular momentum of the electron is an integral multiple of h/2π: L = mvr = nh/2π, n = 1, 2, 3, …
    [1]
  2. (b) de Broglie: the electron has wavelength λ = h/(mv). For a stable orbit the electron wave must be a standing wave, so the circumference holds a whole number of wavelengths: 2πr = nλ.
    [1]
  3. Substituting: 2πr = nh/(mv), hence mvr = nh/2π, which is Bohr’s condition.
    [1]
  4. (c) Graph of binding energy per nucleon (MeV, y-axis) against mass number A: rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks at about 8·8 MeV near A = 56 (56Fe), then falls slowly to about 7·6 MeV at A = 238. Axes labelled with units [1]; peak and the general shape marked [1].
    [2]
Answer
(a) mvr = nh/2π; (b) from 2πr = nλ, λ = h/mv; (c) graph as drawn, maximum near 56Fe.
Common slip Plotting binding energy rather than binding energy per nucleon, or omitting the axis labels and units.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (a) 23592U + 10n → 14156Ba + 9236Kr + 3 10n + energy (about 200 MeV). Check: mass numbers 236 = 141 + 92 + 3; charges 92 = 56 + 36.
    [1]
  2. (b) Stability is judged by binding energy per nucleon: 4He: 28·8/4 = 7·2 MeV per nucleon.
    [1]
  3. 9Be: 63/9 = 7 MeV per nucleon. Since 7·2 > 7·0, the 42He nucleus is more stable.
    [1]
  4. (c)(1) Levels En = −13·6/n2 eV: n = 1: −13·6; n = 2: −3·4; n = 3: −1·51; n = 4: −0·85 eV, drawn with the spacing closing up towards 0 [1].
    [1]
  5. (2) Any downward transition into n = 2, e.g. n = 3 → 2 (Hα): E = 13·6(1/4 − 1/9) = 1·89 eV, λ = 1240/1·89 = 656 nm, visible red light [arrow shown].
    [1]
Answer
(a) U-235 + n → Ba-141 + Kr-92 + 3n; (b) 4He (7·2 > 7·0 MeV per nucleon); (c) diagram as drawn, 3 → 2 emits visible light.
Common slip Comparing the total binding energies (63 > 28·8) instead of the energy per nucleon.
Marking note The values 28·8 and 63 MeV are those given in the question; the data-book binding energy of 9Be is about 58 MeV, which would give 6·5 MeV per nucleon, and the conclusion is the same.

Concepts to recall

  • Bohr: mvr = nh/2π. de Broglie: λ = h/p; the standing-wave condition 2πr = nλ reproduces it, and n counts the wavelengths round the orbit.
  • Binding energy per nucleon curve: about 7–8 MeV for most nuclei, maximum about 8·8 MeV near A = 56; fusion of light and fission of heavy nuclei both move towards the peak and release energy.
  • Higher binding energy per nucleon means a more stable nucleus.
  • Fission: a slow neutron splits 235U into two medium nuclei plus 2–3 neutrons and about 200 MeV; conserve A and Z.
  • Binding energy per nucleon is the measure of stability: 4He is exceptionally stable for its size.
  • Hydrogen spectrum: En = −13·6/n2 eV; transitions to n = 2 give the visible Balmer series, to n = 1 the ultraviolet Lyman series; photon energy = Ei − Ef.

The teacher explained how water slows down fast moving neutrons and absorbs the heat produced in the reactor.
(1) What is the use of a nuclear reactor? (2) What is the name given to a material which slows down fast moving neutrons? (3) State how a nuclear reactor can be shut down in case of an emergency.
(b) (1) What is meant by the statement: ‘Angular momentum of an orbiting electron is quantised’? (2) What is the physical significance of the fact that total energy of an orbiting electron is negative?

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a) For the electron of a hydrogen atom in orbit of radius rn with speed v, the Coulomb force supplies the centripetal force: mv2/rn = e2/(4πε0rn2), i.e. mv2rn = e2/4πε0 …(1)
    [1]
  2. Bohr’s quantum condition: mvrn = nh/2π, so v = nh/(2πmrn) …(2). Substituting (2) in (1): m · n2h2/(4π2m2rn2) · rn = e2/4πε0
    [1]
  3. rn = n2ε0h2/(πme2) = n2a0, where a0 = ε0h2/πme2 (= 0·529 Å) is constant. Hence rn ∝ n2.
    [1]
  4. (b)(1) Nuclear fusion takes place continuously in the Sun and other stars (hydrogen fused to helium at about 107 K in the core).
    [1]
  5. (b)(2) The mass defect is the difference between the sum of the masses of the separate nucleons (protons and neutrons) and the actual mass of the nucleus: Δm = [Zmp + (A − Z)mn] − mnucleus; it appears as the binding energy Δmc2.
    [1]
Answer
(a) rn = n2ε0h2/πme2 ∝ n2; (b)(1) in the Sun and stars; (2) mass of separate nucleons minus mass of the nucleus.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (a)(1) A nuclear reactor produces energy under control (for generating electricity); it is also used to produce radioisotopes and for research.
    [1]
  2. (a)(2) The material is a moderator (water, heavy water or graphite).
    [1]
  3. (a)(3) By inserting the control rods (cadmium or boron) fully into the core: they absorb neutrons, the chain reaction stops and the reactor shuts down.
    [1]
  4. (b)(1) The orbital angular momentum can take only discrete values that are integral multiples of h/2π: L = nh/2π (n = 1, 2, 3, …); no value in between is allowed.
    [1]
  5. (b)(2) The negative total energy shows that the electron is bound to the nucleus: energy equal to |E| must be supplied to free it (ionisation).
    [1]
Answer
(a)(1) controlled production of energy (electricity); (2) moderator; (3) insert the control rods; (b)(1) L = nh/2π; (2) the electron is bound.
Marking note Apsara is a pool-type reactor in which the water is both moderator and coolant.

Concepts to recall

  • Bohr’s postulates: stationary orbits, mvr = nh/2π, radiation hf = Ei − Ef on a jump.
  • Results: rn ∝ n2, vn ∝ 1/n, En = −13·6/n2 eV.
  • Mass defect and binding energy: Eb = Δmc2, 1 u = 931 MeV/c2.
  • Reactor parts: fuel (U), moderator (slows neutrons to thermal speed, e.g. water, heavy water, graphite), control rods (Cd, B: absorb neutrons), coolant, shielding.
  • Controlled chain reaction: multiplication factor k = 1; control rods keep k = 1 and lowering them fully makes k < 1.
  • Bohr: mvr = nh/2π; En = −13·6/n2 eV, with E = 0 for a free electron at rest at infinity.
FIVE-MARK QUESTIONS (7)ISC Specimen 2025 Physics Paper 1, Q19
Question 19 · Specimen 2025[5]
(i) (a) Find the binding energy per nucleon of 23592U based on the information given below: mass of neutral 23592U = 235·0439 u, mass of a proton = 1·0073 u, mass of a neutron = 1·0087 u.
(b) Find the energy released during the following fission reaction: 23592U + 10n → 23692U → 9036Kr + 14356Ba + 3 10n, given: 235U 235·0439 u, 90Kr 89·9195 u, 143Ba 142·9206 u, 1n 1·0087 u.
(c) During Rutherford’s gold foil experiment, it was observed that most of the α-particles did not deflect. However, some showed a deflection of 180°. What hypothesis was made to justify the deflection of α-particle by 180°?
OR
(ii) (a) Define unified atomic mass unit.
(b) Calculate its energy equivalent.
(c) In an atom X, electrons absorb the energy from an external source. This energy “excites” the electrons from a lower-energy level to a higher-energy level around the nucleus of the atom. When electrons return to the ground state, they emit photons. Figure 10 is the energy level diagram of atom X with three energy levels, E1 = 0·00 eV, E2 = 1·78 eV and E3 = 2·95 eV. The ground state is considered 0 eV for reference. The transition of electrons takes place between levels E1 and E2.
(1) What wavelength of radiation is needed to excite the atom to energy level E2 from E1?
(2) Suppose the external source has a power of 100 W. What would be the rate of photon emission?

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a) 23592U has 92 protons and 235 − 92 = 143 neutrons. Mass of protons = 92 × 1·0073 = 92·6716 u; mass of neutrons = 143 × 1·0087 = 144·2441 u; total = 236·9157 u.
    [1]
  2. Mass defect Δm = 236·9157 − 235·0439 = 1·8718 u; binding energy = Δm × 931·5 = 1·8718 × 931·5 = 1743·6 MeV.
    [½]
  3. Binding energy per nucleon = 1743·6/235 = 7·42 MeV.
    [½]
  4. (b) Mass of reactants = 235·0439 + 1·0087 = 236·0526 u; mass of products = 89·9195 + 142·9206 + 3(1·0087) = 235·8662 u.
    [1]
  5. Δm = 236·0526 − 235·8662 = 0·1864 u; energy released Q = 0·1864 × 931·5 = 173·6 MeV.
    [1]
  6. (c) The entire positive charge of the atom (and nearly all its mass) is concentrated in a very small, dense region at its centre, called the nucleus. A rare α-particle moving directly towards it is repelled straight back (deflection 180°); the rest, which pass far from it, go undeflected.
    [1]
Answer
(a) 7·42 MeV per nucleon; (b) Q = 173·6 MeV; (c) the atom has a tiny, positively charged, massive nucleus at its centre.
Marking note CISCE’s key gives all three results. Strictly the mass of a free proton plus electrons (i.e. the hydrogen-atom mass 1·0078 u) should be used against a neutral atom; with the data of the question the 7·42 MeV answer is what is required (the true value is about 7·59 MeV). The marks are 2 + 2 + 1.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (a) One unified atomic mass unit (1 u) is 1/12 of the mass of a neutral carbon-12 (126C) atom (1 u = 1·66 × 10−27 kg).
    [1]
  2. (b) E = Δmc2 for Δm = 1 u: E = 931·5 MeV (= 931·5 × 106 × 1·6 × 10−19 = 1·49 × 10-10 J).
    [1]
  3. (c)(1) Energy needed to go from E1 to E2: ΔE = 1·78 − 0 = 1·78 eV = 1·78 × 1·6 × 10−19 = 2·85 × 10-19 J.
    [½]
  4. λ = hc/ΔE = (6·6 × 10−34 × 3 × 108)/(2·85 × 10-19) = 6·95 × 10-7 m ≈ 695 nm (≈ 697 nm with hc = 1240 eV nm).
    [1]
  5. (c)(2) Rate of photon emission = power/energy of one photon = 100 W/(2·85 × 10-19 J) = 3·51 × 1020 s−1 (≈ 3·5 × 1020 photons per second).
    [1½]
Answer
(a) 1/12 of the mass of a 12C atom; (b) 931·5 MeV (1·49 × 10−10 J); (c)(1) λ ≈ 695 nm (697 nm with hc = 1240 eV nm); (c)(2) 3·5 × 1020 photons per second.
Marking note CISCE’s key first works out the E1 → E3 wavelength (420·3 nm, the pumping line for a laser) and only then E1 → E2 (696·6 nm). The question (1) asks for E2 from E1, so 696·6 nm (695 nm with the paper’s own constants) is the answer; credit either value if the candidate states which transition it is for; 420 nm is the wavelength for the E1 → E3 excitation shown by the long arrow in the figure (419 nm). The key’s rate, 3·506 × 1020, used 1·602 × 10−19 J per eV.

Concepts to recall

  • Mass defect Δm = [Zmp + (A − Z)mn] − M; binding energy Eb = Δmc2 = Δm × 931·5 MeV/u; per nucleon Eb/A.
  • Energy released in a reaction Q = (mass of reactants − mass of products) × 931·5 MeV; the neutrons on both sides must be counted (here 1 on the left, 3 on the right).
  • Rutherford: α-scattering shows a nucleus of radius about 10−15 m; Bohr’s model explains the stable orbits that Rutherford’s could not.
  • 1 u = 1/12 of the mass of 12C = 1·66 × 10−27 kg ≈ 931·5 MeV/c2.
  • Photon energy E = hc/λ; useful: hc = 1240 eV nm, so λ (nm) = 1240/E (eV).
  • Rate of photons n = P/Ephoton (power in W, photon energy in J).

Question 19 · Board 2024[5]
Answer either subpart (i) or subpart (ii).
(i) (a) On the basis of Bohr’s theory, derive an expression for the radius of the nth orbit of an electron of hydrogen atom.
(b) Calculate the energy released in the following nuclear fusion reaction: 21H + 21H → 42He + energy
Mass of 21H = 2·014102 u; Mass of 42He = 4·002604 u
OR
(ii) (a) Calculate mass defect and binding energy of 2010Ne nucleus, given Mass of 2010Ne = 19·992397 u, Mass of 11H = 1·007825 u, Mass of 10n = 1·008665 u.
(b) State the Bohr’s postulate of angular momentum of an electron.
(c) (1) What is the velocity of an electron in the 3rd orbit of hydrogen atom if its velocity in the 1st orbit is v0?
   (2) Radius of the 1st orbit of hydrogen atom is r0. What will be the radius of the 4th orbit?

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a) Electron of mass m and charge −e moves in a circle of radius r with speed v round the proton. The Coulomb force provides the centripetal force: mv2/r = e2/(4πε0r2). (1)
    [1]
  2. Bohr’s quantum condition: angular momentum mvr = nh/2π (n = 1, 2, 3, …), so v = nh/(2πmr). (2)
    [1]
  3. Substituting (2) in (1): m · n2h2/(4π2m2r3) = e2/(4πε0r2), which gives rn = n2h2ε0/(πme2) = n2r1 (r1 = 0·529 Å).
    [1]
  4. (b) Mass defect Δm = 2 × 2·014102 − 4·002604 = 4·028204 − 4·002604 = 0·0256 u.
    [1]
  5. Energy released = Δm × 931 MeV = 0·0256 × 931 = 23·83 MeV (about 23·8 MeV).
    [1]
Answer
(a) rn = n2h2ε0/(πme2) ∝ n2. (b) Energy released = 23·83 MeV ≈ 23·8 MeV.
Common slip Subtracting the mass of one deuteron only, or forgetting that two deuterons enter the reaction.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (a) 2010Ne has 10 protons and 10 neutrons. Mass of the separate nucleons = 10 × 1·007825 + 10 × 1·008665 = 10·07825 + 10·08665 = 20·1649 u. (The given hydrogen mass includes one electron, which is how the atomic mass of Ne is also defined, so the electrons cancel.)
    [½]
  2. Mass defect Δm = 20·1649 − 19·992397 = 0·172503 u. Binding energy = Δm × 931 = 0·172503 × 931 = 160·6 MeV.
    [1½]
  3. (b) Bohr’s postulate: the electron revolves only in those orbits for which its angular momentum is an integral multiple of h/2π: mvr = nh/2π, n = 1, 2, 3, …
    [1]
  4. (c)(1) vn ∝ 1/n (vn = e2/2ε0nh), so v3 = v0/3.
    [1]
  5. (c)(2) rn ∝ n2, so r4 = 42r0 = 16r0.
    [1]
Answer
(a) Mass defect = 0·172503 u; binding energy = 160·6 MeV (≈ 160·6 MeV). (b) mvr = nh/2π. (c) v3 = v0/3; r4 = 16r0.
Marking note The mark division 1½ + 1 + 2 + … is the same total of 5 as in the paper; CISCE gives no breakdown, so the allocation here is indicative. The paper gives no value of the electron mass, consistent with using the atomic masses given.

Concepts to recall

  • Bohr postulates: stationary orbits (no radiation); mvr = nh/2π; hν = Ei − Ef for a jump.
  • Results for H: rn ∝ n2, vn ∝ 1/n, En = −13·6/n2 eV.
  • Nuclear energy: Q = (Σmreactants − Σmproducts) × 931 MeV; use the constants table (1 u = 931 MeV).
  • Mass defect Δm = [ZmH + (A − Z)mn] − matom; binding energy = Δm × 931 MeV; binding energy per nucleon here = 8·03 MeV.
  • Using atomic masses (hydrogen atom for the proton) is correct for atomic masses because the electron masses cancel.
  • For hydrogen: vn = v1/n, rn = n2r1, so the orbital period grows as n3.
