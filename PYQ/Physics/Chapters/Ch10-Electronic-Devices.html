ONE-MARK QUESTIONS (15)ISC Specimen 2027 Physics Paper 1, Q1(A)(v)
Question 1(A)(v) · Specimen 2027[1]
Identify the phenomena that occur when a P-N junction diode is forward biased.
(P) The width of the depletion layer decreases.
(Q) The height of the potential barrier is lowered.
(R) The effective resistance of the diode increases significantly.
(S) The reverse current flows mainly due to the diffusion of majority charge carriers.
(a)Both (P) and (Q)
(b)Both (R) and (S)
(c)Both (P) and (S)
(d)Both (Q) and (R)
  1. Forward bias opposes the built-in field: the depletion layer becomes narrower (P) and the barrier height is lowered (Q).
    [½]
  2. The resistance falls sharply, so (R) is false; (S) is false since majority-carrier diffusion gives the forward current, not a reverse current.
    [½]
Answer
(a) Both (P) and (Q)

Concepts to recall

  • Forward bias: p to +, n to −; depletion width and barrier decrease; low resistance; large majority-carrier current above the knee voltage.
  • Reverse bias: depletion width and barrier increase; tiny minority-carrier leakage current.
ONE-MARK QUESTIONS (15)ISC Specimen 2027 Physics Paper 1, Q1(B)(vi)
Question 1(B)(vi) · Specimen 2027[1]
Explain why resistance of a semiconductor material decreases when its temperature is increased.
  1. At a higher temperature more electrons gain enough energy to break their covalent bonds and cross the forbidden gap into the conduction band, so the number of free charge carriers (electrons and holes) rises sharply.
    [½]
  2. Resistivity ρ = 1/(neμ) falls because n increases much faster than the mobility falls, so the resistance decreases.
    [½]
Answer
More carriers are released with temperature, so resistance falls.

Concepts to recall

  • Metals: n fixed, collisions rise with T, so resistance rises. Semiconductors: n rises exponentially with T, so resistance falls (negative temperature coefficient).
  • Energy gap: about 1·1 eV (Si), 0·7 eV (Ge).

ONE-MARK QUESTIONS (15)ISC Specimen 2026 Physics Paper 1, Q1(B)(vii)
Question 1(B)(vii) · Specimen 2026[1]
What is the direction of flow of electrons in a solar cell?
  1. Photons create electron–hole pairs near the junction; the junction field sweeps electrons towards the n-type material and holes towards the p-type material. Inside the cell electrons flow to the n side; through an external load they return from the n side (negative terminal) to the p side.
    [1]
Answer
Electrons move towards the n-type material (and holes towards the p-type); through the external circuit electrons flow from n to p.
Marking note The key: “Electrons move towards the n-type material, and holes towards the p-type material”. Either statement of the direction earns the mark.

Concepts to recall

  • Solar cell: a p-n junction operated with no bias, which converts light into electricity. Emf is about 0·5–0·6 V for Si.
  • The depletion-layer field points from n to p, so it carries electrons to n and holes to p.
  • Conventional current in the load is from p to n, opposite to the electron flow.

If temperature of a block of a semiconducting material is increased, its resistance:
(a)decreases.
(b)increases.
(c)remains constant.
(d)first increases and then decreases.
  1. On heating, covalent bonds break and more electron–hole pairs are produced, so the carrier density ne + nh rises exponentially. This far outweighs the small fall in relaxation time, so conductivity rises and the resistance decreases.
    [1]

  • Semiconductor: negative temperature coefficient of resistance (carrier density grows rapidly with T).

ONE-MARK QUESTIONS (15)ISC 2025 Improvement Physics Paper 1 (861A), Q1(B)(vii)
Question 1(B)(vii) · Board 2025 Imp.[1]
State any one difference in the energy band diagram of a semiconductor and that of an insulator.
  1. In a semiconductor the forbidden gap Eg between the valence and conduction bands is small (about 1 eV, less than 3 eV: 0·7 eV for Ge, 1·1 eV for Si), whereas in an insulator it is very large (greater than about 3 eV, e.g. 6 eV for diamond). (Any one.)
    [1]
Answer
Forbidden gap: small (< 3 eV) in a semiconductor, large (> 3 eV) in an insulator.
Marking note Also accepted: at 0 K a semiconductor behaves as an insulator; at room temperature some electrons occupy the conduction band of a semiconductor but practically none do in an insulator.

Concepts to recall

  • Band theory: valence band (filled), conduction band (empty or partly filled), forbidden gap Eg. Conductor: bands overlap or conduction band partly filled.
  • Semiconductors conduct when thermal energy lifts electrons across the small gap; in an insulator thermal energy at room temperature cannot.

N-type semiconductor is that which has:
(a)majority of holes as charge carriers.
(b)majority of free electrons as charge carriers.
(c)trivalent element added as an impurity.

Question 1(B)(i) · Board 2025[1]
How does the resistance of a semiconductor crystal vary with its temperature?
  1. The resistance of a semiconductor decreases as its temperature rises (negative temperature coefficient): heating breaks covalent bonds and generates more electron–hole pairs, so the number of carriers increases much faster than the lattice scattering.
    [1]
Answer
Resistance decreases with rise in temperature.

Concepts to recall

  • Metals: ρ increases with T (more collisions, n nearly fixed). Semiconductors: n grows roughly as e−Eg/2kT, so ρ falls with T.
  • Negative temperature coefficient of resistance: thermistors.

What energy conversion takes place in a solar cell?
  1. A solar cell (a p–n junction in which photons create electron–hole pairs that the junction field separates) converts light (solar) energy into electrical energy.
    [1]

Light (solar) energy → electrical energy.

Question 1(B)(vii) · Board 2024[1]
In semiconductor physics, what is the function of a rectifier?
  1. A rectifier is a p-n junction diode circuit that allows current to flow in one direction only (it conducts when forward biased and blocks when reverse biased).
    [½]
  2. Its function is to convert an alternating voltage/current (ac) into a unidirectional (pulsating dc) voltage/current.
    [½]
Answer
A rectifier converts alternating current into direct (unidirectional) current.

Concepts to recall

  • Half-wave rectifier: one diode, output in one half-cycle only. Full-wave: two diodes (centre-tapped) or a bridge of four, output in both half-cycles.

TWO-MARK QUESTIONS (2)ISC Specimen 2027 Physics Paper 1, Q7
Question 7 · Specimen 2027[2]
(i) Why the current flowing is extremely small during reverse biasing of a junction diode?
OR
(ii) Extrinsic semiconductors are neither good nor bad conductors of electricity. Why are they still considered useful?

Solution to the first alternative

  1. In reverse bias the barrier is raised and the majority carriers cannot cross it.
    [1]
  2. The only current is due to minority charge carriers, which are very few in number, so the current is extremely small (of the order of μA).
    [1]
Answer
The reverse current is carried only by minority carriers, which are very few.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. Their conductivity can be controlled and tuned precisely by the type and concentration of the dopant added.
    [1]
  2. Hence diodes, transistors, solar cells and other devices can be made from them (p-n junctions), which have wide applications in electronics.
    [1]
Answer
Their conductivity can be controlled by doping, which makes diodes, transistors, solar cells etc. possible.

Concepts to recall

  • Reverse bias: p to −, n to +; depletion layer widens, barrier height rises.
  • Reverse saturation current is independent of the reverse voltage until breakdown (temperature-dependent).
  • Extrinsic semiconductor: pure Si/Ge doped with a pentavalent (n-type) or trivalent (p-type) impurity; conductivity rises by orders of magnitude with a doping of 1 in 108.
  • Joining p and n type gives the junction, the basis of all electronic devices.
TWO-MARK QUESTIONS (2)ISC 2024 Physics Paper 1 (861A), Q8
Question 8 · Board 2024[2]
With reference to a semiconductor diode, define the following terms:
(i) depletion region
(ii) potential barrier
  1. (i) Depletion region: the thin region on both sides of a p-n junction, depleted of mobile charge carriers (electrons and holes diffuse across and recombine), leaving only immobile ionised acceptor and donor atoms.
    [1]
  2. (ii) Potential barrier: the potential difference (about 0·7 V for silicon, 0·3 V for germanium) developed across the depletion region by the fixed ions; it opposes further diffusion of majority carriers across the junction.
    [1]
Answer
(i) Region near the junction free of mobile charge carriers. (ii) The potential difference across this region that opposes the diffusion of majority carriers.

Concepts to recall

  • Formation: diffusion of electrons (n to p) and holes (p to n) → recombination → uncovered ions → built-in field E from n to p; diffusion current balances drift current at equilibrium.
  • Forward bias narrows the depletion region and lowers the barrier; reverse bias widens it and raises the barrier.

FIVE-MARK QUESTIONS (5)ISC Specimen 2026 Physics Paper 1, Q20
Question 20 · Specimen 2026[5]
A band gap is the distance (in energy) between the valence band of electrons and the conduction band. Essentially, the band gap represents the minimum energy required to excite an electron up to a state in the conduction band where it can participate in conduction. An LED is made of a p-type and an n-type semiconductor; recombination releases energy in the form of light and heat, and the wavelength (and hence the colour) of the emitted light depends on the energy band gap of the material. The I–V characteristics of three LEDs are given.
(i) Identify the wavelength that has (a) the maximum energy gap, (b) the minimum energy gap.
(ii) E is the energy of the incident photon and Eg is the energy gap across the depletion layer. What will happen in the following cases: (a) E > Eg; (b) E = Eg; (c) E < Eg?
  1. (i)(a) An LED begins to conduct (and emit) when the forward voltage reaches about Eg/e, so a larger threshold voltage means a larger energy gap; and since Eg = hc/λ, the larger gap is the shorter wavelength. Curve 3 has the highest threshold: maximum energy gap.
    [1]
  2. (i)(b) Curve 1 has the lowest threshold: minimum energy gap (the longest wavelength).
    [1]
  3. (ii)(a) E > Eg: the photon is absorbed and an electron–hole pair is generated (the electron goes from the valence band to the conduction band); the excess energy E − Eg appears as kinetic energy of the carriers.
    [1]
  4. (ii)(b) E = Eg: the photon is just absorbed; an electron–hole pair is produced with no excess energy (the threshold).
    [1]
  5. (ii)(c) E < Eg: no pair can be formed; the photon is not absorbed (the material is transparent to it) and there is no photocurrent.
    [1]
Answer
(i) (a) 3, (b) 1; (ii) (a) absorption, electron–hole pair plus kinetic energy; (b) pair just formed; (c) no absorption. (CISCE’s wording: (a) emission of a photon with energy, (b) emission with no energy, (c) no emission.)
Marking note The printed key for (ii) reads “(a) emission of photon with energy, (b) emission of photon with no energy, (c) no emission of photon”, which describes an LED (photon emitted when a pair recombines across the gap), although the question speaks of an incident photon on the depletion layer. The physically complete answer is the absorption one given here; the key’s threshold idea (E ≥ Eg acts, E < Eg does nothing) is the same, so give the marks to either reading. (i): key 3 and 1.

Concepts to recall

  • Band gap Eg: minimum energy to lift an electron from the valence to the conduction band. LED colour: Eg = hc/λ; threshold voltage ≈ Eg/e.
  • Photodiode / solar cell: a photon with E ≥ Eg creates an electron–hole pair, which the junction field separates; E < Eg does nothing. Cut-off wavelength λc = hc/Eg.
  • Light emitters (LEDs) use forward bias, light detectors (photodiodes) reverse bias, solar cells no bias.

When a television set (scientifically known as a television receiver) is opened, many components like semiconductor diodes, transistors, capacitors, resistors etc. can be observed on its motherboard. There are different types of diodes like photo diode, Zener diode, LED etc. found in the T.V. set. With the help of these components, a television receives audio as well as video signals.
(i) A semiconductor diode has two types of semiconducting materials: ‘P’ type and ‘N’ type. What is the difference between them?
(ii) Draw a labelled diagram of a full wave rectifier. Show graphically how its output voltage varies with time.
(iii) What type of diode can be used as a voltage regulator?
  1. (i) p-type: obtained by doping Si or Ge with a trivalent impurity (B, Al, In); holes are the majority carriers and electrons the minority. n-type: doped with a pentavalent impurity (P, As, Sb); free electrons are the majority carriers and holes the minority.
    [1]
  2. (ii) Circuit: centre-tapped transformer (secondary ends S1, S2 and centre tap C), two diodes D1 and D2 with their anodes at the ends of the secondary and cathodes joined, and the load RL between the joined cathodes and the centre tap C; ac input to the primary. All parts labelled.
    [2]
  3. Output graph: Vout against t: a succession of half-sine humps, all on the same side of the axis (pulsating dc), at twice the input frequency; D1 conducts in one half-cycle and D2 in the other.
    [1]
  4. (iii) A Zener diode, operated in reverse bias in its breakdown region, is used as a voltage regulator.
    [1]
Answer
(i) trivalent dopant, holes majority (p) against pentavalent dopant, electrons majority (n); (ii) circuit and graph as drawn; (iii) Zener diode.
Marking note The 2 marks for the circuit are for correct connections of the transformer, two diodes and load, and for labels; the output graph carries 1 mark.

Concepts to recall

  • Extrinsic semiconductors: p-type (acceptor, trivalent) with holes as majority carriers; n-type (donor, pentavalent) with electrons as majority carriers.
  • Full-wave rectifier: two diodes on a centre-tapped transformer, each conducting in alternate half-cycles; output is unidirectional with ripple frequency 2f (a half-wave rectifier gives f).
  • Zener diode: heavily doped, reverse breakdown at a sharp voltage Vz; the voltage across it stays nearly constant over a wide current range: a regulator.

Question 20 · Specimen 2025[5]
Sanya performed an experiment of obtaining characteristic curves of a junction diode. When she forward biased it, she found that beyond forward voltage V = Vk, the conductivity is very high. When she reverse biased the diode she found that a very small current (of about a few microamperes) flows in the diode. It remained constant even though she varied the voltage.
(i) In Figure 11, which one of the diodes is forward biased?
(ii) What is meant by a saturation current?
(iii) When applied voltage during forward bias is small, why does no current flow in the diode?
(iv) The circuit shown in the Figure 12 contains two diodes, each with a forward resistance of 50 Ω and with infinite resistance during reverse bias. If the battery voltage is 6 V, then calculate the current through the 100 Ω resistance.
  1. (i) A diode is forward biased when its anode (the triangle’s flat side) is at the higher potential. The −5 V end is higher than the −10 V end. Diode D2 has its anode towards the −5 V side, so D2 is forward biased; D1 has its anode towards the −10 V side and is reverse biased.
    [1]
  2. (ii) The saturation current is the small (a few μA) current in reverse bias, due to minority carriers, which stays constant (independent of the applied reverse voltage) until breakdown.
    [1]
  3. (iii) The potential barrier (barrier potential, about 0·7 V for Si) at the junction opposes the flow of majority carriers; until the applied forward voltage exceeds it, carriers cannot cross the depletion region and no appreciable current flows.
    [1]
  4. (iv) The cell’s + plate (long plate, left) feeds the diode in the upper branch (anode on the left): it is forward biased and conducts, with resistance 50 Ω. The diode in the middle branch is reverse biased: infinite resistance, no current (its 50 Ω resistor carries nothing).
    [1]
  5. The loop contains the cell, the upper diode (50 Ω), the 150 Ω resistor and the 100 Ω resistor in series: R = 50 + 150 + 100 = 300 Ω, so I = 6/300 = 0·02 A, the current through the 100 Ω resistor.
    [1]
Answer
(i) D2; (ii) the constant reverse current of a few μA; (iii) the potential barrier; (iv) 0·02 A (20 mA).
Marking note CISCE’s key: D2; “current that remains constant even on increasing the p.d.”; “due to potential barrier”; and 6/(50 + 150 + 100) = 0·02 A. All agree. The 50 in the sum is the diode’s forward resistance, not the middle 50 Ω resistor, which sits in the blocked branch. Strictly, in Figure 11 the series diode D1 is reverse biased, so no current flows in that circuit at all; the question asks only about the polarity of each diode.

Concepts to recall

  • Forward bias: p side (anode) at the higher potential; current flows only beyond the knee Vk (about 0·7 V Si, 0·3 V Ge). Reverse bias: small minority-carrier current, the saturation (leakage) current, until breakdown.
  • Circuit problems with ideal diodes: first decide which diode is forward biased, replace it by its forward resistance (here 50 Ω) and the reverse one by an open circuit; then use I = V/Rtotal.
  • The knee voltage is the barrier potential: the applied voltage must first cancel it.
