Question 1(A)(i)[1]
Fill in the blanks by choosing the appropriate word(s) from those given in the brackets [methyl alcohol, ethyl alcohol, paired, one, Cannizzaro, d–d, salicylaldehyde, Perkin’s, unpaired, f–f, salicylic acid, Kolbe’s, two, 193000, 96500, Reimer–Tiemann].
Formaldehyde reacts with conc. alkali to give sodium formate and ______ due to the absence of α-hydrogen atom. The reaction is known as ______ reaction.
  1. HCHO has no α-hydrogen, so with conc. NaOH it undergoes self oxidation–reduction (disproportionation): 2HCHO + NaOH → HCOONa + CH3OH. One molecule is oxidised to sodium formate, the other reduced to methyl alcohol. This is the Cannizzaro reaction.
    [1]
Answer
methyl alcohol; Cannizzaro

Concepts to recall

  • Cannizzaro reaction: aldehydes with no α-H (HCHO, C6H5CHO, (CH3)3CCHO) + conc. alkali → alcohol + salt of the acid.
  • Aldehydes with α-H give aldol condensation with dilute alkali instead.
  • Benzaldehyde: 2C6H5CHO + NaOH → C6H5CH2OH + C6H5COONa.

Electrochemical equivalent (Z) is the quantity of substance deposited or discharged by a charge of ______ coulomb, whereas the equivalent conductance (E) is the amount of substance deposited or discharged by a charge of ______ coulombs.
  1. The electrochemical equivalent Z is the mass deposited by one coulomb, Z = M/(zF). The chemical equivalent (equivalent mass) is the mass deposited by one faraday, i.e. by 96500 coulombs.

one; 96500

  • Z × 96500 = equivalent mass; one faraday = charge on one mole of electrons = 96500 C.
  • The printed blanks list both 96500 and 193000 (= 2F) as distractors; 193000 C deposits one mole of a divalent metal.

Fill in the blanks by choosing the appropriate word(s) from those given in the brackets [methyl alcohol, ethyl alcohol, paired, one, Cannizzaro, d–d, salicylaldehyde, Perkin’s, unpaired, f–f, salicylic acid, Kolbe’s, two, 193000, 96500, Reimer–Tiemann].
The colour of transition metal ions is due to ______ electron(s) in d-subshell and ______ transition.
  1. A transition metal ion is coloured when it has unpaired (partly filled d) electrons: the ion absorbs a part of visible light to promote a d electron from the lower t2g to the higher eg level, a d–d transition, and shows the complementary colour.
    [1]
Answer
unpaired; d–d

Concepts to recall

  • d0 (Sc3+, Ti4+) and d10 (Zn2+, Cu+) ions are colourless: no d–d transition is possible.
  • The colour seen is complementary to the colour absorbed; the d-level splitting Δ depends on the ligand.
  • f–f transitions (not d–d) colour lanthanoid ions.

Question 1(B)(i)[1]
The correct order of freezing point of 0·5 M solution of urea, AlCl3, KCl and MgCl2 is:
(a)AlCl3 > MgCl2 > KCl > urea
(b)Urea > KCl > MgCl2 > AlCl3
(c)MgCl2 > KCl > AlCl3 > urea
(d)KCl > MgCl2 > AlCl3 > urea
  1. ΔTf = iKfm: for equal molarity the depression is proportional to the van’t Hoff factor i (the number of particles per formula unit). Urea i = 1, KCl i = 2, MgCl2 i = 3, AlCl3 i = 4.
    [½]
  2. The larger the depression, the lower the freezing point, so the freezing points run: urea > KCl > MgCl2 > AlCl3.
    [½]
Answer
(b) Urea > KCl > MgCl2 > AlCl3
Common slip Writing the order of depression (AlCl3 largest) instead of the order of freezing points reverses the answer.

Concepts to recall

  • ΔTf = iKfm is a colligative property: it depends on the number of particles, not their nature.
  • i for complete dissociation = number of ions per formula unit: KCl 2, MgCl2 3, AlCl3 4, urea 1.
  • More particles → larger ΔTf → lower freezing point. Watch that the question asks for the order of freezing points, not depressions.

Question 1(B)(ii)[1]
Glucose molecules react with (x) number of molecules of phenyl hydrazine to yield glucosazone. The melting point of glucosazone is 206°C. The value of (x) is:
(a)one
(b)two
(c)three
(d)four
  1. Glucose has a CHO group. The first molecule of phenylhydrazine forms the phenylhydrazone; the second oxidises the neighbouring CH(OH) group to C=O (itself giving aniline and NH3); the third condenses with this new carbonyl to give the bis-phenylhydrazone, the osazone. Hence x = 3.
    [1]
Answer
(c) three

Concepts to recall

  • Osazone formation: 3 C6H5NHNH2 per sugar molecule (one is lost as aniline + ammonia).
  • Glucose, fructose and mannose give the same osazone because C-1 and C-2 are the only carbons involved.
  • The osazone is a yellow crystalline solid; its melting point (206 °C) helps identify the sugar.

Question 1(B)(iii)[1]
Cobalt (III) chloride forms several octahedral complexes with ammonia. Which of the following will NOT give a test for chloride ions with silver nitrate solution at 25°C?
(a)CoCl3·6NH3
(b)CoCl3·5NH3
(c)CoCl3·4NH3
(d)CoCl3·3NH3
  1. Each complex is octahedral, so six ligands are in the coordination sphere. Only Cl− outside the sphere is free to precipitate AgCl. CoCl3·6NH3 = [Co(NH3)6]Cl3 (3 free Cl−); CoCl3·5NH3 = [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2 (2); CoCl3·4NH3 = [Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl (1); CoCl3·3NH3 = [Co(NH3)3Cl3], all three Cl− are bonded to Co: no free chloride, no precipitate.
    [1]
Answer
(d) CoCl3·3NH3

Concepts to recall

  • Werner: primary valence (oxidation state, ionisable) and secondary valence (coordination number, non-ionisable).
  • Ligands inside the square brackets do not ionise; only the counter-ions give a precipitate with Ag+.
  • Number of free Cl− = number of ions − 1 of the complex: this is how conductivity data are used (Q20(iii)).

The following data was obtained for a hypothetical reaction 2A + B → 2AB.
S. No.[A] (mol L−1)[B] (mol L−1)Initial rate (mol L−1 s−1)
10·50·51·8 × 10−4
21·00·53·6 × 10−4

The rate law expression for this reaction is:
(a)rate = k[A]1[B]1
(b)rate = k[A]1[B]0
(c)rate = k[A]2[B]1
(d)rate = k[A]0[B]1
  1. Experiments 1 and 2: [B] constant, [A] doubled (0·5 → 1·0), rate doubled (1·8 → 3·6 × 10−4): 2a = 2, so order in A is a = 1.
    [½]
  2. Experiments 2 and 3: [A] constant, [B] doubled, rate unchanged: 2b = 1, so order in B is b = 0. Rate = k[A]1[B]0.
    [½]
Answer
(b) rate = k[A]1[B]0

  • A zero-order species does not affect the rate; its concentration drops out of the rate law.

Assertion: When an ether containing a primary alkyl group and tertiary alkyl group is treated with hydrogen iodide, tertiary alkyl iodide is formed.
Reason: The reaction takes place via SN1 mechanism, and the more stable tertiary carbocation is formed easily which combines with iodide ion to form tertiary alkyl iodide.
(a)Both Assertion and Reason are true, and Reason is the correct explanation for Assertion.
(b)Both Assertion and Reason are true, but Reason is not the correct explanation for Assertion.
(c)Assertion is true and Reason is false.
(d)Both Assertion and Reason are false.
  1. Assertion true: for (CH3)3C–O–CH3 + HI the products are (CH3)3C–I and CH3OH. Reason true: the protonated ether loses CH3OH to give the stable tertiary carbocation (SN1), which is captured by I−. The reason explains the assertion.

Assertion: Magnesium metal can liberate H2 gas from an aqueous solution of HCl.
Reason: Magnesium has positive value of standard reduction electrode potential.
(a)Both Assertion and Reason are true, and Reason is the correct explanation for Assertion.
(b)Both Assertion and Reason are true, but Reason is not the correct explanation for Assertion.
(c)Assertion is true and Reason is false.
(d)Both Assertion and Reason are false.

(c) Assertion is true and Reason is false.

Passage. The hydroxy derivatives of aliphatic hydrocarbons are called alcohols. Ethanol is perhaps the most important aliphatic alcohol. During COVID-19 pandemic alcohol-based sanitizers became an essential protective tool worldwide, reducing the spread of the virus. Nowadays, it is also used as a substitute for petrol in internal combustion engines.
The boiling points of alcohols are considerably higher than those of the corresponding ethers due to the presence of intermolecular hydrogen bonding in alcohols and it increases with increase in molecular mass. For isomeric alcohols the boiling point follows the order: primary (1°) alcohol > secondary (2°) alcohol > tertiary (3°) alcohol, which is evident from the following data:
CompoundPropan-1-olButan-1-olButan-2-ol2-Methylpropan-2-ol
Boiling point370 K391 K371 K356 K
Aliphatic alcohols behave as weak acids and ionize to a small extent. Their acidic strength is even less than that of both water and phenol. Phenols are hydroxy derivatives of arenes and have varied application in manufacturing of dyes, plastics, drugs, explosives, etc. At different concentrations, phenols are used both as a disinfectant and an antiseptic.
The boiling point of 2-methylpropan-2-ol is much lower than that of butan-1-ol, although both have the same molecular formula. Give a reason to explain such observation.
  1. Both are C4H10O and both form hydrogen bonds, but 2-methylpropan-2-ol (tertiary) is branched: its molecule is nearly spherical, so its surface area is smaller and the van der Waals forces between molecules are weaker (the bulky CH3 groups also shield the O–H from hydrogen bonding). Less energy is needed to separate the molecules, so it boils lower (356 K against 391 K).
    [1]
Answer
Branching decreases the surface area (weaker van der Waals forces and less effective hydrogen bonding), so the boiling point falls.

Concepts to recall

  • For isomers, boiling point falls with branching: straight chain > branched, 1° > 2° > 3° (propan-1-ol 370 K, butan-1-ol 391 K, butan-2-ol 371 K, tertiary 356 K).
  • Boiling point rises with molecular mass and with hydrogen bonding; alcohols boil much higher than ethers or alkanes of similar mass.
  • Intermolecular forces act through surface contact; a compact molecule has less contact.

Boiling point370 K391 K371 K356 K

Phenols are hydroxy derivatives of arenes and have varied application in manufacturing of dyes, plastics, drugs, explosives, etc. At different concentrations, phenols are used both as a disinfectant and an antiseptic.
Although alcohols are weaker acids than water, phenol is more acidic than alcohols. Justify.
  1. On losing H+ phenol gives the phenoxide ion, whose negative charge on oxygen is delocalised into the benzene ring by resonance (over the ortho and para carbons), so the phenoxide ion is stabilised. In an alkoxide the charge stays on oxygen and the +I effect of the alkyl group intensifies it. The O–H bond of phenol is also weakened because the sp2 carbon withdraws electrons. Hence phenol ionises more readily.
    [1]
Answer
The phenoxide ion is resonance stabilised; the alkoxide ion is not (and is destabilised by the +I effect).
Marking note The CISCE key explains this by resonance; the two points (resonance of the phenoxide, +I effect in the alkoxide) are both accepted.

Concepts to recall

  • Acidic strength depends on the stability of the conjugate base: more stable anion = stronger acid.
  • Order of acidity: phenol > water > alcohols (1° > 2° > 3°); alkyl groups push electrons (+I) and destabilise RO−.
  • Electron-withdrawing groups (−NO2) on the ring increase the acidity of phenol further; electron-donating groups (−CH3) decrease it.

The standard reduction electrode potential (E°) of some metals is given below:
MetalE°
Ag+/Ag+0·80 V
Mg2+/Mg−2·37 V
Li+/Li−3·05 V
Cu2+/Cu+0·34 V
Fe2+/Fe−0·44 V
Arrange these metals in the increasing order of their reducing power. Justify the order.
  1. The reducing power of a metal is its tendency to lose electrons (be oxidised), which is the reverse of its reduction potential: the more negative the E°, the stronger the reducing agent.
    [1]
  2. Arranging E° from most positive to most negative: Ag (+0·80) > Cu (+0·34) > Fe (−0·44) > Mg (−2·37) > Li (−3·05). Increasing reducing power: Ag < Cu < Fe < Mg < Li.
    [1]
Answer
Ag < Cu < Fe < Mg < Li (reducing power increases as E° becomes more negative)

  • Li has the most negative E° of all metals (−3·05 V) although it is not the most reactive in water; E° is a thermodynamic quantity in aqueous solution.

An alkene [A] with molecular formula C5H10 on ozonolysis and subsequent hydrolysis gives a mixture of two compounds, [B] and [C]. Compound [B] gives positive Fehling’s test and also reacts with iodine and NaOH solution. Compound [C] does not give Fehling’s test but forms iodoform.
Identify compounds [A], [B] and [C]. Write the iodoform reaction either with compound [B] or [C].

[A] 2-methylbut-2-ene; [B] ethanal; [C] propanone

SECTION B – 20 MARKSCISCE Specimen 2027 Chemistry Paper 1 (862A), Q4
Question 4[2]
(i) Answer the following questions with respect to the coordination complex ion [Cr(NH3)6]3+
  (a) What is the hybridisation state of the central metal atom?
  (b) Comment on the magnetic nature of the complex ion.
OR
(ii) Observe the diagram of splitting of d-orbitals in octahedral field and answer the following questions.
  (a) Identify the electronic configuration of d5 ion, if Δo < P.
  (b) Comment on the relationship between crystal field stabilisation energy (Δo) and the strength of the ligand.

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a) Cr (Z = 24): [Ar] 3d54s1; Cr3+ is 3d3. The three electrons occupy three separate 3d orbitals (Hund’s rule), leaving two 3d, the 4s and three 4p orbitals vacant. These six orbitals hybridise to receive six NH3 lone pairs: d2sp3 (inner orbital, octahedral).
    [1]
  2. (b) Three 3d electrons remain unpaired (n = 3), μ = √(n(n+2)) = √15 = 3·87 BM. The complex ion is paramagnetic.
    [1]
Answer
(a) d2sp3; (b) paramagnetic (three unpaired electrons)

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (a) If Δo < P (the pairing energy), it costs less to promote an electron to eg than to pair it in t2g. The five electrons therefore go one each into all five orbitals (Hund): t2g3 eg2, a high-spin complex with five unpaired electrons.
    [1]
  2. (b) The splitting Δo increases with the strength of the ligand (spectrochemical series, I− < Br− < Cl− < F− < H2O < NH3 < en < CN− < CO): a strong-field ligand gives a large Δo (low-spin complex), a weak-field ligand a small one. CFSE = (−0·4nt2g + 0·6neg)Δo + pairing terms.
    [1]
Answer
(a) t2g3 eg2 (high spin); (b) the stronger the ligand, the larger Δo (and CFSE); strong-field ligands give low-spin complexes
Marking note The paper writes ‘crystal field stabilisation energy (Δo)’; Δo is the crystal field splitting energy, but the intended answer (larger Δo for stronger ligand) is the same.

Concepts to recall

  • Valence bond theory: six ligands, octahedral. Inner orbital (d2sp3) uses (n−1)d; outer orbital (sp3d2) uses nd.
  • Cr3+ (d3) has exactly three unpaired electrons whatever the ligand, so there is no choice between high and low spin for d3.

(i) C6H5NO2 → C6H5NH2 → C6H5N2Cl → C6H6; (ii) C6H5N2Cl + Cu2Br2/HBr → C6H5Br; (iii) CH3CN + 4[H] → CH3CH2NH2

Concepts to recall

  • Nitro → amine: Sn/HCl, Fe/HCl or H2/Pd. Amine → diazonium: NaNO2 + HCl at 273–278 K. Diazonium → H: H3PO2 (or C2H5OH).
  • Sandmeyer: CuCl/HCl (Cl), CuBr/HBr (Br), CuCN/KCN (CN); Gattermann uses Cu powder.
  • Nitrile reduction gives RCH2NH2; CH3CN has two carbons, so the product CH3CH2NH2 keeps both and the C≡N carbon becomes CH2.

Question 19[5]
(i) An organic compound [A] with molecular formula C2Cl3O2H is obtained when compound [B] reacts with red phosphorous and Cl2. The organic compound [B] can be obtained on the reaction of methyl magnesium bromide with carbon dioxide followed by acid hydrolysis. Upon partial reduction, compound [B] forms compound [C] with molecular formula C2H4O, which gives yellow precipitate of iodoform on heating with iodine in presence of sodium hydroxide. Compound [C] also reacts with dilute NaOH to form compound [D].
  (a) Identify the compounds [A], [B], [C] and [D].
  (b) Write down the reaction for the formation of [A] from [B]. What is the reaction called?
  (c) Write the chemical test to distinguish between compound [C] and acetone.
  (d) Which will be more acidic, compound [A] or [B]? Why?
OR
(ii) Identify the compounds [A], [B] and [C] in the following reactions:
(a) CH3C≡N → (SnCl2/HCl, then H2O) [A] → (CH3MgBr, then H2O) [B] → (acidified K2Cr2O7) [C]
(b) H–C≡C–H → (H2O, Hg2+/H2SO4) [A] → ([O], K2Cr2O7 + H2SO4) [B] → (PCl5) [C]
(iii) Write chemical equations to convert the following:
  (a) Acetone to propene
  (b) Benzaldehyde to cinnamic acid

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a) CH3MgBr + CO2 → CH3COOMgBr → (H3O+) CH3COOH, so [B] = CH3COOH (ethanoic acid). [A] (C2HCl3O2) = CCl3COOH (trichloroethanoic acid). [C] (C2H4O) = CH3CHO (ethanal; gives iodoform). [D] = the aldol of ethanal, CH3CH(OH)CH2CHO (3-hydroxybutanal).
    [2]
  2. (b) CH3COOH + 3Cl2 (red P) → CCl3COOH + 3HCl. This is the Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ) reaction (α-halogenation of a carboxylic acid).
    [1]
  3. (c) Ethanal [C] reduces Tollens’ reagent (silver mirror) and Fehling’s solution (red Cu2O precipitate) and restores the colour of Schiff’s reagent; acetone gives none of these. (Both give the iodoform test, so that test cannot distinguish them.)
    [1]
  4. (d) [A], trichloroacetic acid, is more acidic: the three Cl atoms exert a strong −I effect, pull electron density from the carboxylate, disperse the negative charge and stabilise CCl3COO−. In [B] the CH3 group has a +I effect, which destabilises the anion.
    [1]
Answer
[A] CCl3COOH; [B] CH3COOH; [C] CH3CHO; [D] CH3CH(OH)CH2CHO (3-hydroxybutanal). (b) HVZ reaction. (c) Tollens’/Fehling’s test. (d) [A] is more acidic (−I effect of Cl)
Common slip Calling [D] 2-hydroxybutanal: the aldol joins the CH3 carbon (α-C) of one ethanal to the carbonyl carbon of the other, putting OH on C-3.
Marking note CISCE’s key names [D] ‘2-hydroxybutanal’, which is wrong. The structure it draws, CH3–CH(OH)–CH2–CHO, is 3-hydroxybutanal (the OH is on carbon 3, the aldol of two ethanal molecules). Full marks for the structure; the correct name is 3-hydroxybutanal.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (ii)(a) Stephen reaction: CH3CN + 2[H] (SnCl2/HCl) → CH3CH=NH → (H2O) [A] = CH3CHO. Grignard addition: CH3CHO + CH3MgBr → (H2O) [B] = CH3CH(OH)CH3 (propan-2-ol). Oxidation of the 2° alcohol: [C] = CH3COCH3 (acetone).
    [1½]
  2. (b) Kucherov hydration: HC≡CH + H2O (Hg2+/H2SO4) → CH2=CHOH → [A] = CH3CHO. K2Cr2O7/H2SO4 oxidises it to [B] = CH3COOH. PCl5 replaces OH by Cl: CH3COOH + PCl5 → [C] = CH3COCl + POCl3 + HCl.
    [1½]
  3. (iii)(a) CH3COCH3 + 2[H] (LiAlH4 or NaBH4) → CH3CH(OH)CH3;  CH3CH(OH)CH3 (conc. H2SO4, 443 K) → CH3CH=CH2 + H2O.
    [1]
  4. (b) Perkin reaction: C6H5CHO + (CH3CO)2O (CH3COONa, Δ, then H2O) → C6H5CH=CHCOOH (cinnamic acid) + CH3COOH.
    [1]
Answer
(ii)(a) [A] CH3CHO, [B] (CH3)2CHOH, [C] CH3COCH3; (b) [A] CH3CHO, [B] CH3COOH, [C] CH3COCl. (iii) (a) reduce, dehydrate; (b) Perkin reaction
Marking note In (a) the intermediate imine hydrolyses in the same step (Stephen reduction: nitrile → aldehyde). For (iii)(a) any route through propan-2-ol (or the Clemmensen/Wolff–Kishner product propane is not accepted, as it is not an alkene) is acceptable.

Concepts to recall

  • Grignard + CO2 → carboxylic acid with one more carbon than R in RMgX. Partial reduction of an acid (via its chloride, Rosenmund, or DIBAL-H) gives the aldehyde.
  • Aldol condensation: aldehydes with α-H + dil. NaOH → β-hydroxy aldehyde: 2CH3CHO → CH3CH(OH)CH2CHO (heating gives crotonaldehyde).
  • Distinguishing aldehyde from ketone: Tollens’, Fehling’s, Schiff’s, Benedict’s tests; ketones are negative. Distinguishing methyl ketones/ethanal from others: iodoform.
  • Acid strength of substituted ethanoic acids: Cl3C– > Cl2CH– > ClCH2– > CH3– (the more Cl, the stronger the acid; pKa 0·65, 1·29, 2·86, 4·76).

Question 20[5]
(i) The compound [PtCl2(NH3)2] also known as cis-platin, is used in chemotherapy. It also demonstrates how coordination geometry impacts medical science.
  (a) Write its IUPAC name and the type of isomerism it exhibits.
  (b) What is the geometry of the compound?
(ii) What type of structural isomers are [Pt(NH3)3Br]NO3 and [Pt(NH3)3(NO3)]Br? How can they be distinguished by using a chemical test?
(iii) A coordination compound CoCl3·4H2O precipitates silver chloride when treated with silver nitrate. The molar conductance of its solution corresponds to a total of two ions. Write the structural formula of the compound and name it.
  1. (i)(a) IUPAC name: diamminedichloridoplatinum(II) (ligands in alphabetical order: ammine before chlorido; Pt in +2 state). It shows geometrical (cis–trans) isomerism: the cis isomer (the drug) has the two Cl on the same side, the trans isomer on opposite sides.
    [1½]
  2. (b) Pt(II) is d8 with four ligands: dsp2 hybridisation, square planar geometry.
    [½]
  3. (ii) They are ionisation isomers (the same formula, but they give different ions in solution: Br−/NO3− exchange between the sphere and the counter-ion). Test: [Pt(NH3)3(NO3)]Br gives a pale yellow precipitate of AgBr with AgNO3 solution (Br− is free); [Pt(NH3)3Br]NO3 gives none, but its free NO3− gives the brown ring test (FeSO4 + conc. H2SO4).
    [1½]
  4. (iii) The compound precipitates AgCl and gives only two ions, so one Cl− is free and the other two are bonded to Co; octahedral Co(III) needs six ligands: 4 H2O + 2 Cl−. Formula [Co(H2O)4Cl2]Cl; name tetraaquadichloridocobalt(III) chloride.
    [1½]
Answer
(i) diamminedichloridoplatinum(II); geometrical isomerism; square planar. (ii) ionisation isomers (AgNO3 test for Br−). (iii) [Co(H2O)4Cl2]Cl, tetraaquadichloridocobalt(III) chloride
Marking note CISCE’s key adds that a heated solution of the nitrate isomer with conc. H2SO4 evolves brown NO2 and that the bromide isomer gives AgBr ‘sparingly soluble in excess of NH4OH’ (AgBr is pale yellow and only slightly soluble in conc. ammonia); any of these tests is accepted. The cis/trans form of the cobalt complex is not asked for.

Concepts to recall

  • IUPAC naming: ligands first in alphabetical order (ignoring prefixes di, tetra), then the metal with its oxidation state in Roman numerals; anionic ligands end in ‘-o’ (chlorido); cationic/neutral complex names have no ‘ate’.
  • Isomerism: geometrical (cis–trans, in square planar MA2B2 and octahedral MA4B2), optical, ionisation, hydrate, linkage, coordination.
  • Number of ions in solution (from conductance): [Co(NH3)6]Cl3 4, [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2 3, [Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl 2, [Co(NH3)3Cl3] 0.
  • Cisplatin: cis isomer only is an anticancer drug (it binds DNA); trans-platin is inactive.

Question 21[5]
(i) Answer the following:
  (a) 300 mL of aqueous solution of protein contains 1·85 g of protein, the osmotic pressure of the solution at 25°C is found to be 3·05 × 10−3 atm. Calculate the molar mass of the protein.
  (b) Phenol (C6H5OH) associates in benzene to form a dimer. A solution of 2·5 g of phenol in 120 g of benzene lowers its freezing point by 0·85 K. Calculate the degree of association of phenol. (Kf for benzene = 5·12 K kg mol−1, molecular mass of phenol = 94 g mol−1)
  (c) The molecular weight of potassium chloride and sucrose is determined by the depression of freezing point method. Compared to their theoretical molecular weight, what will be their observed molecular weights when determined by the above method? Justify your answer.
OR
(ii) (a) Arrange the following aqueous solutions in increasing order of osmotic pressure. Give reasons in support of your answer.
  (1) 6·0 g per litre of urea solution (molecular weight of urea = 60 g mol−1)
  (2) 72·0 g per litre of glucose solution (molecular weight of glucose = 180 g mol−1)
  (3) 5·85 g per litre of sodium chloride solution (molecular weight of sodium chloride = 58·5 g mol−1)
(b) Calculate the mole fraction (x) of ethanol and water if 92 g of ethanol is dissolved in 540 g of water. (Atomic mass of C = 12, O = 16 and H = 1)
(c) What type of azeotropic mixture will be formed by a solution of chloroform and acetone? Justify your answer based on strength of intermolecular interactions that develops in the solution.

Solution to the first alternative

  1. (a) πV = (w/M)RT, so M = wRT/(πV), with V = 0·300 L, T = 298 K, R = 0·0821 L atm K−1 mol−1
    [½]
  2. M = 1·85 × 0·0821 × 298/(3·05 × 10−3 × 0·300) = 49466 g mol−1
    [1]
  3. (b) Observed molar mass Mobs = 1000 Kf w/(ΔTf W) = 1000 × 5·12 × 2·5/(0·85 × 120) = 125·49 g mol−1
    [½]
  4. i = Mnormal/Mobs = 94/125·49 = 0·749. For dimerisation (n = 2): 2A ⇒ A2, i = 1 − α/2, so α = 2(1 − i) = (1 − 0·749)/(1 − 1/2) = 0·502
    [1]
  5. Degree of association α = 0·502, i.e. about 50·2 %
    [½]
  6. (c) ΔTf depends on the number of solute particles. KCl ionises: KCl → K+ + Cl−, i ≈ 2, so ΔTf is doubled and the observed molar mass is about half the theoretical (≈ 37·3 g mol−1 against 74·6). Sucrose is a non-electrolyte (i = 1): the observed molar mass is the same as the theoretical value (342 g mol−1).
    [1½]
Answer
(a) M ≈ 4·95 × 104 g mol−1; (b) α = 0·502 (≈ 50·2 %); (c) KCl: observed ≈ half the theoretical; sucrose: same
Marking note CISCE’s key gives 49466 g mol−1, 125·49 g mol−1, i = 0·749 and α = 0·502 (50·2 %); the figures here agree to the rounding used. Values within 1 % are accepted.

Solution to the OR alternative

  1. (a) Osmotic pressure is a colligative property, π ∝ total number of particles per litre. Urea (non-electrolyte): 6·0/60 = 0·1 mol L−1 → 0·1 mol of particles. Glucose: 72·0/180 = 0·4 mol L−1 → 0·4 mol. NaCl: 5·85/58·5 = 0·1 mol L−1, NaCl → Na+ + Cl− (i = 2) → 0·2 mol of particles (= 12·046 × 1022 particles per 0·1 mol; urea 6·023 × 1022; glucose 24·09 × 1022).
    [1½]
  2. Increasing order of π: urea (1) < NaCl (3) < glucose (2).
    [½]
  3. (b) n(C2H5OH) = 92/46 = 2 mol; n(H2O) = 540/18 = 30 mol; total = 32 mol. x(ethanol) = 2/32 = 0·0625; x(water) = 30/32 = 0·9375
    [1½]
  4. (c) Chloroform + acetone form a maximum-boiling azeotrope: the C–H of CHCl3 (made acidic by the three Cl) forms a hydrogen bond with the C=O oxygen of acetone, so the A–B attractions are stronger than A–A and B–B. The solution shows negative deviation from Raoult’s law (vapour pressure lower than ideal, ΔmixH negative, ΔmixV negative), so its boiling point is above that of either component.
    [1½]
Answer
(a) urea < NaCl < glucose; (b) x(ethanol) = 0·0625, x(water) = 0·9375; (c) maximum-boiling azeotrope (negative deviation)
Marking note Water is counted as 18 g mol−1 (H = 1, O = 16) and ethanol as 46 g mol−1 as the question gives atomic masses. Mole fractions add to 1 (0·0625 + 0·9375).

Concepts to recall

  • Osmotic pressure π = CRT = (w/MV)RT; suitable for proteins and polymers (large M gives a small but measurable π). V in litres, R = 0·0821 L atm K−1 mol−1.
  • van’t Hoff factor i = Mnormal/Mobs = observed/calculated colligative property. Association (i < 1): i = 1 − α(1 − 1/n); dissociation (i > 1): i = 1 + (n − 1)α.
  • For an electrolyte the colligative-property molar mass is smaller than the true molar mass (more particles); for an associating solute it is larger.
  • ΔTf = iKfm, so Mobs = 1000Kfw/(ΔTfW) (the formula without i).
