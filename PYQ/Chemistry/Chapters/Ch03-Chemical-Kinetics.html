Question 1(B)(iv) · Specimen 2027[1]
The following data was obtained for a hypothetical reaction 2A + B → 2AB.
S. No.[A] (mol L−1)[B] (mol L−1)Initial rate (mol L−1 s−1)
10·50·51·8 × 10−4
21·00·53·6 × 10−4
31·01·03·6 × 10−4
The rate law expression for this reaction is:
(a)rate = k[A]1[B]1
(b)rate = k[A]1[B]0
(c)rate = k[A]2[B]1
(d)rate = k[A]0[B]1
  1. Experiments 1 and 2: [B] constant, [A] doubled (0·5 → 1·0), rate doubled (1·8 → 3·6 × 10−4): 2a = 2, so order in A is a = 1.
    [½]
  2. Experiments 2 and 3: [A] constant, [B] doubled, rate unchanged: 2b = 1, so order in B is b = 0. Rate = k[A]1[B]0.
    [½]
Answer
(b) rate = k[A]1[B]0
Common slip Taking the coefficient 2 of A as the order. Orders come from the data, not the balanced equation.

Concepts to recall

  • Method of initial rates: change one concentration at a time; (rate2/rate1) = (conc2/conc1)order.
  • The order is found only from experiment, never from the stoichiometric coefficients (2A + B does not mean order 2 in A).
  • A zero-order species does not affect the rate; its concentration drops out of the rate law.

Question 1(C)(i) · Board 2025 Imp.[1]
Passage. In 1889 Svante Arrhenius proposed that the dependence of the rate constant (k) of a reaction on temperature can be expressed by k = Ae−Ea/RT, the Arrhenius equation. In logarithmic form log10k = log10A − Ea/(2·303RT), which is like y = mx + c. Ea can be found by a graphical method or by the rate-constant method.
The rate of a particular reaction triples when the temperature changes from 30°C to 60°C. Calculate the activation energy (Ea) of the reaction.
  1. T1 = 303 K, T2 = 333 K, k2/k1 = 3. log(k2/k1) = (Ea/2·303R)[(T2 − T1)/(T1T2)]; so Ea = 2·303 × 8·314 × log 3 × (303 × 333/30) = 3·073e+04 J mol−1 ≈ 30·7 kJ mol−1.
    [1]
Answer
Ea ≈ 30·7 kJ mol−1
Marking note Use R = 8·314 J K−1 mol−1 (paper’s data). 30·7 kJ mol−1; 30–31 kJ accepted.

Concepts to recall

  • Two-temperature Arrhenius: log(k2/k1) = (Ea/2·303R)(1/T1 − 1/T2); convert °C to K.
  • “Rate triples” means k2/k1 = 3 (same concentrations); log 3 = 0·4771.

Passage. The rate of a reaction depends on the concentration of reactants. The rate law for a hypothetical reaction aA + bB → cC + dD is rate = k[A]x[B]y, where x and y are calculated experimentally and known as order of reaction. In most cases, the mechanism of a reaction is not straightforward but is broken down into simple elementary steps. These steps represent the progress of the overall reaction at the molecular level.
(i) What will be the order of reaction if the unit of k is mol−2 L2 s−1?
  1. Unit of k = (mol L−1)1−n s−1. Here mol−2 L2 = (mol L−1)−2, so 1 − n = −2 and n = 3.
    [½]
  2. The reaction is of third order.
    [½]
Answer
Order = 3 (third order)

  • Units of k: zero order mol L−1 s−1; first order s−1; second order L mol−1 s−1; third order L2 mol−2 s−1.

ONE-MARK QUESTIONS (13)ISC 2025 Chemistry Paper 1 (862A), Q1(C)(ii)
Question 1(C)(ii) · Board 2025[1]
(ii) State any one difference between order of reaction and molecularity of reaction.
  1. Order is determined experimentally (from the rate law); molecularity is a theoretical quantity, the number of reacting species in an elementary step.
    [1]
Answer
Order: experimental, can be zero, fractional or whole. Molecularity: theoretical, always a whole number (1, 2, 3), never zero.
Marking note Any one correct difference earns the mark (e.g. “order can be fractional, molecularity cannot”).

Concepts to recall

  • Order = sum of the powers of concentrations in the experimentally found rate law; it applies to the overall reaction.
  • Molecularity applies only to an elementary step; a complex reaction has no single molecularity.

Question 1(C)(iii) · Board 2025[1]
(iii) For the reaction 2A → B + C, the rate law is rate = k[A]3/2. What is the order and molecularity of the reaction?
  1. Order = the power of [A] in the rate law = 3/2 = 1·5.
    [½]
  2. Molecularity (from the equation 2A, two molecules of A) = 2; a fractional order shows the reaction is not elementary, so strictly the molecularity is meaningful only for its elementary steps.
    [½]
Answer
Order = 3/2 (1·5); molecularity = 2 (from the equation as written).
Common slip Writing the order as 2 from the coefficient of A: order comes only from the rate law.

Question 5 · Specimen 2027[2]
Rohan, a patient of thyroid disorder, was given certain amount of radioactive Iodine-131 as a part of his treatment.
What amount of Iodine-131 will be left in Rohan’s body after 48 days, if it is known that the half-life period of radioactive iodine-131 is 12 days? Assume that no amount of the isotope was eliminated through biological processes.

    [1]
  2. Amount left = A0(1/2)n = A0(1/2)4 = A0/16.
    [½]
  3. So 1/16 of the initial amount (6·25 %) remains.
    [½]

One-sixteenth (6·25 %) of the initial amount

In general, it is observed that the rate of a reaction becomes double with every 10°C rise in temperature. If this generalisation holds true for a reaction, calculate the value of activation energy when temperature changes from 295 K to 305 K.
  1. Doubling of rate for a 10 K rise: k2/k1 = 2. Arrhenius (two-temperature) equation: log(k2/k1) = (Ea/2·303R)[(T2 − T1)/(T1T2)]
    [½]
  2. log 2 = 0·3010 = (Ea/(2·303 × 8·314)) × (10/(295 × 305))
    [½]
  3. Ea = 0·3010 × 2·303 × 8·314 × 295 × 305/10 = 51860 J mol−1
    [½]

    [½]

Question 8 · Board 2026[2]
A first order reaction is 40% complete in 20 minutes. Calculate the time required for 90% completion of the same reaction.
  1. k = (2·303/t) log([A]0/[A]); after 40% reaction [A] = 60 if [A]0 = 100: k = (2·303/20) log(100/60) = (2·303/20) × 0·2218 = 0·0255 min−1

  2. t90% = (2·303/k) log(100/10) = (2·303/0·0255) × 1 = 90·2 min
    [1]

t ≈ 90·2 min (about 90 minutes)

Consider yourself a research scholar in the forensic science department studying the age of a dead biological sample. During one of the studies, you found that the sample decomposed by following first order kinetics. If 50 % of the sample is decomposed in 120 minutes, how long will it take for 90 % of the sample to decompose?

    [1]

Question 4 · Board 2025[2]
In the reaction 2NO + O2 → 2NO2, the rate law is rate = k[NO][O2]2.
(i) How will the rate of reaction change if [NO] concentration is doubled and [O2] concentration is halved at the same time?
(ii) Write the order of reaction if [NO] concentration is in large excess.
  1. (i) Initial rate r1 = k[NO][O2]2.
    [½]
  2. New rate r2 = k(2[NO])(½[O2])2 = k[NO][O2]2 × 2 × ¼ = 0·5 r1
    [½]
  3. The rate becomes half of its initial value (decreases by 50%).
    [½]
  4. (ii) With [NO] in large excess its concentration stays effectively constant, so rate = k′[O2]2 with k′ = k[NO]: second order (pseudo-second order).
    [½]
Answer
(i) rate becomes 1/2 of the original; (ii) order = 2 (pseudo second order).
