Question 72 marks
(i)1 mark

What is meant by the depletion region of a p-n junction? (Recall)

Answer

The thin region on either side of the junction that is depleted of free charge carriers and contains only immobile ions (positive donors on the n-side, negative acceptors on the p-side).

(ii)1 mark

How is the potential barrier across the junction formed? (Understand)

Answer

Electrons diffuse from n to p and holes from p to n. They leave behind uncovered immobile ions, whose field (n-side positive) opposes further diffusion; the potential difference so built up is the barrier.

Question 153 marks

Using Huygens' principle, draw the reflected wavefront when a plane wavefront is incident obliquely on a plane reflecting surface. Hence prove the law of reflection. (Understand)

Answer
AB: incident wavefront; A′C: reflected wavefront, tangent from A′ to the wavelet of radius vt drawn from A.
  1. While the disturbance at B travels BA′ = vt to the surface, the secondary wavelet from A spreads back into the same medium to a radius AC = vt.
  2. The tangent A′C to this wavelet is the reflected wavefront.
  3. Triangles ABA′ and A′CA: right-angled at B and C, common hypotenuse AA′, and BA′ = AC. They are congruent (RHS), so ∠BAA′ = ∠CA′A.
  4. These are the angles the wavefronts make with the surface, equal to the angles the rays make with the normal: i = r. The incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in one plane.

  1. Mass M ≈ A × m (m = mass of a nucleon); volume V = (4/3)πR3 = (4/3)πR03A
    ρ = Am(4/3)πR03A = 3m4πR03
  2. which contains no A: nuclear density is the same for all nuclei.

Calculate the energy released in the fusion reaction 21H + 21H → 32He + 10n.
(Masses: 2H = 2·014102 u, 3He = 3·016029 u, n = 1·008665 u) (Apply)

Answer
  1. Δm = 2 × 2·014102 − (3·016029 + 1·008665) = 0·003510 u

(c)1 mark

Why is fusion called a thermonuclear reaction? (Understand)

Answer

It can occur only at extremely high temperatures (≈ 107 K), when nuclei have enough thermal kinetic energy to overcome their mutual Coulomb repulsion.

An optics kit for a school exhibition contains a thin convex lens L1 of focal length 20 cm, a thin concave lens L2 of focal length 30 cm, both made of glass of refractive index 1·5, and a concave mirror M of radius of curvature 40 cm. The students use them to build a slide projector, a magnifier and a ‘light collector’ for a small telescope.

(i)1 mark

L1 and L2 are placed in contact. Find the focal length and the nature of the combination. (Apply)

Answer

1/F = 1/20 − 1/30 = 1/60 ⇒ F = +60 cm, converging (P = +1·67 D)

(ii)1 mark

Using L1 alone, a slide is to be projected sharply on a screen 80 cm from the lens. How far from the lens must the slide be placed, and what is the magnification? (Apply)

Answer

1/v − 1/u = 1/f with v = +80: 1/u = 1/80 − 1/20 = −3/80 ⇒ u = −26·7 cm; m = v/u = −3 (inverted, three times enlarged)

(iii)1 mark

An object is placed 30 cm in front of M. Draw a ray diagram to show the formation of the image and state its nature. (Understand)

Answer

f = 20 cm; 1/v = 1/20 − 1/30 ⇒ v = 60 cm in front: real, inverted, magnified (×2), beyond C.

(iv)1 mark

What will be the focal length of L1 when it is immersed in water (n = 4/3)? (Apply)

Answer

fw/fa = (1·5 − 1)/(1·5/1·333 − 1) = 0·5/0·125 = 4 ⇒ fw = 80 cm

(v)1 mark

Why would M be preferred to L1 as the light-collecting element of a telescope? (Recall)

Answer

A mirror has no chromatic aberration, can be made with a much larger aperture (more light, better resolution) and, being supported from the back, is lighter and easier to mount.
